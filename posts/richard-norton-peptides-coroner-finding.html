Coroner Links Mad Max Actor Richard Norton's Death to Unapproved Peptides

By Editorial Team · 7 min read

A Victorian coroner has found that Australian actor and martial artist Richard Norton's death was triggered by an injection of two unapproved peptides, in findings handed down on October 28 that now pressure Australia's medicines regulator to tighten the rules on a booming wellness market.

What happened

  • The finding. Victorian Coroner David Ryan found that Norton's death was triggered by his injection of the peptides CJC1295 and ipamorelin, writing: "I am satisfied that Richard's death was triggered by his injection of the peptide CJC1295/Ipamorelin."
  • The reaction. Ryan found that the injection caused an anaphylactic reaction which in turn led to cardiac arrest, and Norton died at Melbourne's Alfred Hospital on March 30, 2025, two days after collapsing at home.
  • The overdose detail. Ryan found that Norton had been taking amounts in excess of the dose prescribed by his medical practitioner, and on March 28 Norton told a nurse at his doctor's clinic that he had been administering double the prescribed dose.
  • The final evening. That same evening, after another injection, Norton complained of feeling hot and flushed, then collapsed and became unresponsive, and he never recovered.
  • The man. Norton, 75, was a veteran fight choreographer, stuntman and martial artist whose career ran alongside stars including Chuck Norris and Jackie Chan, and his death is believed to be the first confirmed death in Australia from unapproved peptide use.

How it unfolded

  • The first visit. Norton approached medical practitioner Dr Jerzy Muchnicki in March 2025, seeking peptide therapy for fatigue, shoulder pain, slow physical recovery and loss of lean muscle, and the two discussed the treatment's experimental nature and risks at length.
  • The consent form. Norton signed an informed consent form setting out the treatment, its benefits and its risks, including the possibility of a severe allergic reaction, and on March 19 he was prescribed injections of CJC1295 and ipamorelin, filled through a compounding pharmacy.
  • The check in. On March 25 Norton told Muchnicki he was feeling good and that the therapy was improving his symptoms, a reassuring report that came three days before the fatal dose.
  • The warning missed. On March 28 Norton told a nurse he had been taking double the prescribed dose, and the nurse advised him to return to the prescribed dose from the following week, but he injected again that evening and collapsed.

What the coroner recommended

  • The review. Ryan recommended that the Therapeutic Goods Administration review the therapeutic benefits and safety risks of peptides such as CJC1295 and ipamorelin, and consider whether further restrictions ought to be imposed on their use.
  • The reschedule option. The review should consider moving the peptides from Schedule 4, which allows prescription by medical practitioners, to Schedule 10, a category reserved for clinical trials and other experimental use.
  • The blunt assessment. Ryan said he was not satisfied that the substances should remain available for doctors to prescribe, adding: "It is not clear to me how their safe use can be clinically indicated given the acknowledged uncertainties as to their therapeutic benefit and their risks (as yet not fully known) to the safety of patients."
  • The regulator's reply. The Therapeutic Goods Administration said it continues to warn consumers about the risks of unapproved peptides, which have not been assessed for safety, quality or effectiveness.
  • The political call. Independent MP Dr Monique Ryan renewed her call for an urgent parliamentary inquiry into Australia's online healthcare market, arguing that the TGA regulates products and AHPRA regulates individual doctors, while nobody regulates the business models driving the market.

Why the case matters beyond one death

  • The scale of use. NSW Health recorded 1,180 emergency department presentations associated with unapproved peptides between July 1, 2025 and September 30, 2026, a caseload that grew from rare to routine in barely a year.
  • The anaphylaxis cluster. Victoria's Chief Health Officer issued an advisory on October 27 reporting 13 cases of anaphylaxis associated with unapproved peptides notified in Victoria since July 2025, including a cluster of five cases in the second half of September 2026, and warned that unapproved products carry a risk of immediate and potentially fatal anaphylaxis.
  • The approval gap. Neither CJC1295 nor ipamorelin is approved by the Therapeutic Goods Administration for anti ageing, body composition or performance use, and an independent expert told the Medical Board there was insufficient evidence that the combination is safe, effective or beneficial for human use.
  • The hidden contents. Victoria's advisory warns that the contents of unapproved peptide products, including the substance, the concentration and whether contaminants or toxins are present, are unknown, because the products have not been assessed by the regulator.
  • The warning to users. The Therapeutic Goods Administration advises consumers to avoid unapproved peptide products, particularly those obtained online or from overseas, and to seek advice from a qualified healthcare professional, while NSW Health urges anyone already using an unprescribed peptide product to speak with a qualified health professional before taking the next dose.

What to watch next

  • The TGA review. Whether the regulator acts on the coroner's recommendation and moves to restrict prescribing, and how quickly any rescheduling decision follows.
  • The inquiry call. Whether parliament takes up the call for an inquiry into online prescribing and telehealth, which would examine the gap between product regulation and doctor regulation.
  • The market response. Whether clinics and online sellers change how they promote peptide therapy now that a coroner has tied a death to the products, and whether prescribing volumes fall.
  • The data trend. Whether NSW emergency presentations keep rising in the December quarter, and whether Victoria's anaphylaxis notifications continue after the October advisory.
