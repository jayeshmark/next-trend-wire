Reflect Orbital Raises $73.5 Million to Beam Sunlight After Dark

By Jayesh Marathe · November 11, 2026 · 7 min read

Reflect Orbital announced $73.5 million in new funding on November 10, taking its total funding past $100 million for satellites that reflect sunlight onto precise spots on Earth after dark. The money funds a first demonstration mission in the second quarter of 2027, which will test whether its mirrors can deploy and steer sunlight from orbit.

The round

  • The size. Reflect Orbital raised $73.5 million in new funding, and the company says the round takes its total funding to more than $100 million. The round follows its Series A in May 2025.
  • The investors. Existing backers Sequoia Capital, Lux Capital and Starship Ventures returned, alongside new investors including Buckley Ventures, Convective Capital, The Uncommon Group and Salesforce Ventures.
  • The use of funds. The money supports Reflect Orbital's first satellite launches, continued development of its orbital reflector technology and expansion of commercial partnerships across energy and lighting.
  • The founders. Ben Nowack and Tristan Semmelhack founded the company in 2021. Nowack worked at SpaceX and Zipline before starting Reflect Orbital, and he serves as chief executive.

How it works

  • Mirrors in orbit. Each Reflect satellite launches folded up and opens a square mirror about 60 feet across once in orbit. The mirror is made of reflective film 28 times thinner than a human hair, and it folds down to the size of a shoebox for launch.
  • A spot of sunlight. From low Earth orbit, a mirror steers reflected sunlight onto a spot roughly five kilometers across, about the size of San Francisco's airport, at the brightness of a full moon. A single satellite lights a spot for about four minutes as it passes overhead, and with many satellites in orbit each one picks up where the last left off.
  • Ordered like a service. A customer requests light online, with no equipment needed on the ground. The service is permission based, so light goes only where it has been requested and approved.
  • The factory pace. Reflect Orbital builds a large reflector quadrant every three and a half days, delivers a four boom deployable structure every week and builds a 12,000 part satellite from scratch every two months.

What the light is for

  • Solar power after sunset. The company's main pitch is extending electricity generation at solar farms after the sun goes down, turning evening hours into productive generation time for installations that would otherwise sit idle.
  • Search and rescue at night. The same mirrors can light a search area or a work site in the dark, giving emergency responders light where the hardest work often happens after sunset.
  • Construction after dark. Reflect Orbital says its mirrors can also help construction crews work more safely after dark, another use for light delivered on demand.
  • The deliberate pace. "We've made substantial progress on our journey to deliver net new, clean solar energy to a world that urgently needs it," said Nowack. "We're taking a deliberate, science-led approach to deployment and are motivated by the practical benefits our technology can bring here on earth."

What to watch

  • The demonstration mission. Reflect Orbital expects to launch its first demonstration mission in the second quarter of 2027, after receiving approval from the Federal Communications Commission. The mission will collect data on how the reflectors deploy and perform in low Earth orbit.
  • The unproven part. Nothing in orbit has yet shown the concept works at scale. The demonstration is the test, and the company's commercial case rests on what that mission proves about deployment, steering and brightness.
  • The launches behind it. The company has secured multiple new launch opportunities in 2027 in addition to its existing bookings, and it recently announced the purchase of two dedicated launches with India's Skyroot Aerospace.
  • The sky question. Mirrors in orbit raise fair questions about the night sky, and Reflect Orbital says it has worked with regulators and the dark sky community from the start. It says it is excited to begin sharing the work of its Impact Science team soon.
