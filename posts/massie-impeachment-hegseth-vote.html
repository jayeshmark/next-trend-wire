Thomas Massie Files Eight Articles of Impeachment Against Pete Hegseth and Forces a House Vote

By Jayesh Marathe · November 9, 2026 · 7 min read

Rep. Thomas Massie refiled eight articles of impeachment against Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth on the House floor on November 9, forcing the House to act on removing one of President Donald Trump's most prominent cabinet secretaries within two legislative days.

The eight articles

  • The filing. Massie, a Kentucky Republican, read the 34 page resolution on the House floor and filed it as a privileged resolution, which requires House leaders to act on it by Wednesday. The resolution accuses Hegseth of "high crimes and misdemeanors."
  • Three articles on the Iran war. The first three charge Hegseth with waging war against Iran in violation of the War Powers Resolution: starting hostilities on February 28, 2026 with no declaration of war, no authorization from Congress and no imminent threat; ignoring a congressional order to withdraw; and continuing the war past the law's 60 day termination deadline.
  • The withdrawal order. The second article says Hegseth directed the Defense Department to ignore House Concurrent Resolution 86, which passed the House on June 3 and the Senate on June 23 and directed US forces to leave the conflict, with strikes continuing on June 26.
  • Civilian protections. The fourth article says Hegseth weakened the Defense Department systems designed to prevent, track, investigate and respond to civilian casualties, and ties the charge to a strike on a primary school in Minab, Iran, which the articles say killed schoolchildren.
  • The boat strikes. The fifth article charges "extrajudicial killings" in lethal strikes on suspected drug trafficking vessels in the Caribbean Sea and the eastern Pacific since September 2, 2025, which the articles say killed more than 200 people, including a second strike on survivors of an earlier attack.
  • A senator's speech. The sixth article accuses Hegseth of retaliating against Sen. Mark Kelly of Arizona, a retired Navy captain, for constitutionally protected speech after Kelly's November 18, 2025 video telling troops they can refuse illegal orders, citing a January 5 censure letter and threats that included reducing Kelly's retired military grade.
  • Venezuela. The seventh article covers Operation Absolute Resolve, the January 3 operation that captured Venezuelan President Nicolas Maduro and his wife Cilia Flores, which the articles say killed at least 83 people.
  • Yemen. The eighth article covers Operation Rough Rider, a campaign of more than 1,100 strikes in Yemen between March 15 and May 6, 2025, which the articles say caused an estimated 224 civilian deaths.

How the vote works

  • A privileged resolution. Because Massie filed the articles as a privileged resolution, House rules require leaders to act within two legislative days, so a vote is expected by Wednesday, November 11.
  • Three possible paths. The House can vote on the articles themselves, vote on a motion to table them, which would kill the effort, or vote to refer them to a committee.
  • A simple majority. Impeaching Hegseth would take a simple majority of the House, and Republican leaders can still try to block it with a tabling motion before the articles ever come to a vote.
  • The Senate bar. If the House impeached Hegseth, a trial would follow in the Senate, where two thirds of the 100 senators would have to vote to remove him from office.
  • A rare event. If the House votes to impeach, Hegseth would be only the third cabinet secretary ever impeached in American history, after Secretary of War William Belknap in 1876 and Homeland Security Secretary Alejandro Mayorkas in 2024.

How it got here

  • The first filing. Massie first filed the eight articles on September 15, arguing that Hegseth had subordinated the constitutional limits on the use of military force to his own policy objectives.
  • The early recess. The next day, Speaker Mike Johnson cancelled the rest of the week and sent the House home for a seven week campaign recess, which pushed the vote past the midterm elections and caused the resolution to expire.
  • A lame duck push. Massie lost his re-election primary to a Trump backed opponent and leaves Congress at the end of the term, so the lame duck session is his last chance to force the vote.
  • The midterm shift. Republicans suffered sweeping losses in the November 3 midterms, which Trump and Vice President JD Vance have attributed to the war in Iran and the rise in gas prices, and Democrats take control of both chambers of Congress in January.

Where the votes stand

  • The Democratic leader is in. House Minority Leader Hakeem Jeffries, who is expected to become speaker in January, has said he is a "hard yes" on impeaching Hegseth, while some Republicans have raised concerns, including Rep. Don Bacon, who said he will weigh the debate, and Sen. Thom Tillis, one of his strongest GOP critics.
  • The skeptics. Rep. French Hill told CBS News that a poor job rating "doesn't mean per se that he's committed an impeachable offense," and Oversight Chairman James Comer has declined to back the effort.
  • The math. Just three Republicans would need to vote with all Democrats for the articles to pass, according to ABC News, though Massie himself has said he does not know if the resolution has enough votes.

The response

  • Hegseth dismisses it. Hegseth has called the effort "sham articles of impeachment" from "a disgruntled, defeated congressman," told reporters on Monday he is "not worried" about it, and said he will defend his record at a Pentagon news conference on Tuesday, his first in more than 180 days.
  • Trump stands by him. President Trump has said he has "every intention to keep" Hegseth, and the White House has backed the defense secretary throughout the impeachment push.
  • Massie's demand. Massie has said there is one contingency in which he will not reintroduce the articles: Hegseth resigns, and he has urged the secretary to step down before Democrats take control of Congress in January.
  • What the articles are not. The eight articles are charges brought by one member of Congress, not findings by a committee or a court, and Hegseth has not been found guilty of the conduct described in them.
