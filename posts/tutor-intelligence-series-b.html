Tutor Intelligence Raises $75 Million to Put Robot Workers on Factory and Warehouse Floors

By Editorial Team · 7 min read

Tutor Intelligence announced November 2 that it has raised a $75 million Series B round led by Andreessen Horowitz, bringing the Watertown, Massachusetts robotics company's total capital raised to $117 million. The funding will train Tutor's frontier robot foundation models and build and deploy Sonny and Cassie, its robot workers.

What happened

  • The round. Tutor Intelligence announced a $75 million Series B led by Andreessen Horowitz, taking the company's total capital raised to $117 million. Union Square Ventures, which led the Series A, and Neo, which led the seed round, also took part, giving Tutor a syndicate that has backed the company from its earliest days through this round.
  • The use of the money. The funding will support frontier robot foundation model training as well as the manufacturing and deployment of Sonny and Cassie, Tutor's robot workers, which run the company's models and work jobs across the United States. The split matters because Tutor's pitch depends on both halves at once: better models make the robots more capable, and more deployments generate the data that trains better models.
  • The company behind it. Tutor Intelligence is a robotics research and deployment company founded out of MIT CSAIL and based in Watertown, Massachusetts, led by co-founder and chief executive Josh Gruenstein with co-founder Alon Kosowsky-Sachs. The MIT roots explain the research ambition, while the Watertown base keeps manufacturing and deployment in the United States, a point the announcement stresses twice.

How Tutor sells robot work

  • Labor, priced by the hour or the pick. Tutor sells robot work the way businesses already buy labor, by the hour or by the pick, with no upfront purchase and no long-term contract, while Tutor handles deployment and maintenance and sends upgraded software and intelligence on a regular basis. The model removes the two biggest barriers to factory automation, the capital outlay and the fear of being stuck with a machine that cannot adapt.
  • Real work, not demonstrations. Tutor deploys fleets of internet-connected robots into production environments, where they do real work for real customers with input from remote operators known as tutors, and every task the robots complete generates real-world experience data. That data is the missing piece in the company's telling of the robotics problem: AI learned to converse and code from the internet's vast record of text and images, but the physical world has no such record to train on.
  • Where the robots already work. Tutor's robots are currently in operation across some of the country's largest manufacturing and logistics networks, handling the goods that fill the average American home, including breakfast cereals, snacks, candy, cosmetics, home goods, and toys. The list is deliberately ordinary, and that is the point: Tutor is aiming at everyday lifting, moving, and manipulation work, not at showcase tasks.

Why investors are backing it

  • Deployment as the training strategy. Raghu Raghuram, general partner at Andreessen Horowitz and former chief executive of VMware, said Tutor's insight is that deployment is not just the destination for robotics research but how you overcome its limitations. Rather than asking the real world to fit the research, he said, Tutor starts with what real-world deployments demand and works backward through distribution, hardware, and research, with continual learning across an expanding range of deployments as the path from specialized capabilities to general-purpose robotics.
  • Demand the company can barely meet. Gruenstein said the intelligence Tutor's models provide also makes robots affordable on a whole new level, unlocking broader access for businesses that need to survive and making basic goods cheaper for everybody, and added: "We can hardly build robots fast enough". The claim frames the funding round as a response to demand rather than a bet on a future market.
  • Customers coming back for more. Nearly a third of organizations that received Tutor robots in the past 12 months were existing customers installing more Tutor robots, and Tutor's robots are now working in almost four times as many facilities as a year ago. Repeat orders are the strongest signal a robotics company can offer, since they show the robots earn their keep after the novelty wears off.

What comes next

  • The 1,000 robot target. The team is on track to have more than 1,000 robots under contract by the end of 2027, all of which will be built in and deployed from the United States. Hitting that number would turn Tutor from a promising deployment story into a fleet operator at real scale, and missing it would raise questions about whether the manufacturing side can keep up with the model side.
  • The flywheel to prove. By offering a full-stack platform spanning robots, models, and deployments, Tutor is building a network of intelligence in which each new deployment strengthens the entire fleet, making robots smarter, cheaper, and easier to deploy over time. The next two years will show whether each deployment genuinely makes the next one better or whether the gains flatten as the easy tasks are automated first.
  • The test to watch. The funding answers the question of whether top investors will back a robot workforce sold by the hour, and the answer is yes, but the harder test is whether Tutor can build robots fast enough to meet the demand its chief executive describes.
