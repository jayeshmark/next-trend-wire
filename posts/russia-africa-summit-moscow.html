Putin Hosted 22 African Leaders in Moscow. The Pitch Is a New World Order.

By Editorial Team · 7 min read

Vladimir Putin opened the third Russia-Africa summit in Moscow on October 28, 2026, with delegations from 48 African countries, 22 of them led by their leaders. His pitch was blunt: the Western-led order is ending, Russia is the partner that will help Africa profit from what replaces it, and the deals on grain, energy, and medicine prove it.

What happened

  • The summit opened. Putin welcomed African leaders to Moscow on Wednesday, October 28, 2026, for a two-day summit, the third since Sochi in 2019 and St. Petersburg in 2023. It opened with the forum's plenary session at the Gostiny Dvor complex, co-chaired by Putin and Burundian President Evariste Ndayishimiye, the current African Union chairman.
  • The turnout was the message. Delegations from 48 of Africa's 54 countries took part, with 22 represented by their leaders, according to Kremlin aide Yuri Ushakov. Ushakov called the countries "natural allies" in building a multipolar world order, where power is no longer concentrated in the West, and 33 documents were slated for signing.

Why it matters

  • The pitch is a world order, not a trade deal. Putin framed the summit as an organic part of a global transformation, telling the forum that a multipolar world is becoming a reality in an objective and irreversible trend. In his opening address, published by the Kremlin, he said the past era, when the future of whole continents was determined by a very small group of select states, is past us.
  • Moscow needs the audience as much as the audience needs Moscow. The summit comes as Russia faces heavy Western sanctions over its war in Ukraine, and winning over the Global South is now central to Moscow's counterweight strategy. Africa's 54 nations form the largest voting bloc at the United Nations, and a continent of nearly 1.6 billion people is exactly the constituency Russia wants beside it.
  • Security is the quiet half of the pitch. Russia provides security assistance to several African governments, and Russian paramilitary fighters from the Africa Corps remain active on the continent. In the Sahel, military governments that seized power have cut ties with Western partners, above all France, and turned to Moscow, including Mali's Assimi Goita, whom Putin was scheduled to meet.

Second order effects

  • Trade is growing but still small. Putin told the forum that Russian-African trade exceeded $27 billion last year, up from $18 billion in 2022, and said it could at least double soon and pass $50 billion. That growth is real, but $27 billion is a fraction of Africa's $1.5 trillion in annual trade, while Russia still trails China, the EU, and the US there.
  • Energy and grain are the concrete offers. Russia is involved in more than 30 prospective energy projects across 16 African countries, and Putin said Russia delivers grain and fertilizer to more than 40 African countries while standing ready to expand supplies. He cited 13 million tonnes of agricultural products shipped to Africa in the first nine months of this year, despite Black Sea attacks disrupting grain exports.
  • Medicine and students are the long game. Putin announced plans to localize production of Russian medicines and vaccines in Africa. He noted African students in Russia are up 50 percent since 2020, with around 40,000 now studying there, and invited African airlines to begin flights to Russia. Students, clinics, and flight routes outlast any summit declaration.

Third order effects

  • The debt argument is the sharpest weapon. Putin accused Western countries of driving Africans back into debt bondage, imposing needed funds on extortionate terms that force repayment many times over. He quoted Burkina Faso's Thomas Sankara, who said in 1987 that debt is neocolonialism, a skilfully managed reconquest of Africa, and said Russia runs joint programs using debt funds to finance growth projects.
  • The colonial legacy argument has numbers behind it. Putin told the forum that Africa's infrastructure was built in colonial times to export raw materials to the metropoles, and that manufacturing value added is less than 2 percent, while only 15 percent of Africa's $1.5 trillion in annual trade happens within the continent. He pointed to the African Continental Free Trade Area as the mechanism changing that.
  • BRICS is the institutional vehicle. Putin called BRICS the world's largest association by landmass, with three African members, and said its countries accounted for over 40 percent of global GDP in the past five years, against less than 30 percent for the Group of Seven. If that holds, African leaders gain a second table where their votes count.
  • Demographics make the pitch harder to ignore. Putin cited United Nations forecasts that Africa's population will exceed 2.5 billion by 2050, 70 percent under 30, and African Development Bank figures of 4.2 percent average growth, with 12 of the world's 20 fastest-growing economies African. A continent that young and fast-growing cannot be treated as a junior partner forever.

What to watch next

  • Watch the documents. The summit was expected to adopt a final declaration and an artificial intelligence communique. The declaration's language on Ukraine, sanctions, and the multilateral system will show how far African leaders were willing to go with Moscow in writing, which matters more than anything said from a podium.
  • Watch the bilateral deals. The real business happens in Putin's one-on-one meetings: Mali's Goita discussed a 350-vehicle contract and assembly project with truck maker KAMAZ, while Algeria's Abdelmadjid Tebboune said his country will remain a reliable gas supplier. Watch whether those deals close, and whether others leave with contracts or photographs.
  • Watch what Africa does with the pitch. Putin said Russia has advocated a stronger African role at the International Monetary Fund and the World Bank, and called for reforming them toward developing economies' needs. The test is whether African leaders press that in Washington, and whether summit promises become shipments, factories, and students.
