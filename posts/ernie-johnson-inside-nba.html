Ernie Johnson Announced His Final Season Hosting Inside the NBA

By Editorial Team · 7 min read

Ernie Johnson announced on October 28, 2026, that the 2026-27 season will be his last hosting Inside the NBA, ending a 37 season run at the desk. Johnson, 70, said the decision was his own, that he is not retiring outright, and that he will keep working on NCAA tournament coverage and other projects.

What happened

  • The announcement. Johnson delivered the news himself in a video of about two and a half minutes posted to his own social media account, captioned simply "It's time." "This will be my last season," he said. "Totally my decision. It's just time."
  • The reason he gave. Johnson said the deciding factor was preparation, the hours of work he puts in every day before the show, which he has long described as a "get to" job rather than a chore. He said he always promised himself he would step away once that work became more of a challenge than a joy, and after 37 seasons, that moment has arrived.
  • The health factor. Johnson revealed that he has dealt with heart issues for nearly two years and had bypass surgery 14 months ago, a major operation he went through without the public hearing about it. He stressed that he feels great and that his doctors never told him to stop working, but the experience changed how he wants to spend his time.
  • The family reason. Johnson said going through the heart issues made him treasure even more every second with his wife of 44 years, Cheryl Ann, and being Poppy to their five grandchildren. "I just want to slow down a bit, spend more time enjoying that," he said, framing the decision as a choice about time rather than a medical order.
  • Not a retirement. Johnson was explicit that he is not leaving television, saying "Just to be clear, I'm not retiring," and describing the move as cutting back rather than stopping. He said he will still host March Madness and other projects for TNT and CBS, and he has started a production company called 32 Bowties focused on feel good stories in sports.
  • The thank you. Johnson closed by thanking his co-hosts Kenny Smith, Charles Barkley and Shaquille O'Neal, the production crews, and the leadership at TNT and ESPN for allowing the show to continue. "This is by no means the end," he said. "It's just a turning of the page. And I'll forever cherish every chapter we have written with Inside the NBA."

Why it matters

  • The host is the show's operating system. Inside the NBA works because one person keeps four huge personalities on track, knows when to let a joke run and when to pull the conversation back, and prepares enough to steer arguments in useful directions. That job looks easy only when it is done well.
  • Preparation was the whole point. Johnson said his calling card was the hours of work before the red light comes on, and that he would leave the day that work stopped being a joy. That standard explains why replacing him is about work ethic as much as on camera talent.
  • A farewell season changes the show. Every broadcast from now on carries the knowledge that it is part of a goodbye, which gives the show a season long story arc that most studio programs never get. Which way this one goes will define how the run is remembered.

Second order effects

  • The successor question starts now. The show needs someone who can manage Barkley, O'Neal and Smith, and that combination is rare. Viewers will spend the year watching tryouts whether anyone calls them that or not.
  • Barkley's future becomes the next story. Johnson's exit raises the question of whether the rest of the desk stays together without him. The show's next chapter depends as much on him as on whoever replaces Johnson.
  • March Madness keeps Johnson visible. Because Johnson is staying on for NCAA tournament coverage and other TNT and CBS projects, his departure from Inside the NBA is a narrowing of his schedule rather than a disappearance. That softens the blow for viewers and gives the networks a way to keep his credibility attached to their biggest events.
  • 32 Bowties is the second act. It lets Johnson shape programming from the other side of the camera, choosing stories rather than reacting to games. If it works, his influence on sports television could outlast his time at the desk.

Third order effects

  • Studio shows face a succession problem everywhere. The most successful sports studio programs were built around one irreplaceable host, and the industry has no reliable formula for replacing that person. How ESPN and TNT handle this transition will become the case study other networks copy or avoid.
  • Preparation as a brand. Johnson built his reputation on homework, not hot takes, in an era when sports debate increasingly rewards the loudest voice. His exit raises the question of whether the next generation of hosts will be rewarded for the same discipline.

What to watch next

  • The on air tributes. Watch how the show handles the farewell across the season, because the tone of those moments will tell viewers whether this is a celebration or a slow goodbye.
  • Barkley's signals. Watch what Barkley says about his own plans as the season goes on, since his decision shapes whether the show continues with a new host or gets rebuilt from scratch.
  • The successor search. Watch who gets extended time at the desk this season, because the next host is most likely to emerge from those appearances rather than from a surprise announcement.
  • The final broadcast. Watch the last show of the season for what Johnson says about the run and what the network signals about the future, because that night will set the terms for everything that follows.
