Nidus Raises $30 Million to Put AI Robots in Defense Factories

By Jayesh Marathe · November 11, 2026 · 7 min read

Nidus, a New York robotics startup founded earlier this year, has raised $30 million to put AI-powered robots to work inside America's defense factories. The funding, backed by Kleiner Perkins and Costanoa, arrives just four months after the company started, and it already has more than $1 million in bookings from defense manufacturers.

The round

  • The size. Nidus announced $30 million in funding on November 10, a round that spans a pre-seed led by Costanoa and a seed round led by Kleiner Perkins.
  • The backers. Ludlow Ventures, SF1, Absolute Partners, and Tau joined the round, along with angel investors including Chris Power, founder of Hadrian; Adrian Aoun, co-founder of Dulo; and Nikhil Goel, chief commercial officer at Archer.
  • The scale. Dealroom ranks the $30 million round in the top 1 percent of all US AI seed deals by size, out of a sample of more than 11,000, an unusually large raise for a company that did not exist a year ago.
  • The use of funds. The company will use the money to grow its engineering team, expand from wire-harness automation into additional factory workflows, and scale production deployments with US defense manufacturers.

What Nidus does

  • Embodied intelligence. Nidus builds foundation models that let traditional industrial robots perform precision assembly tasks, starting with the wire harnesses inside every aircraft, missile, and vehicle.
  • The data advantage. The company collects data by having people operate robotic arms through teleoperation, then trains its models on that data, a method that builds skill from real factory work rather than from simulation alone.
  • The customer keeps the knowledge. Nidus uses sovereign AI training practices, which means each customer retains its own collective knowledge, a requirement in secure defense environments where data cannot leave the building.
  • Built for the locked-down world. Its platform was built from the ground up to meet Department of War security requirements, and the company is already delivering customized models to automate wire processing for defense customers.
  • The problem it solves. America's defense industrial base has a capacity crisis: the country that once produced more than half the world's goods now relies heavily on overseas supply chains to manufacture parts for its own weapons systems, and factories urgently need to increase output.

The team

  • The founders. Chief executive Justin Sealy founded Nidus earlier this year with three co-founders, Charu, Harrison, and Cane, all of whom worked together on the hardware team at defense startup Vannevar.
  • The chief executive's story. Sealy grew up in rural South Carolina and watched offshoring create the industrial base crisis he now wants to fix. "We can't out-labor or out-scale our competition," he said. "We need to leapfrog the rest of the world by deploying physical AI that scales America's existing industrial capacity."
  • The early traction. Nidus eclipsed seven figures in revenue in its first four months, booking more than $1 million with unnamed defense industrial customers, a pace that convinced two of Silicon Valley's best-known firms to back a team with no prior company behind it.
  • The hiring plan. The company plans to hire aggressively at its New York headquarters, aiming to build a leading robotics AI lab in the city and ramp up data collection and training for more customers.

Why the investors bought in

  • The Kleiner view. Aatish Nayak, the partner at Kleiner Perkins who led the seed round, said the value in physical AI will go to players that prioritize deployment and customer value, and called Nidus the rare team that has actually deployed AI into the most locked-down environments in the country.
  • The Costanoa view. Greg Sands, founder and managing partner at Costanoa, said the US cannot rebuild industrial capacity through new construction alone, and backed Nidus because the team is using AI and robotics to increase production inside existing factories.
  • The demand signal. Nayak said the demand Nidus is seeing is a testament to the value of its mission and product, and Costanoa backed the team at pre-seed because of its firsthand experience with the challenges of American manufacturing.
  • The mission statement. Sealy said the company only builds for American institutions that are vital for securing democracy, and believes in a future where American industry is self-sustaining and no longer dependent on foreign production.

Why it matters

  • Defense tech is the new frontier for AI funding. The round sits alongside a wave of defense and hardware AI financings, including Nominal's $240 million Series C at a $3 billion valuation announced a day earlier, and it shows investors believe the factory floor is the next place AI will pay off.
  • Speed is the story. Four months from founding to $1 million in bookings to a $30 million raise is a pace that almost never happens in hardware, and it suggests defense customers are moving faster than the rest of the market to buy AI that works on their floors.
  • The bet on existing factories. Nidus is not building new plants. It is putting intelligence into the robots and factories that already exist, which is a cheaper and faster path to more output than construction, and one the Pentagon's own supply chain problems make urgent.
  • The New York angle. A defense robotics lab in New York City, far from the traditional hubs in California and Texas, is a bet that talent and capital will follow the mission rather than the map.

What to watch next

  • The hiring race. Whether Nidus can staff a leading robotics AI lab in New York quickly enough to keep up with the demand it says it is seeing from defense manufacturers.
  • Beyond wire harnesses. Whether the models trained on wire processing transfer to the additional factory workflows the company plans to automate next.
  • The next raise. A $30 million seed at this pace puts Nidus on a path to a much larger Series A, and the defense AI funding wave shows no sign of slowing.
