Trump Names Stephen Miller White House Chief of Staff, Replacing Susie Wiles

By Editorial Team · 7 min read

President Donald Trump has named Stephen Miller, his deputy chief of staff for policy, as the next White House chief of staff. Miller takes over from Susie Wiles on Monday, November 9, in a shakeup that follows heavy Republican losses in the midterm elections.

The announcement

  • Trump announced the move on Truth Social. The president wrote on Friday that Miller, one of his most trusted advisers, will become chief of staff starting Monday, November 9, 2026, and thanked Wiles for doing an outstanding job.
  • Wiles moves to the political operation. Trump said Wiles will move over to the Trump Political Operation after serving as chief of staff since the start of his second term in January 2025.
  • Wiles confirmed Friday was her last day. Wiles posted on X that Friday was her last day as White House chief of staff, hours after Trump announced the change.
  • She resigned on Thursday night. Wiles submitted her resignation on Thursday evening, according to people familiar with the matter, and a White House official confirmed the timing.
  • Trump said he wanted a change. Speaking to reporters shortly after the announcement, Trump said he wanted to make a change and added that sometimes in life you have to make moves.
  • Miller accepted in his own post. Miller wrote on X that serving as chief of staff is an honor beyond words and that it has been the privilege of his lifetime to serve on Trump's team from day one.

Who Stephen Miller is

  • He ran domestic policy as deputy. Miller, 41, has served as deputy chief of staff for policy and homeland security adviser since Trump returned to the White House in January 2025.
  • He shaped the immigration agenda. Miller has been the chief architect of Trump's hardline immigration policies, and he widened his portfolio over the past year to cover wide areas of domestic policy.
  • He is a veteran of the first term. Miller served as a policy adviser and speechwriter on Trump's 2016 campaign and in the first administration, and he has been with Trump from the beginning.
  • He grew up in Southern California. Miller was raised in the Los Angeles area, where he reportedly appeared on conservative talk radio as a teenager, before building a career in Republican politics in Washington.
  • He backs an expansive view of executive power. Miller has argued for a broad reading of presidential authority, and he has pushed the administration toward aggressive use of federal power at home and abroad.

Why the change happened now

  • Republicans lost Congress in the midterms. Democrats won control of both the House and the Senate in Tuesday's midterm elections, a defeat that put pressure on Trump to change personnel.
  • The shakeup is the first major one since the vote. Miller's elevation is the first big staff change since the election, and it came three days after voters delivered the verdict.
  • Trump blamed the strategy. People familiar with the matter said Trump was furious at the strategy that led to the midterm losses, and he signaled on Friday that more changes could follow.
  • Wiles was the first woman in the job. Wiles, a longtime Florida political operative, was the first woman in American history to serve as White House chief of staff.
  • Miller's rise signals doubling down. White House officials said Miller's selection points to an aggressive approach to domestic policy and politics, rather than a change of direction after the defeat.

What the job means

  • The chief of staff runs the White House. The chief of staff controls access to the president, manages the staff, and sets the daily agenda, making it one of the most powerful jobs in the administration.
  • The chief of staff is the president's gatekeeper. The person in the job decides who gets time with the president and which papers reach his desk, so Miller will shape what Trump sees and hears every day.
  • Miller already held unusual influence. Even as deputy, Miller wielded outsize influence across the domestic agenda and had the president's ear, so the promotion formalizes power he already exercised.
  • The role tests his management skills. Miller is known as a policy thinker and communicator, and the job will test whether he can also run a large staff and keep competing factions working together.
  • Wiles brought a low key style. Wiles was known for a disciplined, low profile approach to the job, and Miller's more public and combative style marks a sharp change in how the White House will operate.
  • The transition happens fast. Miller takes over on Monday, giving the White House a weekend to hand over the operation before he starts the job.

What to watch next

  • Whether more firings follow. Trump signaled that Miller's appointment will not be the last personnel move, so more senior officials could leave in the coming days.
  • How Miller staffs the building. The first test will be whether Miller keeps Wiles's team in place or brings in his own people to run the White House.
  • What happens to the policy agenda. Miller's promotion suggests the administration will press ahead with its domestic agenda rather than moderating after the midterm defeat.
  • How Wiles's new role works. Wiles moves to Trump's political operation, and it remains unclear what duties she will take on outside the White House.
  • How Congress responds. With Democrats now controlling both chambers, Miller will face a Congress that can block the administration's agenda and open investigations into its actions.
