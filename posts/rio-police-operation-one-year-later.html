Rio's Deadliest Police Operation, One Year Later. The Toll Is 122.

By Editorial Team · 7 min read

Rio de Janeiro's state government first counted 64 people dead and 81 arrested in Operação Contenção, launched before dawn on October 28, 2025, against the Comando Vermelho gang in the Alemão and Penha favelas. That count later rose to 122 dead, and a year later the operation is back in the news.

What happened

  • The launch. Before dawn on October 28, 2025, about 2,500 Civil and Military Police officers entered 26 communities in the Alemão and Penha complexes, in Rio's North Zone, to execute arrest and search warrants after a yearlong investigation.
  • The stated aim. The Rio state government said the operation would contain the territorial expansion of Comando Vermelho, one of Brazil's largest criminal groups. Local prosecutors said it would dismantle its base in favelas near expressways used for drug and arms trafficking.
  • The first count. On the day itself, official sources counted at least 64 people killed and 81 arrested, and the state government called the action the deadliest operation in Rio's history. Of those, 60 were described as suspected gang members and four were police officers.
  • The count kept rising. The official balance later reached 121 dead, including four police officers, then 122 after a fifth officer died from his wounds on November 22. Of the 122, 117 were not police, while the Rio Public Defender's Office counted 132 dead, higher than the state's count. Police also reported 93 rifles among 118 weapons seized and roughly one ton of drugs.

Why it matters

  • The scale broke every record. The toll surpassed the Jacarezinho operation of 2021, which left 28 dead, and the Carandiru prison massacre of 1992, which left 111 dead, making Operação Contenção the deadliest police operation in Brazil's history.
  • The targets were missed. The judge's order was for arrest warrants to be served, and President Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva later said, as reported, that the order was not for a mass killing. According to the police report as reviewed by Reuters, only five of the people arrested were on the wanted list of 69 names, none of the wanted people were killed, and the central target, Edgar Alves de Andrade, known as Doca, was not captured.
  • The governor spoke before the bodies were identified. Governor Cláudio Castro called the operation a success, saying the only real victims were the slain officers. Later police data, as reported, showed otherwise: of 115 dead identified by police, 108 had proven links to Comando Vermelho and 59 had arrest warrants, leaving some dead with no proven link or warrant.
  • Two accounts of the same day. The state government described legitimate defense against heavy armed resistance, while residents and relatives described executions and torture. Residents gathered bodies at Praça São Lucas in Penha.

Second order effects

  • The investigations stalled. According to anniversary reporting, one year later investigations into the 117 non-police deaths remained unconcluded, while the only homicide inquiries concluded were those into the five dead officers.
  • Officers were charged, but not for the killings. Human Rights Watch reported that 19 officers were charged over other alleged offenses, including home invasion, theft, and tampering with body cameras, but no officer had been charged over unlawful use of force.
  • The anniversary report challenged the official version. Human Rights Watch published findings on October 28, 2026, after reviewing 122 autopsy reports and nearly 4,000 hours of body camera footage. It found that many body camera files were missing or obstructed, with more than 250 camera obstructions documented.
  • Who died, and how, is disputed. Human Rights Watch reported that more than 85 percent of the non-police dead were Black and that two children were among those killed. One witness account reported in anniversary coverage described an officer telling a victim's father that police were not going to take anyone alive. That is a witness account, not an established fact.

Third order effects

  • The security result is unclear. Fogo Cruzado recorded police-involved shootings in Penha and Alemão falling from 33 in the 12 months before the operation to 4 in the year after, though its director warned this measures the absence of police confrontation, not general armed violence. Residents and researchers said Comando Vermelho's territorial control barely changed, with armed men still circulating.
  • The politics split the country. President Lula called the operation disastrous and pushed for an independent probe, while Governor Castro defended it as a success. The federal government said it had not been informed, while Castro pointed to a longer-running dispute over Army and Navy support. It also came days before Rio's C40 summit and Earthshot Prize, and about two weeks before COP30 in Belém.
  • The metric itself is now contested. The operation forced a public argument over whether a death toll measures success or failure.

What to watch next

  • The hearings, and the first unlawful force charge. Watch whether officers involved in the operation are heard by the MPRJ and whether any officer is charged over the killings themselves, since every charge so far concerns other offenses.
  • The forensic fix, and the court's next move. Human Rights Watch called for prosecutors to direct the investigations and for forensic experts to work independently, outside the Civil Police structure, and watch what Justice Moraes does with the case, separate from the stalled state probes.
  • The fugitives. Watch whether the 33 outstanding warrant targets, including Doca, are captured. That 33 is the one-year residual after intervening arrests and deaths, and their freedom is the simplest measure of whether the operation met its stated aim.
  • The anniversary effect. Watch whether the one year mark, the Human Rights Watch report, and the hearings restart a debate that had gone quiet, or whether the operation fades again without answers.
