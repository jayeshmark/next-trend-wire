FDA Classified the Miyoko's Plant Butter Recall as Class II

By Editorial Team · 7 min read

The Food and Drug Administration classified the recall of Miyoko's Creamery European Style Salted Plant Milk Butter as Class II on October 23, 2026, after the product was pulled over yeast, mold and E. coli concerns. The recall covers 1,304 cases, or 7,824 individual units, sold in 10 states, and no illnesses have been reported.

What happened

  • The classification. The FDA gave the recall a Class II rating on October 23, which the agency defines as a situation where exposure may cause temporary or medically reversible adverse health consequences, or where the probability of serious adverse health consequences is remote. Class II sits in the middle of the FDA's three tiers.
  • The product. The recalled product is Miyoko's Creamery European Style Salted Plant Milk Butter, made by Prosperity Organic Foods Inc. of Boise, Idaho. According to the FDA enforcement report, the company voluntarily initiated the recall on September 11, 2026, about six weeks before the FDA assigned its classification.
  • How to identify it. The recall covers 8 ounce packages with UPC 857554005773 and a best by date of November 5, 2026. The retail carton carries lot 07871, while the shipping case carries lot 1007871, so shoppers should check both numbers depending on which packaging they have. The units shipped in six pack cases, and the product is perishable, stored refrigerated or frozen.
  • The scale. The recall covers 1,304 cases, equal to 7,824 individual 8 ounce units. The product was distributed in Connecticut, Florida, Georgia, Iowa, Illinois, Indiana, Maryland, New Jersey, New Hampshire and Wisconsin.
  • The reasons. The FDA enforcement report lists yeast, mold and E. coli as the reasons for the recall. The report does not name a specific strain of E. coli.
  • No illnesses so far. No illnesses have been reported in connection with the recalled butter. The recall is precautionary so far, but that does not make the product safe to eat, since E. coli symptoms can take days to appear.
  • How it surfaced. The issue came to light after Market Basket, a New England supermarket chain, flagged high yeast levels in the product that did not meet quality standards, and later updated its notice to warn that the product could potentially contain E. coli. Miyoko's said it voluntarily recalled a specific lot after testing identified a potential food safety concern.

Why it matters

  • Class II is a precise signal, not a panic button. The FDA's definition means the agency sees a real but limited hazard: temporary or reversible effects are possible, while serious harm is considered remote. Class II tells you to act without assuming the worst.
  • The E. coli word drives the reaction. Most people infected with Shiga toxin producing E. coli experience severe stomach cramps, diarrhea that is often bloody, and vomiting, with symptoms usually starting 3 to 4 days after swallowing the bacteria, and most people recover without treatment after 5 to 7 days. That symptom profile is why a Class II label still makes headlines.
  • Children and older adults face the highest risk. The CDC lists children younger than 5 and adults 65 and older as the groups most affected by Shiga toxin producing E. coli, and notes that some people develop hemolytic uremic syndrome, a serious kidney problem that needs hospital treatment. A plant butter is an everyday food, which means the people most at risk are also the people most likely to eat it without thinking.
  • Plant based brands face a different trust test. People buy plant based butter partly because they trust the ingredient story, so a contamination recall cuts against the brand's core promise. A microbe problem in this kind of product raises questions that go beyond one lot.

Second order effects

  • The lot code confusion is the lesson. The carton and the case carry different lot numbers, which is confusing on its own. Shoppers should match the UPC, the lot number and the best by date together, because a match on one alone is not enough, and confusion is what turns a narrow recall into a broad loss of trust.
  • Testing becomes the story. The recall began with a retailer's quality test finding high yeast levels, not with illnesses, which shows how much of food safety now depends on routine microbiological testing catching problems early. Expect more attention on who tests what and whether retailers or manufacturers catch problems first.

Third order effects

  • Small recalls can still move markets. Only 7,824 units are involved, a tiny number against national food sales, yet the story travelled nationally because it combines a beloved brand, a feared bacterium and a product in millions of fridges. Even small producers now face national reputation risk from a single lot.
  • Transparency decides recovery. Miyoko's said it acted voluntarily and limited the recall to one lot, which is the right message. In a recall, the gap between what a company tells retailers and what it tells the public directly is where trust is won or lost.

What to watch next

  • Illness reports. Watch whether any illnesses get linked to the recalled lot, because a single confirmed case would change the risk picture and could push the FDA to reconsider the classification.
  • The recall status. Watch whether the recall stays open or closes, and whether the FDA adds lots, states or products, because an expanding recall would suggest the problem was wider than one lot.
  • The company's next statement. Watch whether Miyoko's posts a fuller public notice explaining what its testing found and what it changed, since that is the step that usually decides whether shoppers come back.
  • The best by date. Watch what happens as November 5 passes, because remaining units should be returned or discarded by then, and any product still in freezers after that date is a sign the recall did not reach everyone.
