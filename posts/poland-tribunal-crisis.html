Police Surround Poland's Constitutional Tribunal After Its President Is Suspended

By Editorial Team · 7 min read

Police surrounded Poland's Constitutional Tribunal in Warsaw on Friday night after a rival judge suspended the court's president and deputy, plunging the country into a constitutional standoff. President Karol Nawrocki turned his plane back from a trip to Senegal as protesters gathered outside the court and the tribunal's leadership accused the government of staging a coup.

What happened

  • The suspension. Judge Anna Korwin-Piotrowska issued orders suspending Constitutional Tribunal President Bogdan Święczkowski and Vice President Bartłomiej Sochański from their duties for one month, and declared that she was taking charge of the court as its longest-serving member, a status the tribunal's leadership disputes.
  • The notice. A notice announcing the suspension appeared on the court's own website at about 6 p.m. local time on Friday, October 30, and the suspended judges' profiles then vanished from the court's online portal.
  • The police deployment. Riot police deployed in numbers around the court's headquarters in Warsaw on Friday evening, and police said officers moved onto the court's grounds after incidents outside, including objects being thrown at officers. Warsaw police later said officers stayed in the external area and did not enter the building itself.
  • The protest. Dozens of protesters gathered outside the court to demonstrate against the suspension, some breached a perimeter fence, and police detained three people.
  • The president turns back. Nawrocki was flying to Senegal on a working visit when the crisis escalated, and he ordered his plane turned back over Spain, landing in Warsaw on Friday evening.
  • The president's words. Nawrocki wrote on X: "At such a moment, the President must be in Poland. I'm interrupting my flight to Senegal. I'm returning."

The rival claims

  • The tribunal rejects the suspension. The tribunal's official X account said its website had been unlawfully taken over, that Święczkowski remains president and Sochański remains vice president, and that Korwin-Piotrowska is not a judge of the court.
  • The coup accusation. The tribunal's account warned that all persons participating in the actions are committing the crime of a coup d'état, a charge its supporters repeated outside the court.
  • The suspension's stated grounds. The suspension orders accused the two senior judges of improperly discharging their official duties and blocking judges elected by the current parliamentary majority, including Korwin-Piotrowska, from taking part in the tribunal's proceedings.
  • The government defends the move. Prime Minister Donald Tusk wrote on X: "Restoring the rule of law is our duty. After years of PiS-led lawlessness, this is a difficult process, sometimes painful, and it requires decisive action. There is no other way."
  • The immunity request. Justice Minister Waldemar Żurek submitted five requests to lift immunity, four concerning Święczkowski and one concerning Sochański, so that criminal charges could be brought against them.
  • The opposition leader intervenes. Law and Justice leader Jarosław Kaczyński joined the protest outside the court, tried to enter the building, accused Tusk of staging a coup against the institution, and urged the president to intervene.

Why the court matters

  • The court can strike down laws. The Constitutional Tribunal has the power to strike down legislation, which makes control of it decisive for any government's program.
  • The dispute began in 2015. The conflict traces back to 2015, when the nationalist Law and Justice government appointed judges to the tribunal in a process the European Court of Human Rights later ruled irregular.
  • The court became a European flashpoint. Under Law and Justice rule from 2015 to 2023, the tribunal helped impose an almost total ban on abortion and challenged the supremacy of European Union law, putting Poland at odds with Brussels over judicial independence.
  • Tusk returned promising a fix. Tusk won election in 2023 promising to restore the judiciary's independence, and his government has since challenged the legitimacy of parts of the tribunal and stopped publishing some of its rulings.
  • Presidential opposition slowed him. Nawrocki has blocked Tusk's nominations to the court, and his PiS-backed predecessor Andrzej Duda also resisted the government's judicial changes.
  • The current standoff sharpened in October. The conflict sharpened on October 14, when Maciej Berek, a former minister in Tusk's government, took the judicial oath after the Sejm elected him as a tribunal judge, and it exploded on Friday with the suspension orders and the police deployment.

The fallout so far

  • An ambassador resigned. Poland's ambassador to China, Jakub Kumoch, resigned following the developments, writing on X that he had concluded it was no longer possible for him to continue serving as ambassador.
  • The president seeks counsel. After returning to Warsaw, Nawrocki was scheduled to consult Zbigniew Kapiński, the First President of the Supreme Court, about the attempted suspension of the tribunal's president.
  • The court's website went dark. The tribunal's website became inaccessible on Friday evening, leaving the competing claims about who leads the court unresolved in public.
  • The trip that was cut short. Nawrocki had been due to spend October 30 to November 1 in Senegal on a working visit linked to the Youth Olympic Games in Dakar, after an invitation from Senegalese President Bassirou Diomaye Faye.

What to watch next

  • Who opens the court on Monday. Whether Korwin-Piotrowska or Święczkowski exercises the president's powers when the tribunal next sits, and whether police remain deployed at the building.
  • The criminal track. Whether parliament lifts the two suspended judges' immunity and prosecutors bring the charges Żurek is seeking.
  • The president's next move. Whether Nawrocki convenes the National Security Council or takes other formal steps after his consultations with the Supreme Court's leadership.
  • The European reaction. Whether Brussels weighs in on a standoff that revives the rule of law dispute that has dogged Poland's relationship with the European Union for a decade.
