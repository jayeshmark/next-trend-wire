Dolores Huerta Died at 96 After Seven Decades of Organizing

By Editorial Team · 7 min read

Dolores Huerta, the labor leader who co-founded the United Farm Workers and coined Si, se puede, died on October 28, 2026, at her home in Bakersfield, California. She was 96. Her family said she died of cancer, and her death closed a seven decade career that reshaped farm labor in America.

What happened

  • Death at home in Bakersfield. Huerta died on October 28 at home in Bakersfield, surrounded by her close family, according to the memorial published by the Dolores Huerta Foundation. She was 96, and her family asked for privacy while noting that people around the world shared in their mourning.
  • A family statement through the foundation. Her family announced her death in a statement issued through the Dolores Huerta Foundation, saying they would follow up with plans to recognize and celebrate her life and legacy. The foundation's memorial presents her as a labor leader, feminist and environmental justice advocate who transformed the possibilities of Latina leadership in America.
  • Flags lowered in California. California Governor Gavin Newsom ordered flags flown at half-staff in her honor, and he and First Partner Jennifer Siebel Newsom issued a joint statement praising her decades of labor and civil rights organizing. The governor's office recalled her role in the Delano grape boycott and said her organizing fundamentally reshaped California.

Why it matters

  • The 1962 founding changed labor law. In 1962, Huerta joined Cesar Chavez in building the National Farm Workers Association, which merged in 1966 with the Filipino led Agricultural Workers Organizing Committee to form the organization that became the United Farm Workers. The merger joined Mexican and Filipino workers who had previously organized separately, and it gave the movement the scale to win contracts.
  • The boycott made consumers participants. During the Delano strike and nationwide grape boycott, Huerta helped turn the struggles of California farmworkers into a national moral and economic confrontation, and the foundation's memorial says 17 million people stopped buying grapes. The boycott worked because it gave ordinary shoppers a direct role, and it became a model for consumer campaigns that followed.
  • A law followed the organizing. Her advocacy helped win passage of California's landmark Agricultural Labor Relations Act in 1975, giving farmworkers the right to organize and bargain collectively. The law turned the movement's gains into a legal right, which is why it survived long after any single strike or boycott ended.
  • Honors measured the arc. In 2012, Huerta received the Presidential Medal of Freedom from President Obama, and President Clinton had honored her in 1998 with the Eleanor Roosevelt Award for Human Rights. At least 11 schools across the country bear her name, and the foundation says her phrase Si, se puede became a rallying cry for workers, women and immigrants around the world.

Second order effects

  • The UFW must lead without its co-founder. Huerta was the union's principal architect and its most famous living link to the Delano era. Her death forces the United Farm Workers to show that the organization can recruit, bargain and campaign on its own institutional strength rather than on the authority of a founder, and the next contract fights will be the first test.
  • The foundation becomes the keeper of the method. Huerta created the Dolores Huerta Foundation with her three daughters to teach a new generation the organizing lessons she had spent a lifetime learning. With her gone, the foundation's training of community organizers in the Central Valley becomes the main way her approach survives, and its planned Peace and Justice Cultural Center, set to open in 2027, becomes a permanent home for that work.
  • Women's leadership in labor loses a model. Huerta was often the only woman in the room and almost always one of the few women with real power, and the foundation's memorial says she challenged deeply rooted ideas about whether women should lead. Her death removes a living example for Latinas and other women of color entering labor and politics, and the movement will need new visible leaders to fill that role.

Third order effects

  • The movement's history gets retold. In March 2026, Huerta disclosed that Cesar Chavez had sexually assaulted her in the 1960s, saying she had kept the secret for decades because she feared the truth could damage the farmworker movement. Her death reopens that reckoning, because obituaries and memorials now have to tell the story of a movement led by imperfect people, and the way institutions handle that complexity will shape how younger activists judge the movement itself.
  • Credit gets redistributed. Huerta spent decades seen as Chavez's partner rather than as the movement's principal strategist and negotiator. Her death, and the foundation's memorial calling her the principal architect of the union's power, accelerates a correction in how history assigns credit, and future accounts of the farmworker movement will center her negotiating and boycott work more than past accounts did.

What to watch next

  • Memorial plans from the family. The family said it would follow up with plans to recognize and celebrate her life and legacy. Watch for public memorials in Bakersfield and beyond, and for whether they become organizing events in her style or purely ceremonial tributes.
  • The 2027 cultural center. The foundation plans to open the Dolores Huerta Peace and Justice Cultural Center in the Central Valley in 2027. Its opening will be the first major test of whether the foundation can carry her work forward without her, and whether it attracts the organizers and funding it needs.
  • Reconsideration of Chavez honors. Watch whether more schools, streets and parks revisit honors for Chavez in light of Huerta's disclosure. Each decision will signal how institutions balance a legacy of achievement against documented harm, and the pattern of those decisions will define the movement's public history.
