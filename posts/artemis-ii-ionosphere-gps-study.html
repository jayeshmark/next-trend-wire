Artemis II Tore Holes in the Upper Atmosphere Big Enough to Shift GPS Readings

By Editorial Team · 7 min read

NASA's Artemis II mission punched temporary holes in Earth's upper atmosphere so large they stretched 3,100 miles across North America, according to a study reported on November 4. The disturbances were strong enough to shift GPS readings by several metres, a warning for self driving cars and drones as heavy launches become routine.

What the study found

  • Three disturbances from one mission. Researchers at National Cheng Kung University in Taiwan found that Artemis II produced three separate disturbances in the ionosphere, the electrically charged layer of the upper atmosphere, over the same region within about 25 hours, the first time a single space mission has done so.
  • The holes spanned a continent. Each disturbance stretched about 5,000 kilometres, or 3,100 miles, across North America, with the largest extending about 50 degrees of longitude, according to the study posted as a preprint.
  • The strongest came after liftoff. The launch itself, at 22:35 UTC on April 1, 2026, set off shock and gravity waves in the atmosphere, but the most intense disturbance followed an engine burn by the Orion spacecraft about two hours later, with a third, weaker disturbance after the burn that sent Orion toward the Moon.
  • It beat every previous launch. The disturbances were stronger than any other rocket launch has caused so far, and up to ten times more intense than those seen after SpaceX Starship test flights, even though Starship has roughly twice the liftoff thrust of NASA's Space Launch System.
  • The upper stage fired inside the ionosphere. The Orion burns took place about 115 miles above Earth, directly inside the ionosphere, where the exhaust reacted with electrically charged particles and carved out the gaps, like a smaller and shorter lived solar storm.

How the researchers measured it

  • They used GPS receivers as sensors. The team analysed data from about 3,500 ground based GPS, GLONASS and Galileo receivers across the Americas, measuring how long satellite signals took to travel through the atmosphere to track changes in the concentration of charged particles.
  • The changes showed up as GPS errors. The measurements indicated positioning errors of several metres across North America, the kind of error that could put a self driving car in the wrong lane or shift a drone off its course.
  • No outages were recorded. The study found no evidence of widespread GPS outages, car crashes or drones going off course, and the disturbances were temporary, fading as the ionosphere recovered.
  • The work is a preprint. The paper, led by Jong-Min Choi with Charles C. H. Lin as corresponding author, is posted on the ESS Open Archive and has not been peer reviewed, so its findings are preliminary.
  • The mission itself made history. Artemis II launched from Kennedy Space Center on April 1, 2026, carrying Reid Wiseman, Victor Glover, Christina Koch and Jeremy Hansen around the Moon, the first crewed flight past the Moon in more than 50 years.

Why it matters

  • Heavy launches are becoming routine. Artemis II was the first of a planned series of Moon missions, and the study's authors warn the effect could grow as flights to the Moon and Mars increase, with more giant rockets firing engines high in the atmosphere.
  • GPS is now critical infrastructure. Positioning signals guide everything from aircraft and ships to farm equipment and delivery drones, so even errors of a few metres can matter when machines, not people, are doing the steering.
  • The risk is new, not hypothetical. The study shows a single launch can disturb the sky over an entire continent, which means launch planners may need to account for atmospheric effects they have largely ignored until now.
  • The Moon program has a side effect. NASA's return to the Moon is also a large scale experiment in the upper atmosphere, and each launch writes a temporary signature across the sky that scientists are only now learning to read.
  • It reframes the cost of going big. The Space Launch System is the most powerful rocket NASA has ever flown, and its very power is what makes its atmospheric footprint larger than any rocket before it.

What to watch next

  • More Artemis flights. Whether later missions in the Artemis program produce similar disturbances, and whether NASA adjusts launch timing or trajectories to limit the effect.
  • Real time warnings. Whether space weather forecasters start issuing GPS advisories around major launches, the way they do for solar storms.
  • The next generation of rockets. Whether Starship, New Glenn and other heavy lift vehicles show the same pattern when their upper stages fire inside the ionosphere.
  • Autonomous vehicle testing. Whether car and drone makers build in safeguards for the brief positioning errors a big launch can cause.
  • The follow up research. What the Taiwan team finds when it applies the same GPS measurement technique to other large launches, and whether the Artemis II result turns out to be typical or exceptional.
