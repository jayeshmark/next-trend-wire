Nasdaq Drops 2.86 Percent in Worst Day Since June as AI Spending Fears Roil Tech

By Editorial Team · 7 min read

US stocks tumbled on Friday, October 30, as the Nasdaq Composite fell 2.86 percent in its worst day since June. Disappointing results from Intel and Amazon deepened worries that massive spending on artificial intelligence is not paying off fast enough, capping a brutal October for technology shares.

What happened

  • The Nasdaq led the fall. The Nasdaq Composite dropped 747.73 points, or 2.86 percent, to close at 25,437.00, its largest one-day percentage decline since June 5.
  • The broader market fell too. The S&P 500 lost 140.32 points, or 1.82 percent, to close at 7,580.07, its worst day since June, while the Dow Jones Industrial Average fell 572.25 points, or 1.09 percent, to 51,770.31.
  • All three indexes ended the week lower. The S&P 500 and the Nasdaq also finished October with monthly losses, while the Dow finished the month higher.
  • The Nasdaq is well off its peak. The index closed 7.8 percent below its record closing high from October 6, after two days of heavy selling to end the month.
  • October was the worst month in over a year. The Nasdaq fell 5.3 percent in October, its steepest monthly decline since March 2025, while the S&P 500 slipped 0.9 percent and the Dow gained 1.7 percent.

What triggered it

  • Intel sank on its outlook. Intel forecast fourth quarter revenue of $15.0 billion to $16.0 billion, below what Wall Street expected, and its shares plunged about 29 percent, even though the chipmaker had just reported third quarter revenue of $16.5 billion, up 21 percent from a year earlier.
  • Amazon disappointed on cash. Amazon reported third quarter 2026 net sales of $201.9 billion, up 12 percent from a year earlier, but its shares fell after the results showed heavy spending on AI infrastructure, and the stock failed to reassure investors that the spending is paying off quickly.
  • Alphabet had already rattled the market. A day earlier, shares of Google parent Alphabet fell sharply after the company disclosed a greater cash outflow in the summer than in the spring, starting the two day slide that Friday completed.
  • Chip stocks slid across the board. Nvidia and AMD fell sharply alongside Intel, and the broader semiconductor sector dropped hard, as investors punished the companies most tied to the AI buildout.
  • Crypto weakened too. Bitcoin and other cryptocurrencies fell after hackers drained an estimated $6.3 billion from Binance, adding to the risk-off mood that gripped markets on Friday.

Why it matters

  • The AI trade is being repriced. For two years, investors paid up for any company spending on AI. Now they are asking when that spending turns into profit, and results that merely meet forecasts are no longer enough to hold a stock up.
  • Guidance matters more than the quarter. Intel beat its own third quarter expectations and still cratered, because its fourth quarter forecast came in light. Amazon grew sales 12 percent and still fell, because its spending overshadowed its growth.
  • The damage is concentrated, not broad. The Dow finished October higher even as the Nasdaq posted its worst month since March 2025, which shows the sell-off is a tech story first, not a whole economy story.
  • The timing is political. The sell-off landed days before Tuesday's midterm elections, handing voters a fresh reminder of market volatility just as they head to the polls.

What to watch next

  • Tuesday's midterms. Whether election results calm or deepen the market's nerves, and whether Washington's next moves change the outlook for tech regulation and spending.
  • The next wave of earnings. Whether other big technology companies reassure investors about returns on AI spending, or confirm the worries that drove Friday's selling.
  • The Nasdaq's next test. Whether the index stabilizes near its current level or extends its slide further below the October 6 record, which would confirm a deeper correction in technology shares.
  • Chip demand signals. Whether Intel's warning about a PC slowdown spreads to other chipmakers, or stays contained to one company's forecast.
