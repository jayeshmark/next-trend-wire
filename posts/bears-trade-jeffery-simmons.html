Bears Land Jeffery Simmons in Blockbuster Trade With Titans

By Editorial Team · 7 min read

The Chicago Bears agreed to acquire All-Pro defensive tackle Jeffery Simmons from the Tennessee Titans on Thursday, October 29, 2026, in a deal ahead of the November 10 trade deadline, sending two first-round picks to Tennessee. The trade was agreed Thursday morning and is pending a physical, and it pairs Simmons with a Chicago defense ranked among the league's best.

What happened

  • The deal. The Titans are trading Simmons and a fifth-round pick in the 2028 draft to the Bears in exchange for a first-round pick in 2027, a fourth-round pick in 2027, and a first-round pick in 2028. The two teams agreed terms on the compensation on Thursday morning, pending a physical, according to the Titans' announcement.
  • The player. Simmons is 29 and was the 19th overall pick of the 2019 draft out of Mississippi State. He made four Pro Bowls and served as a five-time captain in Tennessee, and he earned his first First Team AP All-Pro selection in 2025 with a career-high 11 sacks, 17 tackles for loss, and 21 quarterback hits. Across eight seasons he has totaled 46.5 sacks, 72 tackles for loss, and 96 quarterback hits, and through seven games this season he has 4 sacks, 6 tackles for loss, and 9 quarterback hits.
  • The timing. The move lands 12 days before the November 10 trade deadline, with the Bears at 5-2 and in second place in the NFC North and the Titans at 1-6 in their first season under head coach Robert Saleh. Just one day earlier, Simmons told reporters he had heard nothing about a trade, saying his goal was to be where his feet are and that he had not heard anything from his agent or from the organization.
  • The farewell. Titans controlling owner Amy Adams Strunk released a statement thanking Simmons, saying there are few people like him and that the team is forever grateful for all he has given the team and the city. She added that his presence on the field was surpassed only by what he did off it, pointing to his Give 'Em A Reason Foundation and its work in Middle Tennessee and Mississippi.
  • The contract. Simmons signed a three-year extension with Tennessee in June, reported at $105.8 million with $100 million guaranteed, running through the 2030 season. The reported $35.27 million average annual value made him the highest-paid defensive tackle in NFL history at signing, ahead of Kansas City's Chris Jones.
  • The history. Simmons is the first defensive tackle in the common draft era, since 1967, to be traded for multiple first-round picks, according to ESPN's Adam Schefter. That rarity is the clearest measure of how highly Chicago values a 29-year-old interior lineman in his prime.

Why it matters

  • Chicago is going for it. The Bears have not reached a Super Bowl since the 2006 season, and this trade is an all-in move from a 5-2 team chasing its first Lombardi Trophy since the 1985 team. Giving up two first-round picks for a defensive tackle only makes sense if Chicago believes its window is open right now.
  • The defense gets its missing piece. Chicago's defense already ranks No. 8 in yards allowed and No. 9 in points allowed, and Simmons joins veteran Grady Jarrett inside plus Montez Sweat, who just returned from a two-game suspension. Sweat and Simmons were teammates at Mississippi State, and together with Jarrett they give Chicago a veteran interior rotation built for January football.
  • Tennessee chose the rebuild. The Titans get two extra first-round picks now and four across the 2027 and 2028 drafts, which is the currency of a full rebuild. General manager Mike Borgonzi framed it as a three-year roster decision, saying the team has to be real about where it is and how it gets better next year, the year after, and the year after that.
  • The signal to the locker room. Trading the team's best player and a five-time captain tells the rest of the roster plainly that 1-6 is not a rough patch to ride out but a starting point to build from. That kind of honesty can steady a rebuild, but it also puts every veteran on notice before the deadline.

Second order effects

  • The defensive tackle market resets. A first-round pick is the price of a franchise quarterback, not an interior lineman, so two of them for Simmons reprices what elite defensive tackles are worth in trades. Teams holding star linemen on losing rosters now have a new asking price, and contenders know what it costs to buy one.
  • The Jarrett question. Jarrett, 33, has zero sacks through seven starts, and Simmons arrives as the immediate upgrade at starting tackle. How Chicago splits snaps between a proven veteran and its new star will shape the defensive line rotation for the rest of the season.
  • The Sweat reunion. Sweat and Simmons played together at Mississippi State, and reuniting them in Chicago gives defensive coordinator Dennis Allen a pass rush built around familiarity. Sweat's return from suspension plus Simmons' arrival in the same week transforms a unit that was already playing well.
  • Tennessee's next moves. The Titans still have reported trade candidates on the roster, including running back Tony Pollard, cornerback Alontae Taylor, and defensive end Jermaine Johnson III. With the Simmons deal done, the next 12 days will show how far Borgonzi takes the teardown before the November 10 deadline.
  • The draft capital clock. Tennessee's extra picks only matter if they turn into players, and the 2027 draft is now the franchise's most important date. The Simmons trade will be judged less on this season than on who the Titans select with Chicago's picks.

Third order effects

  • The contender arms race. Chicago's move pressures the rest of the NFC North and the conference's contenders to answer before the deadline. When a 5-2 team adds an All-Pro in his prime, standing still starts to look like falling behind, and the next two weeks could bring more big swings.
  • The small-market rebuild model. Tennessee is betting that draft picks and cap space beat holding a star through losing seasons, the same logic that has rebuilt struggling franchises before. If the picks hit, the Simmons trade becomes the foundation of the next good Titans team; if they miss, it becomes the day the franchise gave away its best player.
  • The contract precedent. Simmons' extension reset the defensive tackle market in June, and now his trade price resets the trade market in October. Together they raise what elite interior linemen can demand in both negotiations and deals, which ripples into every upcoming extension for players at the position.
  • The fan base split. Titans fans lose the face of the franchise in the middle of a 1-6 season, while Bears fans gain a star for a playoff push. Both reactions are rational, and both will be louder if Chicago makes a deep run while Tennessee keeps losing.

What to watch next

  • The physical. The deal is pending a physical, so the first checkpoint is Simmons clearing it and the trade becoming official. Until then, the blockbuster is agreed but not done.
  • The debut. Chicago plays in prime time Monday night at Seattle against the 4-3 Seahawks, the natural stage for Simmons' first game as a Bear if he clears the physical in time.
  • The deadline. November 10 is the hard stop, and Tennessee's handling of its remaining veterans will show whether the Simmons trade was the biggest move of the teardown or just the first.
  • The picks. Chicago's 2027 first-round pick is now Tennessee property, which means every Bears result from here changes the value of what the Titans received. A deep Chicago run makes the pick later and cheaper; a collapse makes it a prize.
  • The Titans' Sunday. Tennessee visits the 4-2 Cincinnati Bengals on Sunday, its first game of the post-Simmons era, and the performance will hint at how the locker room absorbed losing its captain.
