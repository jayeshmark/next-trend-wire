The Final Week: Trump and Obama Are Rallying. The Polls Still Favor Democrats.

By Editorial Team · 7 min read

Six days before the November 3 midterms, the two final national polls point the same way: Democrats lead the race for Congress, voters say the economy is heading the wrong way, and both parties have sent their biggest names to the states that will decide control. Donald Trump is rallying in Ohio. Barack Obama is rallying in Georgia.

What happened

  • Two polls, one direction. The Reuters/Ipsos poll completed October 26 put Democrats ahead 47% to 36% among registered voters, 50% to 41% with undecided leanings, and 53% to 44% among likely voters. The October Harvard CAPS/Harris poll split it 52% to 48% for Democrats, a 4-point lead. Both agree on the direction.
  • Approval sits near record lows in both. Reuters/Ipsos put Trump's approval at 32%, up one point from the record low 31% a week earlier, while Harvard CAPS/Harris put it at 39%, down three points from last month. The seven-point gap reflects different methods and samples, so the honest reading is the trend both show, not any single number.
  • The economy dominates both polls. Reuters/Ipsos found 73% saying the cost of living is heading the wrong way. Harvard CAPS/Harris found 64% saying the economy is on the wrong track, up seven points from September, with three in five holding Trump responsible. His approval there ran lowest on inflation (33%), tariffs and trade (34%), and the economy (34%), and highest on immigration and fighting crime in cities (44% each).
  • The closing rallies map the fight. Trump returned to Ohio on Wednesday for the third time in a month, appearing with Senator Jon Husted against former Senator Sherrod Brown, after stops in Mississippi and Texas, with South Carolina, Florida, Kansas, and Iowa ahead. Vice President JD Vance campaigned in North Carolina for Michael Whatley against former Governor Roy Cooper, while former President Barack Obama rallied in Georgia with Senator Jon Ossoff and Keisha Lance Bottoms, after stops in Cleveland and Detroit.
  • Texas is effectively tied. The October 21 Fox News poll put Democrat James Talarico and Republican Ken Paxton tied at 49% each among likely voters, down from Talarico's 51% to 49% lead in its previous poll. Emerson put Talarico ahead 48% to 46%, the Cook Political Report 49% to 44%, while other October polls showed narrow Talarico leads or ties, with the Libertarian at 3%.

Why it matters

  • The House is the Democrats' to lose; the Senate is the fight. Analysts see Democrats with a good chance in the House but a steeper climb in the Senate, where they need a net gain of four seats.
  • Approval this low rarely survives contact with voters. Since World War II, the president's party has lost House seats in almost every midterm, except 1998 and 2002. A president near record-low approval, with three quarters saying living costs are going the wrong way, is the classic headwind.
  • Texas is the Senate in miniature. A Talarico win opens new routes to a Democratic majority; a Paxton hold preserves a Republican stronghold and forces Democrats to sweep nearly every other competitive race.
  • The economy question is still narrow. Reuters/Ipsos found voters favoring Democrats on the economy 37% to 33%, and Harvard CAPS/Harris found a small majority believing Democrats can do a better job. The lead is real but thin.

Second order effects

  • Losing either chamber changes the final two years. A Democratic House would block Trump's agenda and open investigations, while a Republican hold on both chambers would let him govern unchecked through the end of his term.
  • The rally map reveals a defensive campaign. Trump is spending the closing days in states he won handily two years ago, returning to Ohio for the third time in a month, while Vance shores up North Carolina, where Republicans are pessimistic about holding the seat. Campaigns that are winning do not spend the final week defending their own ground.
  • Nationalized races squeeze local candidates. Democrats want the vote to read as a check on the president, Republicans as a vote to keep delivering for him. That framing helps each party's base turn out but leaves little room for candidates' own records.
  • Each side will quote the poll that flatters it. Republicans will cite the 39% approval and 4-point race, Democrats the 32% approval and 11-point lead. The useful signal is what both polls agree on: voters are pessimistic about the economy.

Third order effects

  • The 2028 field starts forming on election night. Ossoff is already viewed as a potential future presidential candidate, and a Democratic House would give a new generation a platform, while a Republican hold keeps Trump as kingmaker.
  • Polling itself faces another verdict. Two respected polls put approval seven points apart. If results land near one and far from the other, trust in polling takes another hit. If they land between them, the lesson is to read averages and trends, never a single poll.
  • The Senate map hardens either way. A Paxton hold would confirm that even a tied race in a changing state still breaks Republican, A Talarico win would prove Texas competitiveness is real and pull national money south for years.
  • Cost-of-living politics outlasts this cycle. Whichever party wins, the next two years will be fought on prices. Tariffs, fuel costs, and the Iran war's effect on inflation become the governing test.

What to watch next

  • The House-Senate split. Watch whether Democrats take the House while Republicans hold the Senate, or whether one party sweeps both.
  • Texas. Watch whether the Libertarian's 3% melts toward Paxton on Election Day or holds and helps Talarico.
  • Ohio and North Carolina. Husted against Brown and Whatley against Cooper are the Senate bellwethers.
  • Georgia. Watch Ossoff's margin and the governor's race, with early in-person voting ending Friday, October 30.
