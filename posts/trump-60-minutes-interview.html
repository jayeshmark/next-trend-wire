Trump Tells 60 Minutes He Cannot Forgive His Enemies, Even to Reach Heaven

By Jayesh Marathe · November 9, 2026 · 7 min read

President Donald Trump said in a 60 Minutes interview that he cannot forgive his political enemies, even if forgiveness were the price of reaching heaven, and admitted his deportation policy went too tough. The interview, recorded Friday at the White House and aired Sunday, was his first sit-down since Republicans lost both chambers of Congress in the midterm elections.

Heaven and forgiveness

  • Douthat asked Trump what happens when he dies. The question came after the correspondent noted that Trump has survived multiple assassination attempts, and Trump answered, "Well, I hope I'm missed."
  • Trump said he expects to reach heaven in theory. "Well, I'm a religious person, so in theory I go to heaven," he said, and when Douthat asked if he thought he was going, he added, "I hope so. I don't want to go to hell."
  • Then Douthat put forgiveness to him as a condition of paradise. He asked whether Trump could feel love and forgiveness for former FBI Director James Comey, former House Speaker Nancy Pelosi and others he has expressed hate for, if that were all it took to enter the next life.
  • Trump said no, and said he wanted to be honest about it. "The answer is no. I want to be honest, no," he said, adding, "I probably cannot do that, no, because I do hate some of my enemies and I always will."
  • He said a yes answer would not be real. "Well, then I wouldn't be real, would I?" he said, calling his enemies extremely dishonest people, very bad people, and very bad for the country.

The midterm defeat

  • Trump opened by noting he was not on the ballot. He said that if he had been, "it would've been a much different situation," because his voters had to be transferred to candidates they did not like.
  • He blamed the war, gas prices and dislike of Israel. "They didn't like the war. They didn't like the fact that gasoline went up, 'cause I had it down at a low, and there's a great dislike, incredibly, for Israel," he said.
  • He agreed with Vice President JD Vance on the cause. Vance wrote that "The reason we lost is the war," meaning Iran, and Trump said, "I don't disagree with it." He also blamed bad messaging and endorsed candidates who struggled, including Ken Paxton in Texas and Darline Graham in South Carolina.
  • He defended the Iran war itself. "My answer is an emphatic yes because you can't let Iran have a nuclear weapon," he said, adding, "I've stopped them from having a nuclear weapon. That's a big thing."
  • Democrats won both chambers on November 3. The party took control of the House of Representatives and the Senate, and the new Congress takes office on January 3.

Deportations went too tough

  • Trump said his immigration record cut both ways. "I think it both helped and hurt. I think I got an A+ for closing up the border. I get very high marks for getting the bad ones out," he said.
  • He said the crackdown also swept up people with good jobs. "But I get hurt because we also took people that had good jobs, that created one violation, or breaking of the law, and that was that they came into the country illegally," he said.
  • Douthat asked directly whether the policy went too far. "So you think your deportation policy went too far," Douthat said, and Trump answered, "Too tough." When Douthat pressed him, Trump said, "I think it might be too tough, yeah."
  • He said he wants to work with Democrats on immigration. "I'd love to do something on immigration," he said, after Douthat noted that he had congratulated the Democrats and said he was open to working with them. James Talarico, the new Democratic senator from Texas, has named immigration as something he wants to work with Trump on.

Firings and the chief of staff switch

  • Trump said he does expect to fire people after the defeat. Asked whether he expects to fire anyone from his team, he said, "I do. Yeah," and added, "Little bit."
  • He said changes are coming, but praised his Cabinet. "I love my Cabinet. I think they're good. But I'll be doin' maybe a few changes," he said, declining to name who would go. Asked whether he still has confidence in Secretary of Defense Pete Hegseth, he said, "I do."
  • The first change came within the hour. Susie Wiles, the president's chief of staff, was replaced by Deputy Chief of Staff Stephen Miller, the architect of the president's mass deportation policy, an hour after the conversation ended.

UFOs and artificial intelligence

  • Trump said Air Force pilots told him very strange things. He described round objects passing their F-22 jets at three times their speed in meetings in the Oval Office, in his first term and a little in his second. "It's not aerodynamic, it's round," he said.
  • He said he knows of no secret program hidden from presidents. Asked whether there is a program presidents do not know about, he said, "No. And I haven't heard that. But if there is I'm gonna find out about it right after this interview."
  • He said the risk from artificial intelligence can be managed. "I think that the risk is not nearly as real as they talk. And I think the risk can be managed," he said.
  • He said he does not mind guardrails, with one condition. "I don't mind guardrails. But I don't wanna put us in a deep second position. We're leading number one. And China has no guardrails, by the way," he said, framing the race as a contest with China alone. "Whoever wins that race wins," he said. "It's all between us and China. There's nobody else."

What happens next

  • The interview sets the terms for divided government. Trump now faces Democratic majorities in both chambers, and the staff changes he promised will keep coming: the first, the Miller appointment, landed before the interview even aired.
