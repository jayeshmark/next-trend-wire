Microsoft Offered 60,000 People Money to Leave. AI Is Paying for It.

By Editorial Team · 7 min read

On Monday, October 26, Microsoft offered about 60,000 employees money to leave. The Voluntary Exit Offer covers eligible staff at level 70 and below in the United States, Canada, India and the United Kingdom. Microsoft did not announce a number. A company spokesperson confirmed about 60,000 people are eligible, roughly 27 percent of its workforce.

What happened

Microsoft announced the Voluntary Exit Offer on Monday, October 26, 2026. Chief People Officer Amy Coleman, Executive Vice President and Chief People Officer, shared the announcement with employees, and Microsoft published it on the Official Microsoft Blog.

The offer is time-limited and voluntary, with financial support and career planning resources for those who choose to leave. Eligible employees receive an email with their offer details. They have until early December to decide, and would leave on December 31. The terms resemble Microsoft's standard severance.

The offer is not open to everyone: eligible employees in participating teams at level 70 and below, with some organisations not taking part. Participation varies by each business's priorities and workforce needs. Participating teams include Copilot, CoreAI, Windows and Devices, Xbox, finance and human resources. Marketing, sales, commercial business, security, quantum and discovery teams are sitting out.

Coleman said, "This offer gives eligible employees who may be considering a future outside the company a new choice to make that transition now with support." She also said, "The Voluntary Exit Offer reflects an important part of how we're evolving our approach to change: creating greater flexibility and, where possible, more ways for employees to shape their next chapter."

One number needs care. Microsoft's own announcement did not give a number. The figure of about 60,000 comes from a Microsoft spokesperson, who confirmed it to reporters. Microsoft employed about 223,000 people full-time as of June 30, 2026, per its annual 10-K filing, so 60,000 is roughly 27 percent of the workforce.

Microsoft has done this before. In April 2026, it ran a Voluntary Retirement Program for about 8,600 to 8,750 US employees. More than 30 percent accepted. In July 2026, it cut 4,800 jobs, including 3,200 in Xbox. Microsoft expects a far lower percentage to accept this latest offer. Its shares rose about 1.1 percent to about $530.50 on Monday, October 26.

Why it matters

A buyout of this size is a statement about where the company is going, and the statement is about AI.

Capital expenditure was $41 billion in the April to June quarter. Analysts expect about $51 billion for the July to September quarter. Chief Financial Officer Amy Hood lowered the 2026 calendar-year forecast to $175 billion from $190 billion.

The offer and the spending are two sides of the same decision. Microsoft is paying more than ever for data centers, chips and power, and asking a large part of its workforce whether it wants to leave. It is not cutting people because the business is failing. It is reshaping the workforce while spending at a scale few companies have attempted.

The shape of the offer matters too. The teams taking part are the ones closest to the AI buildout: Copilot, CoreAI, Windows and Devices, Xbox. The teams sitting out are marketing, sales, security and quantum. That is a strange split for cost-cutting. It looks like a company deciding which parts of itself to rebuild first.

Second order effects

The first effect is inside Microsoft. When 60,000 people get that email, all 223,000 employees hear about it. Every employee now has to ask: does the company still want me, and do I still want to be here? Some will look elsewhere. Others will stay with less trust.

The second effect is on the teams taking part. Copilot and CoreAI are building the products Microsoft is betting on. The people most likely to take the money may be the ones with the most options elsewhere, which often means the most experienced people. That will be tested in the next two quarters, when those people are gone and the work still has to get done.

The third effect is on the rest of the tech industry. When Microsoft offers buyouts to a quarter of its workforce while spending $41 billion in a quarter on AI infrastructure, other companies will ask the same question. If Microsoft can shrink its workforce while growing its AI spending, why are we still carrying the same headcount?

Third order effects

The slower effects start with what a career at a large tech company means. For years, the deal was simple: join, stay, get promoted. A buyout offer to 27 percent of the workforce breaks that deal in public and tells every employee that loyalty is no longer the default.

The next effect is on the industry. If the largest companies all make the same trade, fewer people building software and more money building data centers, the industry starts to look different: bigger in spending, smaller in headcount. The question is whether that is temporary or permanent.

The final effect is on the public. Most people do not follow headcount; they use the products. If Copilot gets better, they will not care how many people built it. If it gets worse, they will notice. The buyout is invisible to customers today, but its results will show up in the products over the next year.

What to watch next

Watch the take-up rate. Microsoft expects far fewer to accept than the more than 30 percent who accepted in April. A low number means the offer was mostly a signal. A high one means a much smaller workforce in January.

Watch which teams lose people. Evenly spread, the effect is manageable. Clustered in one team, that team has a problem, and its products will show it first.

The next earnings report will show whether spending is still climbing while the workforce shrinks. Other large companies will read this as a test case. If it works, expect similar offers elsewhere.
