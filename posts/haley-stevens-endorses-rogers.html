Democrat Haley Stevens Endorses Republican Mike Rogers in Michigan Senate Race Two Days Before the Vote

By Jayesh Marathe · November 1, 2026 · 7 min read

Michigan Democrat Haley Stevens endorsed Republican Mike Rogers for the United States Senate on Sunday, November 1, breaking with her party's nominee two days before voters decide a race that could help determine Senate control. Stevens announced it in a video after appearing with Rogers at a Detroit church service, turning a feud into a split in the final hours.

What happened

  • The endorsement. Stevens, a Democratic member of the House of Representatives from Michigan, said in the video that she is endorsing Rogers for the United States Senate, reversing the support she gave to Democratic nominee Abdul El-Sayed after losing to him in the August primary.
  • The appearance. Stevens appeared with Rogers at a service at Perfecting Church in Detroit on Sunday morning, two days before the November 3 election, in a visible break from the Democratic unity event she had been expected to attend the same day.
  • The timing. The endorsement landed in the last weekend of the campaign, when both parties are making their final appeals in Michigan, one of the most closely watched Senate contests in the country.
  • The race. Michigan voters will choose on Tuesday between Rogers, the Republican nominee and a former congressman, and El-Sayed, the Democratic nominee, for an open Senate seat.

What Stevens said

  • Her own words. Stevens said El-Sayed had told her she was no longer welcome in his campaign and should stay away, adding: "And he's right, and that's why I am endorsing Mike Rogers for the United States Senate today."
  • A clean break. Stevens said plainly in the video, "I don't belong in Abdul's campaign," framing the endorsement as the end of a relationship that had already broken down rather than a sudden change of mind.
  • The streamer dispute. Stevens also said she does not stand with streamer Hasan Piker, whose presence around El-Sayed's campaign she has objected to, making the cultural and political divide between the two Democrats explicit in her announcement.
  • A proud Democrat, for Rogers. Stevens described herself in the video as a very proud Democrat, a line that underlined how unusual the move is: a sitting Democratic House member urging voters to back the Republican in a Senate race her party needs to win.

How the feud got here

  • A narrow primary loss. Stevens narrowly lost the Democratic Senate primary to El-Sayed in August, after a contentious campaign, and she endorsed him immediately afterward in a show of party unity that did not hold.
  • A frosty aftermath. The two Democrats never repaired the relationship, and Stevens spent the final weeks criticizing El-Sayed's positions while his campaign grew frustrated with her interventions.
  • The ad that reignited it. The feud flared again in the final week after Stevens released a statement on October 30 urging El-Sayed to disavow an outside ad that linked United States aid to Israel to high grocery prices, an ad she described as antisemitic.
  • El-Sayed's answer. El-Sayed accused Stevens of relitigating a primary that ended three months earlier, and his campaign then signaled that she was not welcome at his events, including the unity church event planned for Sunday.
  • The unity event that fell through. Stevens had been due to attend a Democratic unity church event on Sunday with El-Sayed and Representative Jim Clyburn of South Carolina, an appearance arranged to calm tensions before the election, but that plan collapsed after El-Sayed's campaign made clear she should stay away.
  • From unity event to rival's side. Instead of joining El-Sayed on Sunday, Stevens went to a different Detroit church service with Rogers, and then posted the endorsement video, completing a reversal that had been building for days.

The Democratic response

  • The state party condemns it. Michigan Democratic Party chair Curtis Hertel condemned Stevens's appearance with Rogers, saying Democrats would not let the late dispute distract them from the election, a response that treated the endorsement as a defection rather than a disagreement.
  • Senate leadership pushes back. Senate Minority Leader Chuck Schumer also criticized the move, a sign that national Democrats viewed the endorsement as a threat to party discipline in a race where every seat counts.
  • No sign of a wider break. No other Democratic officeholder joined Stevens on Sunday, and the party framed her decision as her own, not as a broader split.

Why Michigan matters

  • Control of the Senate is at stake. Republicans currently hold a 53 to 47 majority in the Senate, and Michigan is one of the handful of races both parties are fighting hardest over, because the outcome could help decide which party controls the chamber after Tuesday.
  • A seat both parties need. Democrats need to gain seats to take the Senate, while Republicans are defending their majority, so a Democratic defection in Michigan carries weight far beyond one endorsement video.
  • The final 48 hours. With voting on Tuesday, the endorsement gives Rogers a Democratic validator in the closing stretch and gives El-Sayed a fresh problem to answer, at the moment when undecided voters are making up their minds.

What happens next

  • Tuesday decides it. Michigan voters will settle the race on November 3, and Stevens's endorsement will be tested against the ballot box, where party loyalty usually runs stronger than a single endorsement.
  • Watch the party fallout. The open question after Tuesday is what happens to Stevens inside her own party, after a public endorsement of a Republican opponent that drew condemnation from the state chair and Senate leadership.
  • Watch the campaigns. Rogers will look to use the endorsement with voters who are uneasy about El-Sayed, while El-Sayed will look to hold his coalition together and keep the focus on the choice between the two nominees.
  • A split that outlasts the vote. Whatever the result, the break has exposed a divide inside Michigan Democrats over Israel and party discipline that will not disappear when the polls close.
