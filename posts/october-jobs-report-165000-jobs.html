US Added 165,000 Jobs in October as Unemployment Eased to 4.1%

By Jayesh Marathe · November 7, 2026 · 7 min read

US employers added 165,000 jobs in October, the Bureau of Labor Statistics reported Friday, while the unemployment rate eased to 4.1 percent. The gain ran well above the recent monthly pace, and revisions added 84,000 jobs to August and September, giving the labor market a stronger footing heading into the final weeks of the year.

What the report showed

  • The headline number. Total nonfarm payroll employment rose by 165,000 in October, and the unemployment rate changed little at 4.1 percent. The Bureau of Labor Statistics released the October Employment Situation report at 8:30 a.m. Eastern on Friday, November 6.
  • The revisions told the same story. August payroll growth was revised up to 186,000 from 133,000, and September was revised up to 60,000 from 29,000. Together, the two months came in 84,000 jobs higher than previously reported, so the summer slowdown now looks milder than it did at the time.
  • The gain beat the recent pace by a wide margin. October's increase ran well above the average monthly gain of 48,000 over the prior 12 months. Hiring had been drifting for much of the year, which makes a 165,000 month stand out rather than blend in.
  • Professional services led the hiring. Professional and business services added 70,000 jobs in October, the largest gain of any industry in the report, followed by health care with 42,000 and transportation and warehousing with about 25,000. Manufacturing added 19,000 jobs.
  • Pay kept climbing. Average hourly earnings for private workers rose 14 cents to $38.00 in October, up 0.4 percent on the month and 3.1 percent over the year. Wage growth at that pace keeps workers ahead of inflation without the sharp acceleration that would worry the Federal Reserve.
  • The jobless count barely moved. About 7.1 million people were unemployed in October, and the unemployment rate has stayed inside a narrow range of 4.1 percent to 4.3 percent since March. The rate ticked down from 4.2 percent in September, a small move that the Bureau of Labor Statistics described as little changed.

The softer details

  • Part time work jumped. The number of people working part time for economic reasons rose by 339,000 to 4.8 million in October. These are workers who want full time hours but cannot find them, so the rise cuts against the otherwise strong headline.
  • The broader jobless measure rose too. The U-6 rate, the Bureau of Labor Statistics gauge that adds part time workers who want full time hours and people who want a job but stopped looking recently, climbed to 7.9 percent in October from 7.6 percent in September. The official rate and the broader measure moved in opposite directions, which is the report's main tension.
  • Participation stayed flat. The labor force participation rate was 61.9 percent and the employment population ratio was 59.3 percent, both little changed for the month. Neither measure has moved much all year, which suggests the hiring gains came from people already in the labor force rather than from new entrants.
  • One comparison is missing. Year over year comparisons for the household survey are unavailable because the October 2025 reference period fell during a federal government shutdown. The gap is a data quirk, not a signal, but it means the report cannot say how the jobless count compares with a year ago.

Why it matters

  • Hiring sped up after a slow stretch. A year of gains averaging 48,000 a month had raised questions about whether the labor market was stalling, and October answered with a number more than three times that pace. The revisions matter just as much, since they show the summer was stronger than first reported and the acceleration did not start from a weak base.
  • The Federal Reserve is watching closely. The report landed a month before the Federal Reserve's December meeting, and traders raised the odds of a rate rise in December after the numbers came out. Strong hiring with steady wage growth gives the central bank room to act, while the rise in part time work gives it a reason to pause, so the December decision now hinges on the inflation data due next week.
  • Wages are growing at a sustainable pace. Pay up 3.1 percent over the year keeps workers ahead of price increases without the kind of wage surge that feeds inflation, and that balance is exactly what the Federal Reserve wants to see. If wage growth holds near 3 percent while hiring stays strong, the labor market can keep expanding without forcing the central bank's hand.
  • The two surveys disagree on the mood. The payroll survey shows employers adding jobs at a healthy clip, while the household survey shows more people stuck in part time work and a broader jobless rate climbing. Both surveys come from the same release, and the gap between them is the honest picture of a labor market that is hiring again but not yet hiring well for everyone.

What to watch next

  • The inflation report. The Consumer Price Index for October arrives on Tuesday, November 10, and it will shape the Federal Reserve's December decision more than the jobs report did. Hot inflation plus strong hiring points toward a rate rise, while cool inflation lets the central bank wait.
  • The next jobs report. November payroll data arrives on Friday, December 4, just days before the Federal Reserve meets. A second strong month would confirm October as a turning point, while a weak one would mark it as a blip.
  • Whether the pace holds. One month does not make a trend, and payroll numbers get revised twice after their first release. The test is whether November and December keep the gains above 100,000, the level that would show hiring has genuinely recovered rather than bounced.
  • The part time question. The jump in people working part time for economic reasons is the report's warning light, and the next two releases will show whether it fades as hiring broadens or keeps climbing as employers hedge. If it keeps climbing while the headline stays strong, the recovery is thinner than it looks.
