Microsoft Workers Call Work "Joyless" in Open Letter Protesting the Company's AI Push

By Editorial Team · 7 min read

Microsoft workers have gone public with an open letter that calls working at the company "joyless and demoralizing" and accuses its leadership of chasing artificial intelligence at any cost. The letter surfaced days after Microsoft offered buyouts to about 60,000 employees, and it turns a workforce story into a public fight over how AI is changing work.

The letter

  • The signature. A group of current and former Microsoft employees is circulating an open letter signed "Concerned Microsoft Workers," and it accuses the company of chasing AI at the cost of its own people, its customers and its climate goals.
  • The charge. The letter says Microsoft is pursuing AI with a "speed-at-all-costs" approach, and it ties the slump in morale to the period when the company ramped up its AI investments.
  • The quote. "Working at Microsoft has become joyless and demoralizing," the letter says, and it points to heavy workloads, constant reorganizations and the threat of being replaced by AI as the reasons.
  • The timing. The letter began circulating among colleagues before the October 26 buyout announcement, and it surfaced publicly this week as attention turned to the buyout program and what it says about the company.
  • The daily reality. Workers say they are pushed to use AI tools in almost every part of their jobs, often with little or no training, and the only reason given is speed.
  • The safety claim. The letter claims the company's own research shows AI agents do not put user safety first, and it says internal planning documents describe ways to get people hooked on AI products.

What the workers want

  • A say in AI. The letter asks Microsoft to involve employees in decisions about how AI is built and used, instead of imposing tools from above and measuring workers on how fast they adopt them.
  • No AI tied to pay. It calls on the company to stop linking AI use to rewards or reprimands, so that refusing a tool or questioning one does not cost an employee a raise or a good review.
  • Community protections. The letter's demands fall under three headings, and the second is no AI without community protections, meaning the technology should not be deployed in ways that harm the communities around it.
  • Climate promises kept. The third heading is no trading away environmental promises for AI, and the letter asks Microsoft to recommit to being carbon negative, water positive and zero waste by 2030.
  • Who can sign. The group is open to full time staff, employees of subsidiaries like GitHub and LinkedIn, vendors, interns and contractors, so the letter speaks for the whole workforce rather than one corner of it.

The buyout behind it

  • The offer. On October 26, Microsoft offered a Voluntary Exit Offer to about 60,000 employees at level 70 and below in the United States, Canada, India and the United Kingdom, and Microsoft published the offer on its official blog.
  • The scale. About 60,000 people is roughly 27 percent of a workforce of about 223,000, which makes it the largest buyout offer in the company's history.
  • The deadline. Eligible employees have until December 9 to decide, and those who accept would leave on December 31, with financial support and career planning resources.
  • The expectation. Microsoft expects fewer than 5 percent of eligible employees to sign up, a far lower take up than the more than 30 percent who accepted a retirement offer in April.
  • The company line. Chief People Officer Amy Coleman called the offer "a new choice to make that transition now with support," and she said the buyouts are not related to how AI is changing work.
  • The pattern. This is Microsoft's second buyout of 2026, after retirement offers to about 8,600 US employees in April and 4,800 layoffs in July, including 3,200 in Xbox.

The money behind the tension

  • The quarter. Two days after the buyouts were announced, Microsoft reported quarterly profit of $36.6 billion, up 32 percent, on revenue of $92.3 billion, according to its first quarter results.
  • The raises. Employees said merit raises this year were unusually small, in some cases under 1 percent, even as the company posted a 32 percent jump in profit.
  • The town hall. At a town hall last month, a question about how leadership balanced more than $100 billion a year in AI spending against flat rewards drew more than 80 reactions, about double what most questions get.
  • The example. Gretchen Thomas, 57, a design leader who left in September after five years, said her stock award was cut partly for not consistently showing speed and a willingness to experiment with AI.
  • The spending. Microsoft spent $41 billion on capital expenditure in the April to June quarter, mostly on data centers, chips and power, and analysts expected about $51 billion for the July to September quarter.

What happens next

  • The decision date. December 9 is the day the buyout picture becomes real, when Microsoft learns how many of the 60,000 eligible employees chose the money over the job.
  • The take up. A low acceptance rate means the offer was mostly a signal, while a high one means a much smaller workforce in January and a test of whether the teams closest to the AI buildout can keep shipping.
  • The letter test. The open letter gives workers a public list of demands, and the test now is whether leadership answers it or lets it sit while the buyouts run their course.
  • The wider signal. Microsoft is the largest company yet to face a public revolt from its own staff over AI, and other big tech workforces are watching to see whether an open letter changes anything at all.
