Ring Launches a $250 Drone Camera to Patrol Your Home

By Jayesh Marathe · November 10, 2026 · 7 min read

Ring launched Ring Robotics on November 10, a new line of autonomous security devices led by the $249.99 Ring Drone Cam, a flying indoor camera that patrols a home on its own, and the $999.99 Ring Rover Cam, a robot that patrols businesses. The drone ships January 27, 2027, and the rover ships December 16.

The two devices

  • Ring Drone Cam. A flying indoor security camera that goes where a fixed camera cannot reach, flying custom routes through a home to check rooms on a schedule, on demand, or when another Ring device raises an alarm.
  • Ring Rover Cam. An autonomous security robot built for businesses that patrols floors, investigates disturbances, and streams video in up to 4K, with a periscope camera that rises to see over counters and shelving.
  • A new category. Ring calls Ring Robotics a new category of autonomous, mobile security, devices that patrol, investigate, and respond automatically instead of waiting fixed to a wall.
  • The dates. The Ring Rover Cam begins shipping in the United States on December 16, 2026, and the Ring Drone Cam follows on January 27, 2027. The rover is available to preorder now, while the drone is open for email signups only.

What the drone does

  • Whole home visibility. Ring Drone flies custom routes to spots a fixed camera cannot reach, giving a home coverage without a camera mounted in every room.
  • Automatic incident response. When a Ring camera, sensor, or alarm is triggered, Ring Drone automatically flies to preset patrol points to investigate, so it is already in the air showing what set off the alert.
  • Wall to wall coverage. The drone records in clear 2K video with a 148 degree field of view and the ability to rotate 360 degrees, covering a whole room from a single hover.
  • Day and night. Two built in LED lights switch on automatically in low light, so the drone keeps recording clear video after dark.
  • Out of sight when idle. Ring Drone stays docked and out of sight when it is not flying, with its camera powered down until a flight is triggered.

What the rover does

  • A guard that never clocks out. Ring Rover can sweep a floor after close and investigate when a sensor trips at 2 a.m., streaming it all to a phone, covering ground that would take many fixed cameras to watch.
  • Scheduled patrols. Businesses can set automated routes at the intervals that work for them, sending the rover on regular sweeps before open or after close.
  • A closer look. On arrival at a patrol point, Ring Rover performs a 360 degree scan, and its adjustable periscope mast lets it see over counters, shelving, and obstacles, saving the height at each stop for future patrols.
  • Active deterrence. A built in siren and light tower make it clear the space is protected, and two way audio lets someone speak up in real time from anywhere.
  • Continuous video. Ring Rover streams and records in up to 4K video whenever it is moving or patrolling, not just at designated stops, so the whole route is on record.
  • Always alert. Continuous person motion detection means Ring Rover can alert its owner and start recording the moment it detects motion in its view, even while docked.
  • Built on Astro. Ring Rover grew out of feedback from Amazon Astro customers, who said the roving security feature was what they loved most.

AI Pilot runs both

  • It maps the space. AI Pilot, a custom built system, uses the devices' cameras, specialized vision models, on device sensors, and cloud processing to see, think, and navigate autonomously.
  • Simple setup. To map a home, the owner carries Ring Drone through the space or lets Ring Rover explore on its own, and AI Pilot turns what it sees into a floor plan.
  • It picks the patrol points. AI Pilot identifies rooms and suggests patrol points, spots worth extra attention such as a side door, windows, a register, or a long corridor, while owners can add their own.
  • It plans the route. When a patrol or flight starts, AI Pilot plans the most efficient route to each preset point, then reacts in real time, navigating around furniture, shelving, and any obstacle in its path.

Prices, plans, and the FCC catch

  • A subscription is required. A compatible Ring Protect plan is required for scheduled flights and patrols, so the headline prices are not the whole cost of ownership.
  • The drone still needs FCC approval. Ring Drone Cam has not been authorized as required by the rules of the Federal Communications Commission, and the device is not, and may not be, offered for sale or lease, or sold or leased, until authorization is obtained. That is why Ring is only taking email signups for the drone for now.
  • Privacy by default. Ring says privacy is the default, not a feature. Ring Drone powers down in its dock with its camera off, and Ring Rover has a Sleep Mode that retracts the periscope and turns off the microphone with a single tap.
  • You always know when they record. Both devices have LED indicators, and Ring Rover says so on its own screen. The drone's propellers are audible and the rover's light tower is bright and easy to spot, so anyone in the room knows they are being watched.
  • Encrypted video. Videos from both devices will be protected by Throw Away the Key encryption, Ring's strongest default encryption yet.

Why it matters

  • Security that moves. Ring started in a garage with a single video doorbell, and Ring Robotics is its biggest step past that doorbell, turning a fixed camera company into a robotics company.
  • The pitch. "For years, customers told us they wanted eyes in the places a fixed camera couldn't reach, and peace of mind in the spots they'd never want one," said Jamie Siminoff, founder and chief inventor of Ring. "The honest answer wasn't more cameras; it meant reimagining what a security device could be."
  • The test. The drone is the product to watch. A flying camera inside a home raises sharper privacy questions than any doorbell, and its January ship date depends on a federal authorization Ring does not have yet.
