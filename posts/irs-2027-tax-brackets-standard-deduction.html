IRS Raises 2027 Tax Brackets and Standard Deduction

By Editorial Team · 7 min read

The Internal Revenue Service has released the 2027 tax brackets and standard deduction, lifting the income thresholds at every rate and raising the standard deduction to $16,600 for single filers and $33,200 for married couples filing jointly. The new figures apply to income earned in 2027 and the tax returns filed in early 2028.

What changed

  • The IRS announced the new figures on November 5. The release, IR-2026-133, sets the annual inflation adjustments for almost 70 tax provisions for tax year 2027, with the full tables published in Revenue Procedure 2026-35.
  • The tax rates stay the same. The seven rates remain 10, 12, 22, 24, 32, 35 and 37 percent. Only the income thresholds move, so a larger share of each paycheck falls in the lower brackets.
  • The standard deduction rises for every filing status. Single filers and married people filing separately get $16,600, up $500 from $16,100 in 2026. Married couples filing jointly get $33,200, up $1,000 from $32,200. Heads of household get $24,925, up $775 from $24,150.
  • The top rate starts higher. The 37 percent rate now applies to taxable income above $661,375 for single filers and above $793,650 for married couples filing jointly.
  • The child tax credit grows. The maximum credit rises to $2,300 per child, up $100 from $2,200, and the refundable portion rises to $1,800, also up $100 from 2026.
  • The IRS tied the release to the Working Families Tax Cuts. "Hardworking American taxpayers will continue to benefit from the Working Families Tax Cuts and will see WFTC credits and deductions increase with this adjustment," said IRS Chief Executive Officer Frank J. Bisignano.

The 2027 brackets in full

  • Single filers. The 10 percent rate covers taxable income up to $12,800, 12 percent runs to $52,025, 22 percent to $109,125, 24 percent to $208,325, 32 percent to $264,550, 35 percent to $661,375, and 37 percent applies above that.
  • Married couples filing jointly. The 10 percent rate covers taxable income up to $25,600, 12 percent runs to $104,050, 22 percent to $218,250, 24 percent to $416,650, 32 percent to $529,100, 35 percent to $793,650, and 37 percent applies above that.
  • Heads of household. The 10 percent rate covers taxable income up to $18,250, 12 percent runs to $69,650, 22 percent to $109,100, 24 percent to $208,300, 32 percent to $264,550, 35 percent to $661,350, and 37 percent applies above that.
  • Married people filing separately. The thresholds match the single filer schedule through the 35 percent bracket, with the 37 percent rate applying above $396,825.
  • A single filer keeps more in the 12 percent bracket. A single filer pays a top rate of 12 percent on taxable income up to $52,025 in 2027, which means a raise that only keeps pace with inflation does not push that income into the 22 percent bracket.
  • Capital gains thresholds moved too. The zero percent capital gains rate now applies up to $102,100 for joint filers, $68,350 for heads of household and $51,050 for single filers, while the 15 percent rate tops out at $633,600 for joint filers and $563,200 for single filers.

More credits and limits that moved

  • Earned income tax credit. The maximum credit rises to $8,496 for workers with three or more qualifying children, up from $8,231 in 2026. The maximum is $7,552 for two children, $4,573 for one child and $685 for workers with no qualifying children.
  • Alternative minimum tax. The exemption rises to $93,000 for unmarried filers, with the phaseout starting at $515,950, and to $144,700 for joint filers, with the phaseout starting at $1,031,900.
  • Estate and gift taxes. The estate tax exclusion rises to $15,480,000 for people who die in 2027, up from $15,000,000 in 2026. The annual gift exclusion rises to $20,000 per recipient, up from $19,000.
  • Adoption credit. The maximum credit rises to $18,250, up from $17,670, and the refundable portion is $5,280.
  • Health flexible spending. The salary reduction limit for health flexible spending arrangements rises to $3,500, up $100, and the maximum carryover of unused amounts rises to $700, up $20.
  • Foreign earned income. The exclusion rises to $137,300, up from $132,900 in 2026.
  • Business expensing. Taxpayers can expense up to $2,640,000 of section 179 property in 2027, up from $2,560,000 in 2026.
  • The educator expense deduction. Teachers can deduct up to $350 for classroom books, supplies and equipment under the 2027 figures.

What did not change

  • Personal exemptions stay at zero. The personal exemption remains at zero for 2027, as it was in 2026, and the release notes the elimination was made permanent by the Working Families Tax Cuts.
  • The return you file next spring is unaffected. The 2027 figures govern income earned in 2027 and the returns filed in early 2028. The return due in spring 2027 still uses the 2026 brackets and the $16,100 standard deduction for single filers.
  • The rates themselves are set by law. The annual release only adjusts thresholds and amounts for inflation. Changing the seven rates would take an act of Congress, and nothing in this release does that.
  • The extra amount for older and blind filers is small. The additional standard deduction for age or blindness is $1,700 for 2027, or $2,100 for a filer who is unmarried and not a surviving spouse.

What to check now

  • Your withholding. The new tables govern pay earned from January 2027, so the tax taken from your paycheck changes with the new year even if your salary stays the same.
  • Your bracket. Compare your expected 2027 taxable income with the new thresholds to see whether a raise moves you into a higher bracket or whether the wider bands keep you where you are.
  • Your credits. Families should check the higher child tax credit and earned income credit amounts, since both rose and both are claimed on the 2027 return.
  • Your payroll or tax software. Employers and tax software update their tables for the new figures before the 2027 filing season, so the new standard deduction should show up automatically in early 2028.
  • The full tables. The IRS published every adjusted amount in Revenue Procedure 2026-35, which covers nearly 70 provisions, including education credits and other items that sit outside the headline numbers.
