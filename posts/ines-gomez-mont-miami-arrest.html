Inés Gómez Mont, Identified in Reports, Was Secured in Miami After Five Years as a Fugitive

By Editorial Team · 7 min read

Mexico's attorney general's office confirmed on October 30, 2026 that a woman it identified only as "Inés N" was secured in Miami, Florida, after information was shared with the United States government. Reports identified her as former television host Inés Gómez Mont, out of Mexican authorities' reach since warrants were issued in 2021.

What the attorney general confirmed

  • The announcement. Mexico's Attorney General's Office, known as the FGR, said on X on October 30 that "Inés N" was secured in Miami, Florida, as a result of information shared with the United States government.
  • The warrant behind it. The FGR said she has an outstanding arrest warrant in Mexico for her probable participation in the offense of tax fraud equivalent, and it identified her in its statement only as "Inés N," the standard anonymized form used for people under investigation in Mexico.
  • What the statement did not say. The FGR statement named no United States agency involved in the operation, gave no custody location, and said nothing about whether Mexico will seek her extradition or her deportation, promising only that more information would follow.
  • The identification. Reports in Mexico and the United States identified the person secured in Miami as Gómez Mont, the former TV Azteca host. The FGR statement itself gave only the anonymized "Inés N" and the warrant basis, so the full-name identification rests on those reports, not on the FGR's wording.

Who Gómez Mont is

  • The television career. Gómez Mont built her public profile as a Mexican television host, best known as the face of TV Azteca's Los 25+ and as a co-host of the entertainment program Ventaneando.
  • The disappearance from public life. After arrest warrants were issued in 2021, she stopped her public television and social media career and stayed out of reach of Mexican authorities, while her husband, businessman Víctor Manuel Álvarez Puga, faced warrants in the same investigation.
  • The husband's separate detention. Her husband was detained in Miami in September 2025 by United States immigration authorities over his immigration status, according to reports, a detention that was separate from the October 2026 action against Gómez Mont herself.

The case behind the warrants

  • The investigation opened in 2019. The FGR opened its investigation in November 2019 into a suspected network of people and companies accused of simulating business operations to move money and lower tax obligations.
  • The 2022 statement. In a statement dated January 20, 2022, the FGR said its organized crime unit had opened the case against Álvarez Puga, Gómez Mont, other people, and seven companies for their possible participation in organized crime and operations with resources of illicit origin.
  • The alleged scheme. Investigators alleged that the network used contracts with the federal prison administration body inside Mexico's Interior Ministry, issuing tax receipts that covered up operations that did not take place, in order to present money of suspected illicit origin as legitimate business income.
  • The other warrants. Beyond the tax fraud equivalent warrant cited in the October 2026 announcement, Gómez Mont also faces outstanding Mexican warrants in the same investigation for alleged organized crime, embezzlement, and operations with resources of illicit origin.
  • Allegations, not convictions. Every charge described here rests on warrants and an ongoing investigation, and the FGR itself describes her participation as probable, so none of it should be read as a conviction.

The Interpol twist

  • The red notices were withdrawn. In late September 2026, Interpol withdrew the red notices that had flagged Gómez Mont and Álvarez Puga for location and provisional arrest, a step Mexico's president publicly questioned at the time.
  • The warrants survived. The withdrawal of a red notice did not cancel the arrest warrants issued by Mexican courts, which remained in force in Mexico.
  • Why it matters. The sequence undercuts the idea that the withdrawal had closed the case, since the Mexican warrants remained in force and the FGR later reported the Miami securing after an information exchange with United States authorities. The FGR statement did not say the Miami action was itself an execution of a Mexican warrant.

What happens next

  • The custody question. Reports have placed her in ICE custody in Miami, but the FGR statement gave no custody location, and no official statement quoted with it names a facility or a first appearance authority, so those details remain unconfirmed in official statements.
  • The transfer question. Mexico has not said publicly whether it will now file a formal extradition request, seek deportation, or pursue another route to bring her before the Mexican court that issued the warrant.
  • The information gap. The FGR promised more information after its brief October 30 statement, so the next confirmed facts should come from the FGR or from a United States authority, not from the photographs and unverified claims that circulated online before the confirmation.
  • The case to watch. If Mexico files a formal transfer request, the fight could turn on whether a United States court or immigration authority orders her return, and whether the defense revives its argument that the prosecution is political, an argument it used in seeking the red notice withdrawal.
