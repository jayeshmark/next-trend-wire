Lula Won. Bolsonaro Left. What Happens When Losing Means Leaving?

By Editorial Team · 7 min read

Lula won Brazil's presidential runoff, and his opponent left the country. The Superior Electoral Court counted 64,256,232 votes for Luiz Inacio Lula da Silva, 51.79 percent of valid votes, against 59,826,041 for Flavio Bolsonaro. Bolsonaro refused to concede and has reportedly left Brazil for the United States instead.

What happened

Brazil's Superior Electoral Court published the final count after processing 100 percent of 499,248 polling sections. Luiz Inacio Lula da Silva won 64,256,232 votes, 51.79 percent of valid votes, against 59,826,041 votes, 48.21 percent, for Flavio Bolsonaro. Valid votes totalled 124,082,273 out of 128,236,074 ballots cast, a turnout of 80.78 percent and an abstention rate of 19.22 percent.

In the first round on October 4, Bolsonaro led Lula by about 2.2 million votes. Lula is the first candidate since Brazil restored direct elections in 1989 to trail in the first round and win the runoff. The win gives him a fourth non-consecutive presidential term, beginning January 5, 2027.

Bolsonaro has refused to concede. He says he will seek annulment over a court decision that extended voting hours. Supreme Court minister Cristiano Zanin ordered voting extended to 19:00 after complaints about transport problems and heavy rain. Both Lula and Bolsonaro had requested the extension. Bolsonaro's own Liberal Party recognised Lula's victory on Monday, despite his objections.

Supreme Federal Court Justice Alexandre de Moraes ordered Bolsonaro to surrender his passports to the Federal Police within 24 hours as part of an investigation into suspected money laundering and illegal foreign exchange transactions. Bolsonaro denies wrongdoing. His Brazilian and diplomatic passports were cancelled. The Federal Police said there is no formal record of him leaving through a regular migration point. Even so, Liberal Party lawmaker Mauricio Marcon said Bolsonaro has left Brazil for the United States to go into "exile". The US State Department said it will issue visas to Bolsonaro and his family. Bolsonaro has held Italian nationality since 2023.

His father, former President Jair Bolsonaro, did the same in 2022 after refusing to concede losing to Lula, and later received a 27-year sentence for plotting a coup. He is currently under house arrest.

Why it matters

The numbers are not close enough to argue about. Lula won by more than 4.4 million votes out of 124 million valid votes, and the count covered every polling section.

The departure matters more than the complaint. Bolsonaro is leaving while under investigation, after a court ordered him to hand over his passports and after they were cancelled. That turns a dispute about voting hours into a question about whether a losing candidate accepts the country's institutions.

The party's position matters too. The Liberal Party recognised Lula's victory, separating the institution from the man. The dispute is no longer a party challenging an election. It is one person challenging it, while his own party moves on.

The family pattern matters most. In 2022, the father lost to Lula, refused to concede, and left for the US, and later received a 27-year sentence for plotting a coup. In 2026, the son did the same. The repetition turns one man's decision into a pattern.

Second order effects

The first effect is on the investigation. It does not end because Bolsonaro left, but every step of it will now be read as political by his supporters and as necessary by his critics.

The second effect is on the Liberal Party. It recognised Lula's victory and chose to work inside the system. But its most visible figure is now abroad, calling the result into question. The party must decide whether it belongs to the man or the institution.

The third effect is on the United States. Issuing visas is routine, but the man arriving is a defeated candidate who left while under investigation and after a court order. The US has now given shelter, in the practical sense, to a second Bolsonaro, and Brazil will read it as a political act.

The fourth effect is on Lula, who begins his term with a rival in exile calling the result illegitimate. The win is clear, but acceptance of it is not complete, and that gap is now Lula's problem to manage.

Third order effects

The slower effects start with losing itself. Elections work because the loser accepts the result and tries again next time. When losing is followed by leaving, the message is that losing is a signal to exit, not an outcome to accept. If that becomes normal, future candidates will plan their exit before the count is finished.

The next effect is on Brazil's courts. Each institution did its job, and each will now be attacked for doing it. The more losing candidates treat court decisions as optional, the harder enforcement becomes for the courts.

The third effect is on the region. If a candidate can lose, leave, and keep his standing from abroad, that becomes a model others may copy. If he loses his standing instead, that also becomes a model.

The final effect is on the Bolsonaros themselves. Exile preserves a voice. It does not preserve a career. The longer the absence lasts, the more the voice fades.

What to watch next

Watch whether Bolsonaro speaks publicly from the United States. A statement repeating the annulment claim keeps the dispute alive. Silence lets it fade.

Watch the investigation. It is the reason the passports were ordered surrendered, and it will decide whether Bolsonaro can return without facing arrest. Watch for new steps in the case.

Watch the Liberal Party. It recognised the result but has not decided what the departure means for its leadership. Watch who speaks for the party, and whether anyone else repeats the annulment claim or distances themselves from it.

Watch the term beginning on January 5, 2027. Lula takes office with a clear win and an unresolved dispute behind him. Watch whether he reaches for the voters who backed Bolsonaro, and whether the institutions hold their course.
