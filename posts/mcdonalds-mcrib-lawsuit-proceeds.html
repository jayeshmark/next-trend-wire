A Judge Let the McRib Lawsuit Proceed. The Question Is Whether a Rib Shaped Patty Promises Rib Meat

By Editorial Team · 7 min read

A federal judge in Chicago ruled on October 30 that McDonald's must face a proposed class action claiming the McRib misleads customers about its meat. The judge rejected the company's bid to throw the case out, keeping alive claims that the sandwich's name and rib shaped patty promise pork rib meat that buyers do not get.

The ruling

  • The decision. On October 30, 2026, US District Judge April M. Perry in Chicago rejected McDonald's request to dismiss the proposed class action in full, allowing consumer deception, fraud and unjust enrichment claims to proceed while dismissing five other claims without prejudice, which leaves room to refile them.
  • The case. The suit, Le et al. v. McDonald's Corporation, No. 1:25-cv-15609, was filed on December 23, 2025, in the US District Court for the Northern District of Illinois by Peter Le, Charles Lynch, Dorien Baker and Darrick Wilson. The docket lists the October 30 ruling as Document #28, an Order on Motion to Dismiss for Failure to State a Claim.
  • What the ruling is not. The ruling decides nothing about whether McDonald's actually deceived anyone, certifies no class and awards no money. It holds only that the core claims are plausible enough to continue, so the case moves forward instead of ending here.

What the customers allege

  • The name and the shape. The plaintiffs argue that the McRib name, the patty's ridged rib like shape and McDonald's advertising lead buyers to expect pork rib meat, a pricier cut known for fat and flavor, and that they overpaid as a result.
  • The meat in dispute. The complaint alleges the patty is instead restructured pork made from cuts including shoulder, heart, tripe and stomach. Those are allegations, not findings, and the court has not determined what the patty contains.
  • The judge's test. Judge Perry called it premature to accept McDonald's argument that no reasonable consumer could expect rib meat from the McRib, writing that the plaintiffs' reading of the name is "not unreasonable or fanciful." She noted that McDonald's website describes the sandwich only as containing pork, and that a reasonable consumer need not research a $7 sandwich before buying it.
  • The ads matter too. The judge added that ads highlighting the meat's rib like shape could support a claim of intent to defraud, though she said further proof would be needed on that point.

What McDonald's says

  • The denial. McDonald's says the lawsuit distorts the facts and calls the claims meritless, maintaining that it does not use hearts, tripe or stomach in the McRib.
  • The ingredients on its own page. McDonald's own product page describes the sandwich as a seasoned boneless pork patty with barbecue sauce, pickles and slivered onions on a homestyle bun, without naming a cut of pork. The company says the McRib is made with 100 percent pork from farmers and suppliers in the United States and that it has always been transparent about its ingredients.
  • The timing. The ruling landed days after the McRib returned to select US restaurants on October 27 for a limited time, putting a sandwich that has sold periodically since 1981 back in the news for a reason McDonald's did not choose.

What to watch next

  • Class certification. The plaintiffs still need the court to certify the case as a class action before it can represent a larger group of McRib buyers, and their lawyer has said they will keep seeking accountability for the marketing.
  • Discovery or settlement. With the core claims alive, McDonald's must now defend them on the evidence, which raises the odds of a settlement fight or a long discovery process over how the sandwich is made and marketed.
  • The menu question. Nothing in the ruling pulls the McRib from menus or changes its recipe, so the practical question for buyers is whether the case eventually forces a label, ad or name change, or ends with McDonald's winning on the facts.
