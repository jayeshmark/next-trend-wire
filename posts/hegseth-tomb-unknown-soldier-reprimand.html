Sentinel Rebukes Hegseth's Group at Tomb of the Unknown Soldier

By Jayesh Marathe · November 12, 2026 · 7 min read

A soldier guarding the Tomb of the Unknown Soldier shouted at Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth and his group to keep silence during a Veterans Day visit to Arlington National Cemetery, after laughter was heard as the group posed for photographs at one of the nation's most solemn memorials. The Pentagon said the soldier was right to speak up.

What happened

  • The visit. Hegseth visited the tomb on Wednesday, November 11, as part of the Veterans Day ceremony at Arlington National Cemetery. President Donald Trump and Vice President JD Vance also attended the observance and laid wreaths in honor of fallen service members.
  • The photo. After the ceremony, Hegseth posed for photographs on the steps in front of the tomb with his wife, Jennifer, chief of staff Ricky Buria, deputy chief of staff Patrick Weaver and senior adviser Tami Radabaugh.
  • The laughter. As the group broke from the photo and began walking away, laughter could be heard. Video of the moment shows the group talking among themselves on the steps of the memorial.
  • The rebuke. The soldier guarding the tomb stopped his patrol, turned toward the group and shouted: "All visitors will maintain an atmosphere of silence and respect at all times." Hegseth looked back over his shoulder and continued up the steps.
  • Who laughed. The footage does not establish who among the group was laughing, and it is not clear whether Hegseth himself made any noise. The reprimand was aimed at the group as a whole, not at any one person by name.
  • The setting. The tomb is the final resting place of unidentified American service members from World War I, World War II and the Korean War. It has been guarded around the clock since 1937, and by soldiers of the Army's 3rd Infantry Regiment, known as the Old Guard, since 1948.

The Pentagon's response

  • The spokesman. Chief Pentagon spokesman Sean Parnell said the soldier acted properly. "While commemorating this solemn day, a soldier correctly and respectfully reminded everyone to maintain decorum," Parnell said in a statement.
  • The defense. Parnell added that the secretary and the department have always honored the sacrifices of American service members. "The Secretary and the entire Department have always honored, and will always honor, the sacrifices our military men and women have made to keep America safe," he said.
  • No apology. The statement did not apologize for the group's conduct and did not say who laughed. It treated the sentinel's intervention as correct rather than as an incident to explain away.
  • The guard's rules. Soldiers assigned to the tomb are trained to deliver that exact instruction whenever decorum is broken. Under the Old Guard's rules, a sentinel speaks only under exceptional circumstances, and the call for silence is a standard command, not an improvised scolding.

Why it landed

  • The day after. The incident came one day after Hegseth survived an effort to remove him in the House of Representatives. The House voted 211-204 on Tuesday to block articles of impeachment filed by Representative Thomas Massie of Kentucky, who accused Hegseth of executing unlawful orders.
  • The contrast. Hegseth is a former Army National Guard soldier who served in Iraq, and he now runs the department whose soldiers guard the tomb. Being corrected by one of those soldiers, on camera, on Veterans Day, is the kind of image that travels further than any statement.
  • The video. The exchange was captured on the live network pool feed stationed at the tomb for the ceremony and aired by CNN on Wednesday afternoon. The footage spread quickly online, drawing criticism of the group's conduct at the memorial.
  • The standard. The tomb's sentinels march 21 steps, face the tomb for 21 seconds and repeat the pattern without speaking, a ritual tied to the 21 gun salute. The silence is the point of the place, and the guard's job is to protect it.

What to watch next

  • The fallout. The Pentagon has said its piece, and Hegseth has not commented publicly on the incident. Whether the episode fades or follows him depends on how much more attention the video draws.
  • The impeachment aftermath. The House vote to block the articles came on Tuesday, the day before the tomb incident. The two episodes put Hegseth's orders and his conduct in the spotlight in the same week.
  • The ceremony record. The Veterans Day observance itself, with the president and vice president laying wreaths, is now tied in the public record to the moment a sentinel had to call for silence.
