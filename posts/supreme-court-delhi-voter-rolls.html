Supreme Court Presses Election Commission Over Delhi's Missing Voters

By Editorial Team · 7 min read

India's Supreme Court pressed the Election Commission on October 28, 2026, over about 47 lakh Delhi voters missing from the draft electoral rolls. The court questioned why voters already on the rolls must file a first-timer's form to get back on them, and told the Commission not to let the October 30 deadline stand in the way.

What happened

  • The hearing. A bench of Chief Justice Surya Kant and Justices Joymalya Bagchi and V. Mohana heard a petition by Anjali Bharadwaj and Amrita Johri on October 28, 2026, challenging aspects of the ongoing Special Intensive Revision (SIR) of Delhi's electoral rolls.
  • The scale. According to a press note from the Delhi Chief Electoral Officer's office, Delhi had 1,45,10,299 registered electors when the SIR began on June 30, 2026, only 97,53,577 submitted enumeration forms by August 17, and the remaining 47,56,722 did not return their forms and were categorised as absent, shifted, dead, or duplicate (ASDD); the draft roll published on August 31 contains 97,53,577 electors.
  • The Form 6 problem. The bench questioned why previously enrolled voters must file Form 6, which is meant for first-time voters and requires a declaration that they have never voted before, instead of filing enumeration forms or Form 8, which covers a change of address, and the Chief Justice called the arrangement a structural incongruity.
  • The court's directions. The court urged the Election Commission to reach out to deleted voters rather than wait for applications, suggested publishing a list of voters it could not contact along with their previous polling locations, asked it not to be bound by the October 30 cut-off, gave it two weeks to make changes, and set the next hearing in about two weeks.
  • The forms stay fixed. The court observed that Forms 6, 7, and 8 are prescribed forms that cannot be changed by the Election Commission, while the Commission told the bench that exclusions do not happen at the draft roll stage and agreed to extend the deadline for claims and objections.
  • Logical discrepancy left open. The court clarified it has not ruled on the legality of the automated "logical discrepancy" category, noting that the earlier Bihar SIR judgment is not a binding precedent because the Commission did not use that category in Bihar, and its use in West Bengal was allowed only as an interim measure.

Why it matters

  • A vital right. Justice Bagchi asked whether a voter who missed an enumeration form should forfeit the right to be on the roll forever, and the court described the right to vote as a vital right, framing it as a question of rights, not paperwork.
  • The burden question. The case turns on who bears the cost of an administrative miss, since the current arrangement asks the voter who was never reached to reapply as a first-timer, while the court is pushing the Commission to carry the work of finding those voters instead.
  • The petitioners' breakdown. Senior advocate Prashant Bhushan, appearing for the petitioners, told the bench that about 31.59 lakh of the affected voters were marked as permanently shifted, about 11 lakh as missing or untraceable, and about 2 lakh as deceased, and argued many never received their forms.

Second order effects

  • The Trilokpuri audit. The organisers of a door-to-door public audit in Trilokpuri, Jan Sarokar, Mazdoor Kisan Shakti Sangathan, the National Federation of Independent Women, and Voter Adhikar Manch, said the audit covered 1,236 verified voters across four polling booths and found 416 wrongly marked as absent, shifted, dead, or duplicate, and the organisers note the audit was targeted rather than a random sample, so the figure is their finding and not an established error rate.
  • Notices on the draft roll. Of the electors included in the draft roll, 33,14,032 were issued notices, including 13,79,789 unmapped against the 2002 SIR rolls and 19,34,243 flagged for logical discrepancies, according to the Chief Electoral Officer's press note.
  • Most notices resolved, many names still out. The Chief Electoral Officer's office says more than 31.26 lakh notices, or 94.34 percent of the total issued, have been disposed of, yet the gap between the 47,56,722 missing names and the applications for restoration remains wide, since figures from the Chief Electoral Officer's office put Form 6 applications between August 31 and October 26 at 2.67 lakh.

Third order effects

  • Trust in the roll. If large numbers of voters discover deletions only when they try to vote, confidence in the roll suffers even if the final list is later corrected, because the voter has already been told they do not exist on it.
  • The precedent on forms. A ruling that existing voters cannot be made to declare themselves first-timers would reshape how future revisions treat anyone the machinery fails to reach, shifting the default from re-registration toward restoration.
  • Transparency as the remedy. The court's push for published lists of uncontacted voters points toward a model where the Commission shows its working, which would make the next revision easier to audit and harder to dispute.

What to watch next

  • The next hearing. In about two weeks, the court takes the matter up again to review what the Election Commission changed.
  • The final roll. December 4 is the scheduled publication date for Delhi's final electoral roll, and the restored-names count will show whether outreach beat the Form 6 route.
  • The logical discrepancy decision. The court has yet to rule finally on the legality of the logical discrepancy category, and that detailed hearing is still to come, with Bihar and West Bengal already marked as not settling it.
  • How voters rejoin. Affected voters can submit forms online through the Election Commission's voter portal, voters.eci.gov.in, or in person through their booth level officer, and which route restores more names will be visible in the Commission's own figures before December 4.
