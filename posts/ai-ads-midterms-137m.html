The $137 million question: if voters cannot tell what is real, what is left to trust?

By Editorial Team · 7 min read

AI generated ads have crossed $137 million in the 2026 US midterms, according to reporting on Oct 27, 2026. Most spending came from Republican candidates and allied groups. Only 27 percent of these ads say they are made by AI. Through Oct 11, 220 deepfake cases were found in 34 states.

The total is not the story. It tells you AI ads are no longer an experiment, and the problem is national, not one state or one race.

This post is not about whether AI ads should be banned. It asks a simpler question. If voters cannot tell what is real, what happens to trust in the next election after this one?

What happened

Campaigns have always used new tools faster than the rules could follow. First the tool arrives, then campaigns learn it, then the public learns to spot it, then the rules catch up, usually late. AI ads are following that path, only faster.

The $137 million figure covers AI generated ads in the 2026 US midterms. Most of the spending tracked so far came from Republican candidates and allied groups. That does not mean Democrats are not using AI ads. It means the bulk of the money tracked so far is on one side, and that side is building the playbooks first.

The disclosure figure matters most. Only 27 percent of these ads mention they are made by AI. Roughly three out of four do not carry a clear label. A voter can watch one, share one, and argue about one without ever knowing a machine helped make it.

The deepfake count adds a sharper edge. Through Oct 11, 220 cases were found in 34 states, where fake images or videos of candidates were found. That is a floor, not a ceiling. It counts what was found, not what was missed.

Why it matters

Most coverage focuses on the first order effect. A fake video misleads a voter. That can happen, and it is serious. But it is not the biggest effect.

The biggest effect is quieter. It is what happens when voters start to assume everything could be fake.

You do not verify every video frame by frame. You rely on shortcuts: the source, the person who shared it, and the idea that a candidate would not say something that strange in public. AI ads break those shortcuts. If a video can be made cheaply, its existence proves nothing. If a voice can be cloned, hearing it proves nothing.

A label does not fix everything, but it gives the voter a starting point. When only 27 percent carry that label, guessing is the default.

Second order effects

First, the campaigns. If AI ads work and they are cheap, every campaign will use them, because no campaign wants to lose while its opponent uses a cheaper, faster tool. A campaign that once made ten ads can now make one hundred. Voters do not get better information. They get more information, and less confidence in any of it.

Second, the platforms and news outlets in the middle. They must decide what counts as an AI ad, what counts as a deepfake, and what counts as normal editing. A generated image used as an illustration is not the same as one presented as a real photo, but to a voter scrolling fast they can look the same. Label too little and platforms look careless. Label too much and they look biased.

Third, trust in real evidence. When fake videos are common, real videos become easier to deny. A candidate caught on camera saying something unpopular can say the video is AI generated. Real proof loses power when fake proof is everywhere.

Third order effects

What happens in the next election, if this cycle teaches the wrong lesson?

First, who runs. If a candidate knows a fake video can appear at any moment and travel faster than the correction, running becomes a different calculation. Some good candidates will decide it is not worth it. Others will run only if they have a team ready to fight fakes full time. That favors candidates with money, staff, and legal help, not a first time candidate with a small budget.

Second, how voters participate. Some will check more carefully. Many will do the opposite. They will check less, because checking feels pointless, and tune out. That damage does not look like a protest. It looks like lower turnout and a voter who stops trying to learn before voting.

Third, the rules. Some states have deepfake laws. Many face legal challenges. There is still no nationwide guideline. After this cycle, pressure to write clearer rules will grow, and so will pressure to write rules that favor one side.

What to watch next

Do not watch only the spending total. It will go up. Cheap tools get used more, not less.

Watch the disclosure rate. Does it rise above 27 percent or fall? If more ads carry a clear label, the system is taking this seriously. If the rate falls, hiding the label pays better than showing it.

Watch the deepfake count after Oct 11. Does it grow, shrink, or move to new states? Are cases taken down fast, or do they keep circulating after they are flagged?

Watch the next cycle. If the lesson is that AI ads work and labels do not matter, the next election will have more of them. If voters punish campaigns that use fakes, the incentive changes.

If voters cannot tell what is real, what happens to trust in the next election after this one? We do not know yet. Trust is not lost in one ad. It is lost in a pattern, when voters learn they cannot rely on what they see. The $137 million figure tells us the pattern has started. The next election will tell us whether we noticed it in time.
