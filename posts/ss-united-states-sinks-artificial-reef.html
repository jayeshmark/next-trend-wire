SS United States Sinks Off Florida to Become World's Largest Reef

By Jayesh Marathe · November 11, 2026 · 7 min read

The SS United States, the 990-foot ocean liner that still holds the transatlantic speed record it set in 1952, is going down off Florida today on purpose. Okaloosa County is sinking the ship, more than 100 feet longer than the Titanic, about 22 nautical miles southwest of Destin to become what the county calls the world's largest artificial reef.

The sinking

  • The moment. Deployment operations began at 1 p.m. Central time on Tuesday, November 11, with crews opening valves connected to the vessel's original seawater intake pipes and letting the ship make a controlled descent. The whole operation was expected to take about 45 minutes.
  • The change of plan. The sinking was originally scheduled for Thursday, November 12, but Okaloosa County moved it up a day because of forecast heavy winds and unsafe weather. The sendoff ceremony planned for 1:30 p.m. was canceled when the date changed.
  • The site. The ship goes down about 22 nautical miles southwest of Destin-Fort Walton Beach, Florida, and 32 nautical miles southeast of Pensacola, at coordinates the county released as 30 degrees 07.148 minutes north, 86 degrees 46.755 minutes west. It will rest about 180 feet down, with its top decks about 60 feet below the surface.
  • The method. The ship is sunk by controlled flooding, not explosives. Federal rules bar the use of explosives, so crews prepared the vessel by removing tanks connected to its seawater system, cutting large openings in its decks and taking out more than 400 portholes. Some of the vessel's 128 tanks had already been filled with water before the valves were opened.
  • The arrival. The liner was towed from Mobile, Alabama, on Monday, November 9, to the sinking site, where it sat partially flooded. A member of Escambia County's Marine Resources Advisory Committee said the ship was already starting to flood during the tow from Philadelphia to Mobile.
  • The watch. The county streamed the deployment live from its ship website, thessus.com, and crowds gathered at watch parties at AJ's Seafood and Oyster Bar in Destin and Seville Quarter in downtown Pensacola.

The ship

  • The size. At 990 feet, the SS United States is more than 100 feet longer than the RMS Titanic. She was the fastest ocean liner ever built, and the transatlantic speed record she set on her maiden voyage in 1952 still stands.
  • The speed. The ship sliced through the Atlantic at a maximum speed of 38 knots, or 44 miles per hour, and could travel 20 knots in reverse. The Titanic's maximum forward speed was just over 23 knots.
  • The service. The liner carried passengers from 1952 to 1969, then sat in retirement for more than half a century. She spent nearly 30 years docked in Philadelphia before Okaloosa County bought her in October 2024 for $10.1 million and moved her to Mobile for environmental remediation.
  • The cost. The reef project cost $13.5 million, covering the purchase, the tow, the remediation and the sinking itself.

The fight to save her

  • The lawsuit. The SS United States Preservation Foundation, a nonprofit, went to federal court in Pensacola on November 3 and sought a temporary restraining order and a preliminary injunction to stop the towing and sinking. The group alleged the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers approved the project without adequately examining environmental risks or complying with historic preservation requirements.
  • The ruling. U.S. District Judge M. Casey Rodgers denied the emergency request on November 6, clearing the way for the tow and the sinking to go ahead.
  • The long goodbye. The ship's fate was effectively sealed by a rent dispute over her Philadelphia berth, which ended with a court order that she had to leave the pier. Despite several attempts to restore her over the years, no plan ever found the money to save her as a museum or hotel.

The famous passengers

  • Four presidents. From 1952 to 1969, the ship carried Presidents Harry S. Truman and Dwight D. Eisenhower, then Senator John F. Kennedy with his wife Jacqueline, and a young Bill Clinton on his way to study at Oxford as a Rhodes Scholar.
  • A priceless cargo. In 1963, Leonardo da Vinci's Mona Lisa was transported on the SS United States to America, on loan from the Louvre in Paris for exhibitions in Washington and New York.
  • The passenger list. Other famous passengers included Marlon Brando, Coco Chanel, Sean Connery, Gary Cooper, Walter Cronkite, Salvador Dali, Walt Disney, Duke Ellington, Judy Garland, Cary Grant, Charlton Heston, Bob Hope, Marilyn Monroe, Prince Rainier and Grace Kelly, Elizabeth Taylor, John Wayne, and the Duke and Duchess of Windsor.

Why it matters

  • The reef. The wreck will sit about 12 nautical miles from the USS Oriskany, a former aircraft carrier sunk off Pensacola in 2006, and county officials say the pair will help make Northwest Florida a premier diving destination. The new reef is expected to draw visitors interested in exploring both wrecks.
  • The record. No ship has ever held a transatlantic speed record for 74 years and then been deliberately sunk in a single afternoon. The sinking closes the story of the fastest liner ever built and opens a new one as a marine habitat.
  • The scale. At 990 feet, she becomes the largest ship ever intentionally reefed, a mark the county has chased since it bought her in 2024 with the reef and diving attraction in mind.

What to watch next

  • The first divers. Recreational divers are expected to be able to visit the wreck beginning at dawn on Thursday, November 12, provided the deployment proceeds as planned. The Coast Guard and the Florida Fish and Wildlife Conservation Commission will remain at the deployment site through the evening.
  • The final position. The county's dive team will inspect the ship a few hours after deployment and confirm its exact resting coordinates, which could shift slightly from the planned site.
  • The reef's first life. Marine life will begin colonizing the wreck immediately, and the site is expected to become a working reef and a dive attraction in the months ahead.
  • The conservancy. The preservation groups that fought the sinking have not said whether they will pursue any further legal challenge now that the ship is on the seabed.
