Google Takes Over Crusoe's Wyoming Campus as $17 Billion Data Center Build Begins

By Jayesh Marathe · November 7, 2026 · 7 min read

Google has acquired the Wyoming data center site that startup Crusoe spent more than a year preparing, taking direct control of the campus one day after announcing a $17 billion investment in its first data center in the state. Crusoe confirmed the sale on November 6, saying its pre-development work on the southeast Wyoming project is complete.

What happened

  • Google bought the site from Crusoe. Crusoe announced on November 6 that Google has acquired its site in southeast Wyoming, in Laramie County near Cheyenne. Neither company disclosed financial terms, and the $17 billion figure is Google's investment to develop the campus, not the price it paid Crusoe.
  • Crusoe's job on the site is done. The sale completes Crusoe's pre-development work, which covered site selection, government approvals and power strategy. Chief executive Chase Lochmiller said the company's pre-construction work "set up the Wyoming project for construction and eventual operation."
  • The deal was in the works for months. Bloomberg first reported in June that Google was lined up to take over the site, after Crusoe had failed to sign Google as a customer for the campus it was developing. Five months later the handoff is official, and the roles have flipped: Google went from prospective tenant to owner.

The $17 billion build

  • Google's first Wyoming data center. Google announced on November 5 that it is building its first data center in Wyoming, a long-term $17 billion investment in Laramie County. The campus is being built with Tallgrass Energy, Black Hills Energy, American Electric Power (AEP) and Related Digital.
  • The scale of the power behind it. The project is contracted with AEP to feature a first-of-its-kind, commercial-scale, 702 megawatt fuel cell development with carbon capture and storage, which Google calls the largest planned development of its kind to date. The fuel cells run on natural gas and will be made in America by Bloom Energy.
  • Google pays for its own power. Google says it will pay for 100 percent of the power it uses and cover the infrastructure needs directly driven by its growth.
  • No opening date yet. Google's announcement gives no construction timetable and no date for the data center to open. The only dates in the plan belong to the power suppliers, not to the building itself.

The power plan

  • Fuel cells with carbon capture. The 702 megawatt fuel cell development will capture and permanently store a large majority of its carbon emissions underground at Tallgrass's nearby Eastern Wyoming Sequestration Hub, according to Google. The project builds on Google's first carbon capture project in the United States and brings its total contracted carbon capture capacity to nearly 1 gigawatt.
  • Air-cooled to save water. The Wyoming data center will be air-cooled to minimize strain on local water resources, Google says. Water use has become one of the sharpest points of contention around new data centers, and air cooling is Google's answer to it on this site. Whether the capture rates hold up in practice is the number to watch once the fuel cells are running.

Crusoe's wider business

  • A developer, not just a landlord. Crusoe describes itself as the AI factory company, and the Wyoming handoff shows what that means in practice: it finds sites, secures approvals and designs the power strategy, then hands the finished development to a hyperscaler. The company says it has more than 6 gigawatts of contracted capacity and over 40 gigawatts in its development pipeline. Crusoe and Tallgrass Energy initiated the campus together, and Tallgrass stays in the project as a Google partner.
  • The relationship with Google continues. Crusoe is still developing another Google data center campus in Armstrong County outside Amarillo, Texas. Google taking over the Wyoming site did not end the two companies' work together; it changed who builds what, and where.

What it means for Cheyenne

  • Hundreds of millions in property taxes. Google expects the project to generate hundreds of millions of dollars in local property taxes for Laramie County and School District 1.
  • A community that asked questions first. Google and its partners hosted a community meeting in downtown Cheyenne and spoke with more than 200 Laramie County residents, gathering feedback on economic benefits, jobs and how data center growth is affecting the city.
  • A council to manage the growth. Google, Tallgrass, Black Hills Energy, Related Digital, AEP and Bloom Energy are standing up a Community Council to develop common-sense solutions for Cheyenne through this phase of growth. Google says its community investment plans will focus on K-12 education, workforce development and pathways to accelerate Wyoming's economy.

Why this handoff matters

  • The AI buildout is changing hands. The Wyoming story is a snapshot of how AI infrastructure actually gets built: a startup does the hard early work of siting and power, then a hyperscaler with a $17 billion check takes over to build at scale. Crusoe's pipeline of more than 40 gigawatts suggests this pattern will repeat.
  • Power is the product now. The announcement spends more time on fuel cells, carbon capture and cooling than on servers or chips. In the AI era, the constraint is not computing hardware but electricity, water and community consent, and the Wyoming campus is designed around all three.
  • Wyoming becomes a test case. Google is betting $17 billion that a fuel cell and carbon capture power plan, air cooling, and a community council are enough to build the largest data center project the state has seen. If it works, the model travels to the next site. If the capture rates or the community relations falter, the next town will know about it before Google arrives.
  • What to watch. Whether Google names a construction start and opening date, whether the 702 megawatt fuel cell development delivers the carbon capture rates Google describes, and whether Crusoe's next sites follow the same path from startup development to hyperscaler ownership.
