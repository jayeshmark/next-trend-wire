Netanyahu Rules Out an Arab-Backed Government. The Deadlock Continues.

By Editorial Team · 7 min read

Netanyahu has drawn his red line, and the count has confirmed the deadlock. With the regular count essentially complete, his bloc holds 53 seats, the opposition 48, and the Arab parties 19, leaving no camp near the 61 needed to govern. Netanyahu ruled out any government relying on Arab parties and demanded a strong right-wing government instead.

What happened

  • The regular count is done. The Central Elections Committee has counted 93 percent of ordinary ballots, covering 85.6 percent of total votes, with several hundred polling stations still in routine checks and 430,000 to 480,000 double-envelope ballots still outstanding. Netanyahu's bloc holds 53 seats, Eisenkot's opposition 48, and the Arab parties 19, a historic high. With 61 needed for a majority, no camp can govern alone.
  • Likud leads the field. Likud remains the largest party with 26 seats, followed by Yashar with 21. Together and the Joint List hold 12 each, the Democrats 8, and Yisrael Beiteinu, United Torah Judaism, Shas, Otzma Yehudit, and Ra'am 7 each, while Religious Zionism-Zehut holds 6.
  • Netanyahu drew his red line. At Likud's Jerusalem headquarters on Wednesday, Netanyahu said "unequivocally, Likud is the largest party in Israel." He demanded "a strong, right-wing, national government," and, according to commentator Amit Segal's report, ruled out a rotation agreement as a condition for any new party joining his government, and closed one door: "there can be no government that relies on the support of Arab parties."
  • He claimed a different majority. Netanyahu argued the right-wing bloc holds a "clear, decisive majority among the Zionist public, among the Jewish public and among all those who support the Jewish state." He claimed the election was stolen by the Arab-led parties and called for a police and Shin Bet fraud investigation.
  • The exit polls agreed. Updated exit polls from Kan and Channel 13 put the blocs at 51 seats each, with the Arab parties at 18; Channel 12 put them at 52, 49, and 19. No exit poll gave either camp 61, and these are exit polls, not official results.
  • The opposition split. Democrats leader Yair Golan urged Eisenkot's bloc to form a change government in partnership with Israel's Arab citizens. Eisenkot took the opposite line, ruling out Arab parties joining his coalition while saying the public voted against Netanyahu. Joint List chief Yousef Jabareen said his party stands ready to help oust Netanyahu through genuine Jewish-Arab partnership.

Why it matters

  • The arithmetic leaves one majority. The opposition's 48 seats plus the Arab parties' 19 add up to 67, the only combination reaching 61 without Netanyahu. But Netanyahu ruled out relying on Arab parties, and Eisenkot ruled out Arab parties joining his coalition, while leaving open whether he would accept their outside support. So the one majority the numbers allow is one neither man has agreed to build.
  • The red line is the negotiation. By ruling out Arab support before the count finished, Netanyahu turned arithmetic into identity: who counts as a legitimate partner. Any party joining him must accept that framing, and his 53 seats still need 8 more from a pool that has already said no.
  • Turnout gives it weight. Final turnout reached 77.7 percent, the highest since 1999, so neither side can dismiss the outcome as the product of empty polling stations. A divided parliament elected by a mobilised public raises the cost of another election.

Second order effects

  • Herzog holds the next move. Once final results are published, expected November 4, President Isaac Herzog will consult party leaders and task one candidate with forming a government. No candidate can show 61 seats, so the consultations themselves become a negotiation.
  • The envelopes are a small variable. The remaining ballots, including soldiers' votes, are counted at the Knesset over 72 hours. But with Netanyahu 8 seats short and the opposition 13 short, they cannot change the deadlock. No mandate can be handed out until they are counted.
  • A caretaker keeps governing. A caretaker government keeps the same formal powers, including the authority to authorise military action, but is expected to show restraint on irreversible decisions. Israel is still managing the aftermath of the October 7, 2023 attack, so a long caretaker stretch is not a neutral pause.
  • Trump stepped in. Trump, who endorsed Netanyahu on October 18, posted a quote crediting his backing for the result, calling it a "big come-from-behind stalemate, or even a WIN." Netanyahu stressed his partnership with Trump and vowed to end the Iranian threat with him.

Third order effects

  • Another election is real. If no candidate can assemble 61 seats, Israel returns to the polls. A new election would test whether voters punish the politicians who could not form a government, or reward the ones who refused to compromise.
  • Arab leverage outlasts the count. With 19 seats, the Arab parties hold the balance of power in a parliament where neither main camp can govern without them, even though both camps refuse to say so. Every future budget and no-confidence motion will run through that fact.
  • The fraud claim poisons compromise. By claiming the election was stolen and demanding a Shin Bet investigation, Netanyahu attacked the legitimacy of the count itself, not just the outcome. That makes it harder for rivals to accept any result he disputes.
  • The opposition's identity choice. Eisenkot must decide whether Golan's partnership path or his own red line defines the opposition. Accepting Arab support would deliver a government but break a promise to his own voters; refusing it keeps the promise but leaves Netanyahu in office.

What to watch next

  • The double-envelope count. The Knesset counts the remaining ballots over 72 hours, with final results expected November 4. A seat or two moving would change momentum, not the deadlock.
  • Herzog's consultations. Watch who the president tasks with forming a government, and what number that candidate can show.
  • Whether Eisenkot bends. Golan's pressure against Eisenkot's red line is the only crack in the deadlock. If he softens, a change government becomes possible.
  • Whether the fraud claim goes anywhere. Watch whether police or the Shin Bet respond, and whether Netanyahu repeats the claim as final results land.
