North Korea Fired Cruise Missiles From Underwater. Its First Since 2024.

By Editorial Team · 7 min read

North Korea test-fired two submarine-launched strategic cruise missiles into the East Sea on October 14, 2026, its state news agency reported the next day. South Korea's military confirmed it tracked both launches. It was the North's first publicly reported underwater missile test since January 2024.

What happened

  • The test. KCNA reported on October 15 that the Missile Administration test-fired two types of submarine-launched strategic cruise missiles in the East Sea the previous day, to examine the mobilized posture of underwater combat systems and train the navy in strategic missions. KCNA said the firing showcased the reliability of the country's war deterrents, with no adverse impact on neighboring countries.
  • Two tubes, two missiles. South Korea's Joint Chiefs of Staff confirmed it tracked both missiles from before launch to the end of their flights. Spokesman Captain Jang Do-young said one came from a vertical launch system and the other from a torpedo tube, and declined further details.
  • The boat in the photographs. KCNA photographs show a surfaced submarine that South Korean officials and analysts identify as the 3,000-ton Hero Kim Kun Ok, unveiled in September 2023 as the North's first tactical nuclear attack submarine. KCNA did not name the boat, but if the identification is right, this was its first public launch.
  • The caveat that matters. KCNA is the sole source for the claim that the missiles came from a submarine. Seoul confirmed it tracked the missiles, not the boat, and tracking a missile does not require watching the submarine. Whether the Hero Kim Kun Ok itself fired them, rather than a submerged platform, remains unverified.
  • Who watched, and what was missing. Kim Jong Un was not reported as attending. KCNA said the test was overseen by Kim Jong-sik of the Central Military Commission, Missile Administration chief Jang Chang-ha, and navy underwater weapon engineers. KCNA gave no missile names, ranges, flight times, or coordinates.

Why it matters

  • A second strike, from the sea. A submarine missile is about survival. Land-based missiles can be found and struck first, but a submarine can hide, so a retaliatory strike survives an attack. That is why a sea-based deterrent sits at the center of every nuclear arsenal.
  • Cruise missiles are harder to see. Cruise missiles fly low, at around 100 meters, below much of the radar coverage built for ballistic missiles. Tracking them needs airborne or shipborne sensors nearby, so Seoul's tracking claim signals allied sensors were on station in the East Sea.
  • A silence of nearly three years, broken. This was the first publicly reported North Korean underwater missile test since January 28, 2024, when Pulhwasal-3-31 cruise missiles were fired from the 2,000-ton submarine 8.24 Yongung, with Kim Jong Un watching. The gap makes this a restart, not a routine event.
  • One boat is not a fleet. The Hero Kim Kun Ok is a single converted diesel submarine, not a class of boats on patrol. Analysts in Seoul assess that submerged-launch reliability, sustained patrols, and arming a submarine with nuclear weapons remain unproven. One launch proves a launch system works, not a patrol force.

Second order effects

  • The watch gets wider and more expensive. A mobile, low-flying threat forces South Korea, the United States, and Japan to watch more water with more sensors and ships. Ballistic defense is built around fixed sites and high arcs, while a submarine cruise threat pushes spending toward anti-submarine warfare.
  • Leverage before talks, not after. North Korean weapons tests tend to arrive ahead of diplomacy, not behind it. A demonstrated sea-based launch raises the price of any negotiation, because it tells the other side the arsenal is growing harder to find, and gives Pyongyang restraint to offer.
  • A calibrated signal, not a crisis. Kim's absence and KCNA's no-adverse-impact line point the same way. This test was staged to show capability without forcing a confrontation. A leader's presence turns a test into a statement; his absence fits a routine exercise framing.

Third order effects

  • The verification problem grows. KCNA releases photographs, not data. Without ranges, flight times, or coordinates, analysts cannot verify how far the missiles flew, how accurately, or what warhead they could carry. Every claim rests on Pyongyang's word plus Seoul's confirmation that something was tracked.
  • The bar for a sea-based deterrent keeps falling. If a converted diesel submarine can fire cruise missiles from underwater, the technology is no longer confined to great powers with nuclear fleets. Other states watching will conclude a survivable second strike is within reach of a determined mid-size military.
  • Routine tests become a force, quietly. A single test is easy to dismiss, but a pattern is not. If the boat keeps appearing in state media and more launches follow at short intervals, the program crosses from trials into doctrine, where a navy stops proving it can fire and starts planning as if it will.

What to watch next

  • A ballistic missile from the same boat. Analysts in Seoul assess a submarine-launched ballistic missile test is likely before long. Cruise missiles are the easier step. A ballistic launch from underwater would be a bigger jump and a bigger signal, because ballistic missiles travel further and faster.
  • The platform question. Watch whether Seoul or analysts confirm the Hero Kim Kun Ok itself fired the missiles, and whether the boat sails on patrol rather than sitting in port. That confirmation is what turns a test into a capability.
  • The next appearance. Watch for another appearance of the submarine in state media, and another test soon. A second launch soon would suggest routine training; a long silence would suggest the opposite.
  • The diplomatic read. Watch how Washington, Seoul, and Tokyo respond in words, not deployments. If the test is treated as routine, Pyongyang learns it can advance at low political cost. If it draws a sharp response, the next test will answer it.
