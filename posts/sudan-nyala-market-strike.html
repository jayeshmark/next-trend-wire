Drone Strike Hits Crowded Nyala Market as Death Toll Claims Diverge

By Editorial Team · 7 min read

A drone strike hit a busy market in Nyala, the capital of South Darfur, on the evening of October 26, 2026, and two counts of the dead now disagree by a wide margin. A Sudanese rights group says more than 100 people were killed. The hospital that Doctors Without Borders supports received 36 bodies. Neither figure has been independently verified.

What happened

  • The strike. Doctors Without Borders (MSF) says a Sudanese Armed Forces drone struck a busy market in the west of Nyala at around 8 p.m. local time on October 26, causing complete devastation among the people gathered there, and a second strike also hit a civilian neighbourhood, according to MSF.
  • The rights group's count. Emergency Lawyers, a Sudanese rights group tracking violence in the war, said in a statement on October 27 that more than 100 people were killed and 113 injured in the market strike, many of them women and children, and the group blamed the Sudanese military and called for an independent investigation.
  • The hospital's count. MSF says Nyala Teaching Hospital, which its teams support, has so far received the bodies of 36 people, 21 of whom were wearing civilian clothing, while its medical teams reported seeing more than 150 wounded patients in the emergency room, according to MSF.
  • The gap, stated plainly. The two counts are not the same kind of count, since one is a group's overall toll and the other is bodies received at a single hospital, and neither has been independently verified, so this post reports both as claims from their sources and not as confirmed fact.
  • The place. Nyala is the capital of South Darfur and is held by the paramilitary Rapid Support Forces (RSF), which has been at war with the Sudanese army since April 2023, according to the UN Human Rights Office.

Why it matters

  • Markets are civilian ground. MSF's project coordinator in Nyala asked why markets are being targeted, describing them as places where people need to access food and where many civilians gather, and MSF states plainly that markets, homes, and key civilian infrastructure are not targets, according to MSF.
  • The toll is now part of the story. When counts differ this widely, the number itself becomes contested ground, and the dispute over the figure can overshadow the underlying fact that a crowded market was hit in the evening, when traders and shoppers were present.
  • It fits a documented pattern. The UN Human Rights Office documented at least 306 deaths and hundreds of injuries from drone strikes in the first three weeks of October, hitting homes, markets, hospitals, displacement sites, power stations, and water sources, according to the UN Human Rights Office.
  • Nyala was hit before. MSF notes a similar incident in Nyala on October 16, when a drone struck Al Mawashi market, killing at least four people and injuring many more, which means the October 26 strike is the second market strike in the city within two weeks, according to MSF.

Second order effects

  • Hospitals absorb the war. MSF describes patients arriving with shrapnel injuries, blood on their faces, and severe burns, carried to the hospital on tuk-tuks and donkey carts, with staff working around the clock, according to MSF.
  • Food access shrinks. MSF says these incidents expose civilians to direct violence and harm people's access to food and supplies, at a time when malnutrition is a growing concern, which turns a single strike into a longer threat to everyone who depended on that market, according to MSF.
  • Infrastructure strikes spread the harm. MSF says the RSF reportedly carried out drone strikes targeting electricity infrastructure in Khartoum, Al Jazirah, Gedaref, Sennar, and White Nile states, which means the war's damage reaches far beyond the places where explosives land, according to MSF.
  • Displacement sites are being hit. The UN Human Rights Office documented numerous drone strikes on displacement sites, where people who had already fled the fighting sought shelter, and UN News reports strikes on shelters in Ghubeish and El Nuhud in West Kordofan killed at least 25 civilians between October 6 and 10, according to UN News.
  • The deadliest single strike came days earlier. UN News reports at least 139 people were killed on October 18 when a transport truck was hit in a double tap repeat strike in Dibab, West Kordofan, which shows Nyala is part of an October escalation rather than an isolated event, according to UN News.

Third order effects

  • Drones are now the main killer. The UN Human Rights Office says the increasing use of drones already accounts for the majority of conflict-related civilian deaths in Sudan this year, which changes what protection means, because there is no front line for civilians to move away from, according to the UN Human Rights Office.
  • Verification collapses. With access restricted and counts diverging, the world learns about mass casualty events through competing claims from groups on the ground, and every unverified toll makes accountability harder, since there is no agreed factual record for a court or an inquiry to start from.
  • Aid math worsens. The UN Office for the Coordination of Humanitarian Affairs says 33.7 million people require humanitarian assistance in Sudan in 2026, the highest number globally, and each strike on a market or a power station adds to needs that existing funding already does not meet, according to OCHA.

What to watch next

  • An investigation. Emergency Lawyers called for an independent investigation into the market strike, and whether any body with access to Nyala takes it up is the next test.
  • The UN's October tally. The Human Rights Office documented at least 306 drone strike deaths in the first three weeks of October, and its next update will show whether Nyala pushed the month's total further, according to the UN Human Rights Office.
