Intel's Data Center Sales Surged 78 Percent, but a Weak Forecast Hit the Stock

By Editorial Team · 7 min read

Intel reported third quarter revenue of $16.5 billion, up 21 percent from a year earlier, as sales of its data center chips surged 78 percent on demand for AI servers. But the chipmaker forecast a weaker fourth quarter than Wall Street expected, and its shares fell sharply in after-hours trading.

What happened

  • The quarter. Intel reported third quarter 2026 revenue of $16.5 billion, up 21 percent from $13.7 billion a year earlier, with gross margin of 43.0 percent, up from 38.2 percent, and operating margin of 15.3 percent, up from 5.0 percent.
  • The profit split. Net income attributable to Intel was $3.3 billion, down 18 percent from $4.1 billion a year earlier, because other income fell to $1,130 million from $3,670 million. Earnings per share were $0.60, down from $0.90. On a non-GAAP basis, which strips out one-time items, net income rose 169 percent to $2.8 billion and earnings per share rose 126 percent to $0.52.
  • Cash. The company generated $5.1 billion in cash from operations in the quarter, and ended it with $21.8 billion in cash and $32.2 billion in short-term investments.
  • The forecast. For the fourth quarter, Intel forecast revenue of $15.0 to $16.0 billion, GAAP earnings per share of $0.10 and non-GAAP earnings per share of $0.25, with non-GAAP gross margin of 38.0 percent, down from 44.1 percent in the third quarter.
  • The miss. Analysts polled by FactSet had expected fourth quarter earnings of 45 cents a share on $17.29 billion in revenue, according to market coverage, so Intel's forecast fell short on both lines.
  • The market reaction. Shares fell sharply in after-hours trading after the release.

Where the growth came from

  • Data center and AI. The Data Center and AI group posted revenue of $7.3 billion, up 78 percent from a year earlier, and operating income of $3.0 billion, up from $964 million a year earlier. Chief financial officer David Zinsner called it the company's strongest data center revenue growth in 15 years.
  • PCs slipped. The Client Computing and Physical AI Group, the PC business, posted revenue of $8.1 billion, down 5 percent, leaving data center nearly as large as PCs for the first time in years.
  • Foundry still losing money. Intel Foundry posted revenue of $6.7 billion, up 58 percent including internal sales, but still posted an operating loss of $1.9 billion, narrower than the $2.3 billion loss a year earlier.
  • Supply priority. Chief executive Lip-Bu Tan said demand for Intel's CPUs remains robust and that the immediate priority is unlocking additional supply to meet extraordinary demand for the Xeon server chip family.
  • PC slowdown to persist. Zinsner said the company sees the PC slowdown continuing at least through the first half of next year, and Intel's own risk disclosures point to industry-wide substrate and memory shortages.
  • Balance sheet moves. Intel completed a $23 billion public equity offering in the quarter to support future growth while maintaining its investment-grade rating, and announced plans to acquire Classiq, a quantum software company.
  • Process progress. Intel released its 14A 0.9 process design kit in October and announced collaborations with ASML on High-NA EUV manufacturing and with Lam Research and Applied Materials on capacity and joint research.

Why it matters

  • The AI server shift is real. Data center growing 78 percent while PCs shrink 5 percent shows where computing demand now lives, and Intel's strongest data center growth in 15 years suggests it is finally catching some of the AI infrastructure wave it spent years missing.
  • Guidance is the verdict. A quarter that beat its own guidance still sank the stock because the next quarter guides lower on both revenue and margin. For a turnaround story, the market prices the slope, not the level.
  • The foundry question stays open. Foundry revenue growing 58 percent matters less than when it turns profitable, and Intel's own forecast of a thinner fourth quarter margin shows the cost of chasing supply while PC demand cools.

What to watch next

  • Fourth quarter delivery. Whether Intel lands inside its $15.0 to $16.0 billion revenue range and how far margin actually falls toward 38 percent.
  • Xeon supply. Whether the extra supply Tan promised shows up as data center growth sustaining into the fourth quarter.
  • PC demand. Whether the PC slowdown really extends through the first half of next year, as Zinsner warned, or stabilizes sooner.
