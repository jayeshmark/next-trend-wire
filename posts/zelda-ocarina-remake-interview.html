Nintendo Reveals the Team Behind the Ocarina of Time Remake and One Change It Reversed

By Editorial Team · 7 min read

Nintendo has named the team remaking The Legend of Zelda: Ocarina of Time and revealed a modern convenience the developers tried and then removed, in the first two parts of its Ask the Developer interview published November 2. The full remake launches Thursday, November 5, as a Nintendo Switch 2 exclusive.

What Nintendo revealed

  • The director. HokWai Ng is the director, with a role to propose the core development approach for the full remake and oversee the project as a whole. His work on the Legend of Zelda series began with Tears of the Kingdom, and he played the original Nintendo 64 version in high school, calling it unlike any game the world had ever seen.
  • The core team. Aya Shida served as art director, Hiroki Taguchi as program director, and Masato Ohashi as one of the sound directors and the music lead. Series producer Eiji Aonuma and Hidemaro Fujibayashi, who directed Breath of the Wild and Tears of the Kingdom, served as producers alongside them.
  • The guiding rule. Director HokWai Ng said the team rebuilt Ocarina of Time using today's technology guided by the principle that the visuals may change, but the experience does not. Unlike open-air titles such as Breath of the Wild and Tears of the Kingdom, where players can seamlessly explore vast maps, Ocarina of Time has a special appeal that comes from its more self-contained structure, and the team chose to preserve it.
  • The change that did not survive. Producer Eiji Aonuma asked the team to add warp points that did not exist in the original game, giving players access to shortcuts, because in Breath of the Wild many warp points let players move quickly around a vast world while in the original Ocarina of Time players often crossed Hyrule Field by spamming forward roll. When the team actually tried playing with those warp points, they realized they were missing out on the moments of discovery that came from stopping along the way and wondering what might be there, so Aonuma had the team take them all back out again.

Why Nintendo remade it now

  • The anniversary prompt. This year marks the 40th anniversary of the very first Legend of Zelda game, and Aonuma wondered whether team members who have only experienced developing the more recent titles truly understand just how significant Ocarina of Time was to the series as a whole. By learning the game structure and development style behind the original and actually rebuilding it as a full remake, he said, the team may gain insights they can carry forward into future development.
  • A younger team in charge. Aonuma deliberately let the developers make whatever changes they genuinely felt were necessary and took a step back to observe how the younger members of the team would reimagine the game using insights they had arrived at on their own. Fujibayashi supported the team from a broader perspective by helping them clarify what to change and what to leave unchanged, including whether to recreate the same feel as the original or change it for a different controller layout.
  • The original's place in the series. The original Ocarina of Time, released for Nintendo 64 in 1998, was the first 3D title in the series and the first game in the series to introduce Z-targeting, a control system born from the transition to 3D that has since become a standard feature of the series. Aonuma joined the original project partway through, working mainly on dungeon and enemy design, and the game was later re-released on Nintendo 3DS in a version that largely preserved the original presentation.

How the remake looks and feels

  • A puppet-show feel. Early in development the team used phrases like a puppet-show feel and the analog feel of practical effects films as keywords, aiming to rebuild Hyrule as a rich, dense world that felt as though it could truly exist while retaining the feel of the original. Hyrule is beautiful but somewhat dark, while many of the characters who live there are colorful and quirky, and Shida said that contrast gave the original its mysterious, almost dreamlike charm.
  • Characters rebuilt one by one. The team went back to the original and looked carefully at each character, asking who they were, why they were in that particular place, and how they moved, then rebuilt them one by one, taking care to preserve each character's familiar presence even after 28 years. For Young Link's clothes, the design team asked what kind of fabric the children living in the forest might actually weave and wear, looked at materials in museums, and even experimented with making their own fabric from thread.
  • Separate maps, not one seamless world. As in the original game, the team chose not to make the entire world seamless, instead using separate maps for different areas, so that moving from one map to another provides a natural point of transition and a chance to reflect on the adventure so far. Kokiri Forest lies near the Great Deku Tree, and beyond the forest Hyrule Field opens up, and the team asked the artist who created the illustrations for the Nintendo 64 version to draw a new map for this game.
  • A denser overworld. In large areas like Hyrule Field, the team gave areas that could actually be explored a high level of detail, used slightly less visual information in inaccessible areas so they would read as distant scenery, and deliberately enlarged landmarks such as the castle and windmill. The goal was to make it clear just from looking at the screen what might be broken to reveal something and what is a place to take in the view, while preserving how it felt to explore the original map.

What to watch next

  • Parts 3 and 4. Nintendo said parts 3 and 4 of the interview arrive November 3, which should add more detail on the development choices behind the remake before launch.
  • Launch day. The remake launches Thursday, November 5, on Nintendo Switch 2, and the first test will be whether players who grew up with Breath of the Wild's freedom accept a deliberately paced adventure that refuses the shortcuts its own producer first proposed.
  • The lesson for future Zelda games. Aonuma framed the remake as training for the next generation of Zelda developers, so the longer test is whether the insights the team gained from rebuilding a 28-year-old classic show up in whatever Zelda comes next.
