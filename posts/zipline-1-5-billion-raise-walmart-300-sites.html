Zipline Raises $1.5 Billion at a $21.5 Billion Valuation as Walmart Signs for 300 More Drone Sites

By Editorial Team · 7 min read

Zipline, the drone delivery company, has raised $1.5 billion at a $21.5 billion post-money valuation and signed a new national agreement with Walmart to launch 300 additional drone delivery sites across the United States. The company announced both deals on November 5, 2026, and said the money will fund expanded American manufacturing, more hiring, and a nationwide delivery network.

The raise

  • The deal. Zipline announced a $1.5 billion raise at a $21.5 billion post-money valuation, led by Paradigm, WCM Investment Management, and Layer Global, with participation from Valor Equity Partners, Fidelity Management and Research Company, Uber, Tiger Global, J.P. Morgan, ARK Invest, and Salesforce Ventures.
  • The jump in value. The new valuation is nearly three times the $7.6 billion Zipline carried after raising $600 million in January, a rise that took about ten months and puts the company among the most valuable private logistics and robotics businesses in the United States.
  • The use of the money. Zipline said it will use the capital to expand its manufacturing facilities in the United States and ramp hiring as it builds a nationwide drone delivery network, arguing that demand for delivery has never been higher while ground delivery is constrained by traffic, ageing infrastructure, and rising costs.
  • The investor case. "Zipline is now one of the largest autonomous robotics companies in the world, operating with scale, safety, and a magical user experience for consumers and merchants," said Matt Huang, co-founder and managing partner of Paradigm. "It is no wonder that some of the largest merchants in the world are partnering with Zipline to use their delivery network."
  • The WCM view. "Zipline has built a team and culture that has been developing cutting edge drone delivery capabilities for over 10 years, and the market is now recognizing how transformational its technology is," said Alan Tu, portfolio manager at WCM Investment Management. "Within e-commerce and food delivery, Zipline represents a generational improvement in user experience and delight."
  • The Layer Global view. "Zipline has already brought the future of delivery to the communities it serves," said Anton Levy, founder and managing partner of Layer Global. "Now, the opportunity is to reach millions more. With partners like Uber and Walmart and a repeatable model for entering new markets, Zipline is well positioned to make drone delivery a mainstream part of everyday life."

The Walmart expansion

  • The agreement. Walmart and Zipline are now working together to launch 300 additional sites as part of a new national multi-year agreement with potential to grow further, a step that multiplies Walmart's drone footprint several times over.
  • The current footprint. Walmart's whole drone delivery programme covered 66 stores as recently as May, so the 300 new sites represent a far larger commitment than anything the retailer has tried with drones before.
  • The Dallas proof point. Drone delivery is already a routine convenience in Zipline's metro areas, with more than one million deliveries made within the Dallas-Fort Worth metroplex alone this year, a figure the company points to as evidence that customers treat drone delivery as a normal option rather than a novelty.
  • The speed advantage. In October, the median Zipline order was delivered 19 minutes after checkout, compared with 30 minutes or more for traditional delivery, and restaurants with quick preparation times were even faster, with the median bakery order arriving 10 minutes after checkout.
  • The Uber target. Uber, which also invested in the new round, is targeting one million deliveries a day with Zipline by 2029, a goal that would make drone delivery a major channel for the ride hailing and food delivery company.
  • The business model. Hundreds of businesses are now growing their revenue through Zipline orders, the company said, and dozens of new businesses join its network every week, while existing partners expand their relationships to include hundreds or thousands of new locations.

What the numbers say about demand

  • The restaurant cohort. A group of Zipline's longest tenured restaurants shared their results to date: between March and October, Zipline's share of their delivery revenue grew from 10.8 percent to 34.9 percent, showing how quickly drones became a large part of their delivery business.
  • The incremental orders. Zipline orders were incremental and did not reduce demand from other platforms, the company said, and the restaurants saw a 26 percent increase in delivery gross merchandise value, with 80 percent of the increase coming from Zipline orders.
  • The reach argument. Today only 5 percent of restaurant orders in the United States are for delivery, Zipline said, because those orders are in practice constrained to customers within a couple of miles of the store, as cost and travel times become less attractive over longer ranges. Drones remove that constraint without adding traffic.
  • The storefront shift. Companies like Wonder are seizing the opportunity by building new storefronts and kitchens optimised specifically for Zipline, a sign that merchants are designing their operations around drone delivery rather than treating it as an add on.

The technology and the safety record

  • The quieter flights. Zipline recently rolled out sound optimised dynamic flying, an acoustics upgrade that makes flights quieter, faster, and more energy efficient, addressing one of the main complaints neighbours raise about drone delivery.
  • The next aircraft. The company also introduced its next generation aircraft, designed to fly higher and quieter while carrying heavier orders over longer distances, which would let it serve more kinds of orders from each site.
  • The safety record. Ten years after its first flights, Zipline describes itself as the world's largest and most experienced autonomous delivery service, with 150 million commercial autonomous miles flown without a safety incident.
  • The medical work. Zipline's long range medical deliveries help save more than 14,000 lives each year across multiple continents, and in Cote d'Ivoire the company is now building a nationwide network expected to serve more than 2,200 health facilities and 12 million people.
  • The bigger picture. The company argues that using the sky for automated deliveries expands the number of customers businesses can reach while saving time, saving money, and saving lives, and the new funding is meant to turn that argument into a nationwide network.
