Missed Vitamin K Shots Doubled as Newborn Bleeding Rose 347%

By Jayesh Marathe · November 5, 2026 · 7 min read

Two studies published November 5 found that fewer newborns are getting the vitamin K shot at birth, and that bleeding in infants is rising fast. One study of 5.2 million infants found the share missing the shot more than doubled since 2017, and a companion study found cases of vitamin K deficiency bleeding rose 347 percent over the same period.

What the studies found

  • The shot is being missed more often. From 2017 through 2025, the percentage of infants not receiving the injection more than doubled, rising from 3.1 percent to 6.8 percent of newborns, according to researchers at Children's Hospital of Philadelphia.
  • The scale. The researchers analyzed deidentified electronic health records from hospitals and clinics in all 50 states and Washington, D.C., covering 5,240,780 infants born at 35 to 43 weeks' gestation between 2017 and 2025 and followed through their first six months of life. Of those infants, 234,825, or 4.5 percent, did not receive intramuscular vitamin K at birth.
  • More bleeding. Infants without documented vitamin K had more recorded bleeding events in the first six months of life than infants who received the injection, 288 versus 186 per 10,000 births.
  • A 47 percent higher risk. After adjustment for measured risk factors, the lack of documented vitamin K administration was associated with a 47 percent higher risk of any recorded bleeding event.
  • Bleeding in the brain. Babies who did not receive vitamin K had more than twice the risk of recorded intracranial bleeding, with 33 cases per 10,000 births compared with 13 per 10,000 among infants who received the injection.
  • The companion study. A second study in JAMA Pediatrics looked at more than 11 million infants and found that injectable vitamin K receipt fell from 97.7 percent in 2017 to 95.0 percent in 2025, while diagnoses of vitamin K deficiency rose 216 percent and cases of vitamin K deficiency bleeding rose 347 percent.
  • The case counts. Cases of vitamin K deficiency bleeding rose from 189 in 2017 to 903 in 2024, then jumped to 1,764 in 2025, with most of the infants affected not having received the shot.

What vitamin K deficiency bleeding is

  • Blood that cannot clot. Vitamin K deficiency bleeding, also called hemorrhagic disease of the newborn, is a bleeding disorder that develops when a baby does not have enough vitamin K to help the blood clot.
  • Where it bleeds. The bleeding can develop outside the body or internally, including in the intestines and the brain, and the late form is especially dangerous because internal bleeding can occur without any obvious early warning.
  • When it strikes. Early vitamin K deficiency bleeding develops soon after birth, while late bleeding usually shows up weeks later, often after the baby has gone home and seems perfectly well.
  • Why the shot exists. The vitamin K injection given shortly after birth is routine care meant to prevent exactly this bleeding, and it is given to newborns as a standard part of birth care.

What the studies do not prove

  • They are observational. Both studies analyzed health records rather than running a randomized trial that assigns babies to miss preventive treatment, so they report associations after adjustment, not proof that a missing shot caused any one baby's bleed.
  • The researchers urge caution. The authors caution that the findings do not independently establish that declining acceptance of the injections caused the increase in cases, and a missing administration record does not by itself explain why the injection was absent.
  • Bleeding rose in both groups. Bleeding diagnoses rose among babies who got the shot too, which is one reason the researchers describe the results as an association to act on, not a verdict on any single case.
  • The authors. The JAMA study was led by Kristan Scott of Children's Hospital of Philadelphia, and the JAMA Pediatrics study was led by Elizabeth Wolf of Virginia Commonwealth University. Both studies were published online on November 5, 2026.

Why it matters

  • A small refusal with a large consequence. The share of newborns missing the shot is still small, but it more than doubled in eight years, and the bleeding it is linked to can strike the brain and can be life threatening.
  • The guidance has not changed. The injection remains routine care at birth, given to prevent exactly this bleeding.
  • The trend behind the trend. The findings arrive as more parents decline the shot, and as new reporting has drawn attention to severe cases in which treatment came late.
  • What to watch. The question now is whether the decline in the shot keeps rising, and whether hospitals and pediatricians find a way to close the gap before more infants bleed.
