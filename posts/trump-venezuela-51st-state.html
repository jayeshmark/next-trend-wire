Trump Wants Venezuela as the 51st State. The Oil Came First.

By Editorial Team · 7 min read

What happened

  • The pitch. On Tuesday, October 27, 2026, President Donald Trump told a campaign rally in Corpus Christi, Texas, that Venezuela could become the 51st state of the United States, pointing to a tanker carrying 1 million barrels of Venezuelan crude that he called the greatest oil-related victory in history.
  • The tanker. It was Trump's fourth visit to Texas in two months. Trump pointed to a docked oil tanker nearby and said: "There's a little boat over there. That boat has 1 million barrels of crude oil from Venezuela on it. We are celebrating the greatest oil-related victory in history. To the victor go the spoils." He said the oil cost American taxpayers nothing, and earlier in the day he called the shipment a "gift" from Venezuela.
  • The idea itself. "Maybe in the not-too-distant future it'll be a prosperous 51st state of the United States," he said. "It's possible." Then he pressed it further. "Let's make Venezuela the 51st state." "Instead of a president, we'll have an election for a governor."
  • The deal behind it. In January 2026, US forces removed Venezuelan President Nicolas Maduro, who was brought to New York to face drug trafficking charges. In August 2026, the administration announced a deal giving the Pentagon a 35 percent stake in North America Blue Energy Partners, a company overseeing 65 billion barrels of proven oil reserves. The White House said in September that the first shipment under the deal would likely arrive in November. The 1 million barrels shown at the rally represent the first shipment under that deal.

Why it matters

  • The order of events. The oil came first, and the statehood idea came second. Trump opened with a ship, a number, and a victory claim, then attached a political idea to it. It is not being presented as a constitutional project. It is being presented as the next step after a win.
  • The visual. The tanker matters because it makes an abstract deal into something people can see. A ship carrying 1 million barrels, sitting in the port behind the speaker, is easy to picture. The rally turned a business arrangement into a visual, and the visual did the persuading. The words "to the victor go the spoils" did the rest.
  • The setting. This was not a policy speech. It was a campaign rally, Trump's fourth visit to Texas in two months, held for Republican candidates ahead of the November 3 midterms. At a rally, the line costs nothing. It gets a cheer, it makes the news, and nobody has to answer for the details.

Second order effects

  • Other oil suppliers. The first effect is on every other country that sells oil to the United States. If Venezuela's oil can be called a gift and celebrated as spoils, other suppliers will ask what their oil is worth now. A supplier that once negotiated on price now negotiates with a buyer who also owns part of the supply.
  • Inside Venezuela. The second effect is inside Venezuela. Caracas now has to answer two things at once: an oil deal sending its crude to the United States, and a public suggestion that the country could become a US state. Rodriguez has not commented on the shipment, and that silence cannot hold forever. Her answer will define the relationship going forward.
  • The midterm campaign. The third effect is on the midterm campaign itself. The Venezuela line is now part of the campaign's closing message. If Republicans do well, the line will be read as a winner, and it will be repeated. If they do poorly, it will be read as a rally line that stayed a rally line.
  • The label. The fourth effect is on the label itself. Trump has now proposed the 51st state label for Canada, Greenland, and Venezuela. When a label is used three times, it stops being a joke about one country and starts being a pattern.

Third order effects

  • What a state is. The slower effects start with the idea of what a state is. Trump described the change in simple terms: instead of a president, there would be an election for a governor. When the idea is introduced as a simple swap, it starts to sound simple in the public conversation, even though nothing about it is simple.
  • The language of winning. The next effect is on how the United States talks about the countries it deals with. The phrase "to the victor go the spoils" was said about oil that arrived under a White House deal. But that is the language of winning a war and taking the winnings, not the language of a deal. If that language becomes normal, future agreements will be judged the same way: did we win, and what did we take.
  • Venezuela's future. The final effect is on Venezuela's future. The country now has an interim leader, an oil deal with the United States, and an American president who has suggested it could become a state. The claim does not have to be acted on to have an effect. It only has to be remembered.

What to watch next

  • The White House. Watch whether the White House repeats the statehood line outside a rally. If it shows up in an official statement, it has moved from campaign rhetoric to policy.
  • Rodriguez. Watch what Delcy Rodriguez says next. She has not commented on the specific shipment.
  • The next shipment. Watch for the next shipment. The 1 million barrels shown at the rally were the first shipment under the deal. The pace and size of the shipments that follow will show whose schedule the deal is running on.
  • The midterms. Watch the November 3 midterms. The result will be read as a verdict on the closing message, and the Venezuela line was part of that message.
  • The label again. Watch whether the 51st state label appears again. Trump has now used it for Canada, Greenland, and Venezuela. The next use will show whether it is a flourish or a habit.
