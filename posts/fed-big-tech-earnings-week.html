The Fed Meets, Big Tech Reports, and AI Has to Show Its Receipts

By Editorial Team · 7 min read

This week, two stories about the American economy meet in the same few days. One is about the price of money. The other is about the price of the future.

On Tuesday, October 27, 2026, the Federal Reserve kicked off its two day meeting. A decision on interest rates is expected tomorrow, October 28. At the same time, more than a third of the companies in the S and P 500 are scheduled to report earnings this week, including Alphabet, Microsoft, and Amazon. Investors are watching for one question: is the enormous spending on artificial intelligence starting to turn into real business, or is it still mostly a promise?

That is the question this post is built around. If the Fed cuts rates, and Big Tech still says that AI spending is not paying back yet, what breaks first?

What happened

  • The meeting. The Federal Reserve began a two day meeting on October 27, 2026, with a decision on interest rates expected on October 28.
  • The earnings flood. Earnings season is reaching its busiest stretch, with more than a third of S and P 500 companies scheduled to report this week. That group includes Alphabet, Microsoft, and Amazon, three of the companies that have spent the most on building the infrastructure for artificial intelligence. Their reports face a specific test: whether the spending on AI is translating into stronger business performance.

Why it matters

  • Rates change every calculation. A rate decision changes the math for every other decision in the economy. When borrowing is cheaper, projects that looked too expensive start to look reasonable, and when borrowing is more expensive, projects have to prove themselves faster.
  • AI is a different kind of bet. It is a bet that the way work gets done is about to change, and that the companies that build the new tools first will own the next decade. That bet is easy to make when money is cheap, and much harder to defend when investors start asking for receipts.
  • Two assumptions, one week. This week tests the two assumptions that have carried the market for the last two years: that the Fed will keep the path open for cheaper money, and that AI spending will pay for itself.

Second order effects

  • The setup. Suppose the Fed cuts rates, and Big Tech reports that AI spending is still not paying back yet. What happens next?
  • Pressure grows, not shrinks. A rate cut makes it cheaper to keep spending, so the pressure to stop does not arrive right away. But if spending continues without clear revenue, investors ask a harder question: not whether AI works, but whether this company is the one that will make money from it, or whether it is building the roads that someone else will drive on for free.
  • Customers start to wait. A small business paying for an AI tool starts to wonder whether the price will go up, while a large company starts to wonder whether it should keep buying, wait, or build in house. Waiting slows the very revenue that Big Tech needs to show.
  • Everyone else reprices. Most of the companies reporting this week are not AI companies. They buy AI or compete with companies that use it, and if the AI leaders cannot show a clear payoff, projects justified by the idea that everyone else was doing it start to look optional.

Third order effects

  • Selling has to change. Right now, a lot of AI is sold on potential. If revenue is still lagging the spending, the pitch has to change, and the companies that survive will be the ones that can point to a specific customer, a specific task, and a specific saving. The AI market does not collapse in that world. It concentrates.
  • Builders have to adjust. Data centers, chips, and power are expensive to build and slow to change. If the biggest buyers slow their spending, the builders have to decide whether to keep building or pause and wait for demand to catch up.
  • The Fed's room gets smaller. If the Fed cuts now, and the economy does not respond the way it hopes, its next move gets harder. Central banks work on trust as much as on rates, and trust is built or lost in weeks like this one.
  • The consumer waits outside the room. The American consumer lives with the results of both decisions, without being in the room for either one. Cheaper money can mean cheaper mortgages, but only if banks pass the cut through. If the Fed cuts and AI still does not pay, the consumer gets the sense that help is coming, without the evidence that it is working.

What to watch next

  • The Fed decision, October 28. Listen for the reason, not just the result. A cut explained as a response to a cooling economy sends a different signal from a cut explained as a return to normal.
  • The AI line in the reports. Watch the Alphabet, Microsoft, and Amazon reports for the AI line, not just the headline number. The question is whether the part of the business built on AI is growing in a way that can support the spending behind it.
  • A narrower range of stories. This week will not settle the AI question, and it will not settle the Fed question. What it will do is narrow the range of stories that are still plausible. After October 28, and after the Big Tech reports, we will know whether the bet on cheaper money and the bet on AI are still pointing in the same direction, or whether they have started to pull apart.
