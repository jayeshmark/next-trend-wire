Mercor Raises $750 Million at $20 Billion Valuation to Train Enterprise AI

By Jayesh Marathe · November 10, 2026 · 7 min read

Mercor announced a $750 million Series D on November 10 that values the company at $20 billion, a round that included an employee tender and drew in Nvidia, Salesforce, and Zoom alongside the biggest venture firms in Silicon Valley. The money will accelerate Mercor's work helping enterprises measure, train, and improve AI agents on their own data and expertise.

The round

  • The size. Mercor raised $750 million in a Series D at a $20 billion valuation, a large private financing, and the round included an employee tender that let staff sell shares.
  • The investors. Participants included Benchmark, Felicis, Fundomo, funds managed by affiliates of Apollo, G Squared, General Catalyst, Glade Brook Capital, NVIDIA, Salesforce, Sapphire Ventures, StepStone Group, XTX Ventures, and Zoom.
  • The use of funds. The funding will accelerate Mercor's evaluation and post-training business, giving companies in any industry a way to measure and set standards for how reliably AI agents complete tasks and at what cost.
  • The pitch. Mercor says enterprises that own their intelligence can take a capable open model, post-train it with a fraction of the resources required at the frontier, and make it their own, and it sells the data and evaluation work that makes that possible.

What Mercor does

  • Human expertise as the raw material. Mercor connects AI developers with specialists whose knowledge is used to train, test, and improve AI models and agents, paying experts to do the professional work that models learn from.
  • A network of five million. Mercor has built a network of more than five million experts who get paid to train frontier AI models based on their unique knowledge, covering work that spans more than 800 distinct occupations in the United States alone.
  • Evals first. Mercor builds custom evaluations based on a company's actual workflows, showing how models perform against the company's requirements and where additional training or practice in realistic environments could improve performance.
  • Post-training. Once the evaluation shows the gaps, Mercor post-trains models on the company's data and expertise, so the agent learns how a task gets done and how that specific company does it according to its systems, policies, or quality bar.
  • The benchmark. The approach builds on APEX, Mercor's public benchmark series for measuring how AI performs on economically valuable professional work.

The enterprise push

  • From labs to the Fortune 500. The capabilities Mercor built for the frontier labs are now being adopted by big tech, developers of open models, application layer companies, and the Fortune 500, and the Series D is aimed squarely at that enterprise market.
  • New offices. The team has grown beyond San Francisco this year, opening offices in New York and London to sit closer to enterprise and laboratory customers and to recruit from strong talent pools.
  • A deal to build practice grounds. Mercor acquired Deeptune earlier this year, a company whose team spent two years recreating hundreds of enterprise applications where AI agents can practice professional workflows in realistic environments.
  • Both sides feed each other. Working directly with frontier labs and enterprises gives Mercor an edge, the company says, because both draw on the same expert network, evaluation methods, and post-training infrastructure, and what it learns on one side makes it better on the other.
  • AI already pays for itself inside Mercor. The company says it already spends three times as much on inference as on employee salaries, and it pays for itself by making the team better at its jobs.

Why it matters

  • The bet. Mercor argues the next decade of AI will be defined by how companies bring increasingly capable models into every part of their business, and that AI is only as useful as the human expertise behind it.
  • The scale of the claim. If Mercor's ratio holds as AI spreads through the rest of the economy, the company says, it points to something closer to double digit GDP growth over the next five years, nearly three times the rate the economy is used to.
  • The crowded field. Mercor is not alone in selling AI training data and evaluation, and the $750 million round is a bet that its expert network and its evals infrastructure are hard enough to copy that enterprises will buy rather than build.
  • What to watch. The test of the valuation is whether enterprises keep paying for evaluations and post-training once the first wave of AI agents is deployed, and whether Mercor's five million experts stay productive as the work shifts from frontier labs to ordinary companies.
