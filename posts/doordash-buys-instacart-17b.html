DoorDash Buys Instacart in $17 Billion Grocery Delivery Deal

By Editorial Team · 7 min read

DoorDash agreed to acquire Instacart on Thursday, October 29, 2026, in a deal worth about $17 billion, ending Instacart's run as an independent company and pairing the largest US food delivery app with the biggest grocery delivery platform. The companies announced a definitive agreement at $65 per share, with the deal expected to close in late 2027 or early 2028.

What happened

  • The deal. DoorDash and Instacart, formally Maplebear Inc., announced a definitive agreement under which DoorDash will acquire Instacart for $65.00 per share, an equity value of approximately $17 billion. At least 75 percent of the consideration will be cash, with Instacart stockholders able to elect cash, DoorDash stock, or a combination, subject to proration so that DoorDash stock does not exceed 25 percent of the total, according to the companies' announcement.
  • The approvals. Instacart's board of directors unanimously approved the agreement, and holders of about 19 percent of its shares have agreed to vote for the deal, according to the companies' filing. Closing still requires an Instacart shareholder vote, the Hart-Scott-Rodino antitrust waiting period, and approvals in Canada and Israel.
  • The timing. The announcement landed Thursday morning alongside third-quarter results from both companies, after DoorDash moved its own earnings report up to Thursday morning. Instacart shares hit a record high of $62 on Monday following reports that DoorDash was nearing a takeover, and the $65 price is a roughly 26 percent premium to Instacart's October 22 close of $51.68, the last trading day before reports of a possible sale emerged.
  • The financing. DoorDash plans to fund the cash portion with cash on hand and new debt, backed by a 364-day senior unsecured bridge loan commitment from JPMorgan Chase. The company said it is targeting a capital structure that maintains its investment-grade ratings.
  • What stays. DoorDash said it will keep Instacart's brand, enterprise technology products, and app, and the two companies will continue to operate as separate and independent companies until the transaction closes. Customers will keep ordering through the app they already use, at least until the deal closes.
  • The pitch. DoorDash chief executive Tony Xu said grocery is one of the hardest things to get right online, because someone has to pick a ripe avocado, find a substitute the customer would choose, and get the ice cream home before it melts. Instacart chief executive Chris Rogers said that over the last 14 years, Instacart transformed online grocery by helping retailers of all sizes compete and grow, and that joining DoorDash lets it do even more.

Why it matters

  • Grocery is the gap. DoorDash dominates restaurant delivery but has always been thinner in grocery, where Instacart built its business store by store. Buying Instacart gives DoorDash the grocery technology, retailer relationships, and fulfillment know-how it could not build fast enough on its own.
  • The competition is bigger. Amazon keeps expanding its same-day grocery service, which now totals 2,300 locations, Walmart offers 30-minute delivery in some places, and Uber recently struck a deal with Costco. Against rivals of that size, a standalone grocery app is a harder sell than a combined company with DoorDash's delivery network behind it.
  • Instacart's arc. Instacart went public in 2023 after a pandemic-era boom in grocery delivery, then struggled to hold momentum against larger competitors. The $17 billion price ends that independent run, but at a premium that rewards shareholders who held through the harder years.
  • Local stores are the argument. The companies frame the deal as a win for grocers and retailers of every size, offering them DoorDash's delivery network plus Instacart's grocery and fulfillment technology, along with advertising and AI tools, on both apps and on stores' own websites. That pitch matters because most grocery shopping still happens in stores, and thin-margin grocers cannot build delivery fleets, warehouses, and AI shopping assistants alone.

Second order effects

  • The consolidation wave. Delivery is consolidating fast, with DoorDash's purchase of Deliveroo in 2025 and Wolt, Uber's $15 billion deal for Delivery Hero earlier in 2026, and Just Eat Takeaway's $7.3 billion acquisition of Grubhub. The Instacart deal is the largest US move in that wave, and it leaves fewer independent players standing.
  • The gig worker question. Heightened competition after the pandemic order slowdown, rising cost pressures, and tough regulatory scrutiny over the treatment of gig workers helped drive this deal wave. A combined DoorDash and Instacart will face the same scrutiny at larger scale, with Dashers and Shoppers on both sides watching what changes to pay and work.
  • The advertising play. Both companies built advertising businesses on top of delivery, and grocers using Instacart's technology can sell ads on their own ordering platforms. Combining those ad tools with DoorDash's reach gives retailers a bigger reason to stay inside the combined system rather than build their own.
  • The AI tilt. The companies warn that with AI, the playing field risks tilting further away from local commerce, because the biggest retailers are building their own AI shopping assistants. A combined company selling AI tools to independent grocers is their answer to that tilt, and whether grocers buy it will decide how much of the deal's value is real.
  • The premium math. The 26 percent premium to the October 22 close looks generous, but it is only an 11 percent premium to Wednesday's close after deal reports had already lifted the stock. The gap between those two numbers is the market pricing in the deal before it was announced.

Third order effects

  • The storefront shift. If the combined company works, more grocery shopping moves onto phones, and the weekly shop becomes another app order rather than a trip. That changes what a neighborhood store is for, pushing it toward fulfillment and pickup while the app owns the customer relationship.
  • The regulator test. A deal this large in a market this concentrated will draw a hard look, and the Canada and Israel approvals plus the US antitrust waiting period are real gates, not formalities. How regulators treat a restaurant delivery leader buying the grocery delivery leader will set the template for the next deal in the sector.
  • The brand question. Keeping both brands and apps running separately sounds reassuring, but the history of big delivery mergers is that the weaker brand fades over time. Instacart customers should watch whether their app stays a first-class product or slowly becomes a DoorDash feature.
  • The worker split. Dashers deliver meals and Shoppers pick groceries, and the companies promise both groups more ways to earn. Whether the combined system actually creates more work or just shifts it between the two pools will matter more to workers than any announcement language.
  • The rival response. Amazon, Walmart, and Uber now face a combined competitor with real grocery scale, and their next moves in grocery delivery, membership, and pricing will shape what consumers actually pay. A bigger DoorDash could mean better grocery delivery, or it could mean one fewer independent choice.

What to watch next

  • The shareholder vote. Instacart stockholders must approve the deal, and while about 19 percent of shares are already committed to vote for it, the full vote is the first formal checkpoint.
  • The regulators. The Hart-Scott-Rodino waiting period and the Canada and Israel approvals will show whether this deal faces a routine review or a fight, and the timeline to late 2027 or early 2028 leaves plenty of room for either.
  • The third-quarter numbers. Both companies reported alongside the announcement, and the underlying results will show whether DoorDash is buying strength or rescuing a slowing grower.
  • The integration plan. Between now and closing, the companies must explain what actually merges: the apps, the shopper and dasher systems, the retailer tools, and the advertising products. Vague promises now will turn into specific changes later.
  • The grocery price test. The real verdict arrives in shoppers' baskets: whether the combined company delivers groceries faster and cheaper, or whether less competition quietly raises the cost of the weekly shop.
