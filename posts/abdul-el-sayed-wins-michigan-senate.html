Abdul El-Sayed Wins Michigan Senate Race, Becoming the First Muslim Elected to the US Senate

By Jayesh Marathe · November 4, 2026 · 7 min read

Democrat Abdul El-Sayed defeated Republican Mike Rogers in Michigan's United States Senate race on November 3, winning an open seat and becoming the first Muslim ever elected to the Senate. The Associated Press called the race for El-Sayed in the early hours of Wednesday, November 4, capping a hard-fought campaign in one of the country's most closely watched Senate contests.

What happened

  • The result. El-Sayed, the Democratic nominee, beat Rogers, a former congressman, for Michigan's open Senate seat, holding the seat for Democrats after two-term Senator Gary Peters chose not to seek reelection.
  • The call. The Associated Press called the race in the early hours of Wednesday, after returns showed El-Sayed with a clear lead over Rogers as counting continued into the night.
  • The historic first. El-Sayed will become the first Muslim to serve in the United States Senate.
  • Still unofficial. The count remains unofficial until Michigan's Board of State Canvassers certifies the statewide result later in November, the normal final step after election night.

What El-Sayed said

  • The declaration. El-Sayed declared victory before supporters in Detroit in the early hours of Wednesday, telling them: "Michigan, we did it!"
  • His own words. He told the crowd he was proud and honored to stand before them as Michigan's senator-elect.
  • A message to Washington. He also addressed President Donald Trump and Vice President JD Vance directly, joking that once Vance swears him in, Trump could simply call him senator.
  • The theme of the night. He framed the win as a victory for ordinary voters over big spending, telling supporters that in a battle between the money and the many, the many really can win.

Who El-Sayed is

  • A physician in public health. El-Sayed, 42, is a physician and epidemiologist who led Detroit's health department and later served as director of Wayne County's health department before resigning to run for the Senate.
  • The son of Egyptian immigrants. He is the son of Egyptian immigrants, a background he spoke about throughout a campaign built around health care costs and the daily pressures facing Michigan families.
  • A progressive platform. He campaigned on universal health care, including Medicare for All, and on lowering the cost of seeing a doctor, telling voters on the eve of the election that they deserve to see a doctor without getting a huge bill.
  • A primary winner. He won the Democratic nomination in August by defeating Representative Haley Stevens, a victory that set up one of the most expensive and divisive Senate races of the cycle.

The twist in the final days

  • A Democratic defection. Stevens, who lost the August primary to El-Sayed, endorsed Rogers on November 1, two days before the vote, after a public break between the two Democrats in the final week of the campaign.
  • A test at the ballot box. The endorsement gave Rogers a Democratic validator in the closing stretch, but it did not change the outcome, as El-Sayed still won the seat.
  • A race both parties targeted. Michigan drew national attention all year because the open seat was seen as essential to the fight for Senate control, and both parties poured resources into the contest.

Who Mike Rogers is

  • A former congressman. Rogers is a former Republican member of Congress from Michigan who ran as the Republican nominee for the open Senate seat.
  • A second straight loss. Rogers also lost Michigan's 2024 Senate race to Democrat Elissa Slotkin by fewer than 20,000 votes, making this his second straight defeat in a statewide Senate contest.
  • A Trump-backed candidate. Rogers ran with the backing of President Donald Trump, and his defeat added to a string of losses for Trump-backed Senate candidates on election night.
  • A long Republican drought. No Republican has won a United States Senate race in Michigan since 1994, a drought that continues with Rogers's defeat.

Why the result matters

  • A night of Democratic gains. El-Sayed's win came on a night when Democrats retook control of both the Senate and the House of Representatives, according to Associated Press projections, reversing Republican control of Congress.
  • A seat held, not flipped. Because both of Michigan's Senate seats were already held by Democrats, El-Sayed's victory kept the seat in Democratic hands rather than flipping it, but it still added to the party's growing Senate advantage on the night.
  • A new voice in the Senate. As the first Muslim senator, El-Sayed brings a background in public health and a progressive platform into a chamber where health care costs and the cost of living were central campaign themes.
  • A signal from a battleground. Michigan is one of the country's core battleground states, and Democrats held a seat there that Republicans fought hard to take.

What happens next

  • Taking office in January. El-Sayed will take his seat when the new Congress convenes in January, with new senators normally sworn in by the Vice President.
  • Certification first. Before that, Michigan counties and then the Board of State Canvassers will complete the canvass and certify the result later in November.
  • Watch the Senate agenda. With Democrats back in control of the chamber, El-Sayed's health care platform will be tested against the party's broader legislative priorities in the new Congress.
  • Watch the transition. Peters steps down after two six-year terms, so El-Sayed's arrival marks a generational change in Michigan's Senate representation even as the seat stays with Democrats.
