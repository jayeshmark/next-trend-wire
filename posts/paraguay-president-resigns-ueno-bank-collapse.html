Paraguay President Resigns After Ueno Bank Collapse, Then Is Impeached Minutes Later

By Editorial Team · 7 min read

Paraguay President Santiago Peña resigned on Thursday, November 5, days after the collapse of Ueno Bank froze the savings of about 3 million people. Minutes after he announced his resignation, the lower house of Congress voted 78 to 0 to impeach him, ending his presidency in the country's biggest political crisis in years.

What happened

  • The president resigned. Peña announced his resignation in a video message on Thursday, after three days of protests in the capital, Asunción, over the collapse of Ueno Bank, a major digital lender with close ties to his government.
  • Congress impeached him anyway. The Chamber of Deputies voted 78 to 0 to impeach Peña just minutes after his resignation, a stunning repudiation by lawmakers from his own long ruling Colorado Party, who joined the opposition in backing his removal.
  • The charges now go to the Senate. The impeachment vote sends the charges to the Senate, where a two thirds majority is required to convict the president and formally remove him from office.
  • A bank collapse brought him down. The crisis began when Paraguay's central bank seized Ueno Bank, which claimed more than 60 percent of the country's adults as customers, after finding its finances in far worse shape than its books had shown.
  • Protesters filled the streets. Thousands demonstrated for a third day on Thursday, demanding their savings, wages and pensions back, as the bank's app, cards and transfers stayed dark and branches kept their doors locked.

How the bank collapsed

  • The central bank moved on November 2. The Banco Central del Paraguay voted unanimously on November 2 to start a resolution procedure for Ueno Bank, and announced the move in the early hours of November 3, after adjustments to the bank's financial statements showed its solvency ratio had fallen below half the legal minimum.
  • The regulator blamed bad loans to insiders. Regulators investigating Ueno's loans to companies linked to its owners found poorly documented lending, the central bank said, as well as expenses the bank had wrongly counted as assets, and once the books were corrected the bank's capital cushion fell to less than half the legal minimum.
  • The bank grew fast and fell faster. Ueno went from an obscure finance company to a household name in less than five years, winning about 3 million customers in a country of 6.5 million people, and it held roughly $800 million to $850 million in public money, including funds from the state pension and health system and the operators of Paraguay's giant hydroelectric dams.
  • The central bank says the case is isolated. The bank's announcement of the resolution stressed that the problem concerns one institution rather than the wider banking system, and that the procedure aims to protect depositors and preserve financial stability under Law 2334/03.
  • The central bank chief also fell. Central bank President Carlos Carvallo, who had run Ueno himself before Peña appointed him to the post, resigned on November 3 at Peña's request, and Liana Caballero took over as interim head.

The political fallout

  • Peña had a direct link to the bank. Peña, a former economist at the International Monetary Fund, held shares until March 2025 in Grupo Vázquez, the group that controls Ueno, and opposition lawmakers accused him of conflicts of interest, misuse of pension funds and institutional cover-ups linked to the bank's collapse.
  • Executives were detained. Authorities detained eight senior Ueno executives, including founder Federico Miguel Vázquez and bank President Juan Manuel Gustale, and prosecutors charged 27 bank officials and business associates with offenses including breach of trust and attempted fraud.
  • Impeachment moved fast. Thirty five lawmakers filed impeachment articles on November 4, and the lower house took up the case the next day, with Peña's own party turning against him after three days of demonstrations rocked the capital.
  • The crisis hit the president's own base. The unanimous vote marked a repudiation of Peña by the Colorado Party that has ruled Paraguay for most of the past eight decades, a sign of how deeply the bank's collapse has shaken the country's politics.
  • The bank stayed silent. Ueno has made no public statement on the collapse, leaving depositors to learn what happens to their money from the central bank and the courts.

What happens to depositors

  • Deposits are guaranteed up to a limit. Paraguay's deposit guarantee fund protects savings of up to 228.3 million guaraníes, about $38,000, per depositor, and the central bank's preliminary estimate is that about 97 percent of Ueno's depositors hold balances fully covered by the guarantee.
  • Advance payments have begun. The central bank began sending payment notices on November 5, starting with advance payments of up to 10 million guaraníes, about $1,700, per depositor, and depositors can check which bank will handle their payment through the central bank's official payments page.
  • The legal deadline is November 17. Under Paraguayan law the guaranteed deposits must be paid within 15 calendar days of the intervention, a deadline the central bank can extend once, and the regulator has said it is working to shorten the wait.
  • Accounts will move to other banks. The central bank plans to transfer customers' accounts, along with whatever assets still hold value, to other banks before liquidating what is left of Ueno, and depositors without accounts elsewhere will have new savings accounts opened for them.
  • Fraud warnings are out. The central bank has warned depositors to use only official channels and to reject fraudulent recovery requests for passwords, codes or upfront payments.

What to watch next

  • The Senate trial. Whether the Senate reaches the two thirds majority needed to convict Peña and formally remove him, and how the case shapes the succession.
  • Whether depositors get paid on time. Whether the central bank meets the November 17 deadline for guaranteed deposits, and whether the advance payments reach the millions locked out of their accounts.
  • The criminal cases. How the prosecutions of the 27 charged officials and executives proceed, and whether investigators trace the missing money to its final destination.
  • Confidence in the banking system. Whether other Paraguayan banks absorb the surge of customers leaving Ueno without disruption, and whether the central bank's promise that the case is isolated holds.
  • The political aftermath. Who leads Paraguay next, and whether the crisis reshapes a political system that has been dominated by one party for generations.
