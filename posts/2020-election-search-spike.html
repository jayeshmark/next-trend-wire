Who Won the 2020 Election Trends Again Days Before the Midterms

By Editorial Team · 7 min read

Searches for who won the 2020 presidential election surged on Google Trends in the United States days before the November 3, 2026 midterm elections. The query rose into the 20,000-plus search band. The certified answer has been settled for years, which is what makes the spike itself the story.

What happened

  • The spike. Google Trends listed "who won the 2020 presidential election" among its active United States trends over the past 24 hours, with search volume in the 20,000-plus band and growth of 300 percent, according to Google Trends.
  • The certified result. The National Archives records 306 electoral votes for Joseph R. Biden Jr. and 232 for Donald J. Trump, with 270 needed to win the presidency.
  • The count. Congress counted the electoral votes in a joint session, and the Archives notes the results were not certified until 3:44 a.m. on January 7, 2021, after violent unrest in and around the Capitol delayed the proceedings, while objections filed over the Arizona and Pennsylvania votes were not sustained by either House, so all votes were counted, according to the National Archives.
  • The timing. The spike lands days before voters go to the polls in the November 3, 2026 midterm elections, when control of Congress is decided, and voters can check their registration and polling information through the federal portal Vote.gov.
  • What the data does not say. A trend measures how many people searched, not why any individual person searched, so the spike itself is the verified fact here, and any claim about what is driving it would be speculation that this post does not make.

Why it matters

  • A settled question, still asked. Six years after the election, one of the most basic factual questions about it is surging in searches, which shows the argument over 2020 has not closed in public life even though the official record has not changed.
  • Elections run on shared facts. Midterm results are administered by state and local officials, and the count depends on voters accepting the result, so when the last presidential result is still being searched as an open question, the next count enters a noisier environment.
  • Search is a signal, not a verdict. People search to check a fact, to argue a point, to follow a story, or to learn something new, and treating a spike as proof of what voters believe would misread the data in every one of those cases.
  • The record is public. The Archives tally, the state certificates held by the Archives, and the congressional count are all public documents, which means anyone can verify the 306 to 232 result without relying on a news report or a social media post, according to the National Archives.

Second order effects

  • Coverage follows searches. Newsrooms and creators watch trending lists closely, so a spike can generate a wave of explainers and reaction posts, which in turn generates more searches, keeping the question visible because it is visible rather than because anything new happened.
  • Officials get asked. Election administrators heading into November can expect questions about 2020 alongside questions about 2026 procedures, which pulls time and attention away from the election actually being run and toward one that ended years ago.
  • Platforms face the query. Search engines and social feeds must decide what surfaces for a factual question with a settled answer, and those presentation choices then become part of the argument themselves, with each side reading bias into the same results page.
  • Campaigns read the room. Candidates may treat the spike as a sign of what their audiences care about, and shape their closing messages around election trust rather than the policy issues that will actually come before the next Congress.

Third order effects

  • Trust compounds in both directions. Each cycle in which a past result is relitigated makes the next certification fight easier to start, because the script already exists and the audience already knows it, while each cleanly accepted count makes the next one slightly easier to defend.
  • Administration gets heavier. If officials must defend old counts while running new ones, the work of recruiting poll workers, securing counting rooms, and communicating results grows, and the cost of running elections rises with it.
  • A permanent question mark. If a settled result can trend indefinitely, future results may be treated as provisional in public debate even when the count is complete, and the gap between the official record and the public argument becomes a fixed feature of American elections.
  • The archive matters more. In that environment, primary documents like the Archives tally gain value, because they are the one version of events that does not change with the news cycle, and they give any voter a direct check that does not pass through a partisan filter, according to the National Archives.

What to watch next

  • Election Day itself. November 3, 2026 is the next hard date, and the count that follows will show whether the argument stays attached to 2020 or moves on to the results of 2026.
  • Whether the query fades. If searches for the 2020 winner fall after the midterms, the spike reads as election-week noise, while if they stay high, it reads as something more durable that will still be present for the next presidential cycle.
  • Official results channels. State election authorities publish the 2026 results, and the federal portal Vote.gov points voters to their state election offices, which are the check against any competing claim about the outcome.
  • The next presidential count. The next time Congress counts electoral votes for president, how 2020 is talked about now will shape how that count is received, which is why a search trend this week is worth more attention than its novelty suggests.
