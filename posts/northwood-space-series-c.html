Northwood Space Raises $375 Million to Build the Ground Network for Satellites

By Jayesh Marathe · November 2, 2026 · 7 min read

Northwood Space has closed a $375 million Series C round to scale the shared ground network that connects satellites to Earth, the Torrance, California company announced November 2. The funding will speed delivery on existing contracts, expand hiring, and build out a global network for commercial, United States government, and allied space missions.

What happened

  • The round. Northwood closed a $375 million Series C equity financing round, with Andreessen Horowitz and DFJ Growth co-leading the round. Alpine Space Ventures, Washington Harbour Partners, Stepstone Activate, In-Q-Tel, and others also took part, and a separate multi-year credit facility from HSBC Innovation Banking will support working capital and network expansion.
  • The use of the money. The funding will accelerate delivery on existing contracts, talent acquisition, and the buildout of Northwood's global ground network for commercial, United States government, and allied space missions. Northwood builds the network connecting satellites to Earth, combining modular antenna hardware, manufacturing, software, and global site operations into a shared ground network.
  • The pace since the last round. Since its Series B less than nine months ago, Northwood has deployed six multi-orbit-capable sites on two continents, demonstrated geosynchronous command capacity from a commercial phased array in live tests, and developed and launched a high-throughput antenna system. The company also opened a new 185,000-square-foot headquarters and factory in Torrance, expanding its manufacturing footprint more than fivefold, and added customers and contracts across United States government agencies, commercial new-space operators, and traditional satellite operators.
  • The founder's framing. Co-founder and chief executive Bridgit Mendler said in the announcement: "We have built hardware that performs and a network that can support the most demanding missions. This funding lets us scale that network and build the infrastructure the next generation of space missions will depend on."

Why the ground matters

  • The bottleneck. Active satellites have grown from about 3,000 in 2020 to more than 14,000 by the end of 2025, with tens of thousands more projected by 2030, while the ground infrastructure they rely on was built for a different era of space and has not kept pace. Missions need more ground capacity, in more places, with more capabilities, and without modern infrastructure the ground will limit what satellites can do, from communications to Earth observation, national security missions, and cislunar exploration.
  • The shared network pitch. Because Northwood controls the full stack, from antenna hardware to site selection and buildout to scheduling software, space operators book the capacity they need on a shared network instead of spending millions of dollars on dedicated ground stations. Standardized and modular antennas make the network grow quickly, with a new site taking three months rather than years and new antennas going from truck arrival to live links in three to four hours.
  • The hardware. Northwood designs and manufactures its own modular antennas in Torrance: Portal phased arrays for command and control and Prism high-throughput dishes for payload data. At full production the company can build hundreds of antennas per year, and each system ships by standard freight and needs only flat ground, power, and fiber, with none of the extensive civil works traditional ground stations require.
  • The software layer. Northwood's own software and proprietary techniques combine multiple antennas into larger effective apertures, and in a recent milestone the company arrayed 10 Portals to achieve the receive performance of a 20-foot dish. A modern API lets satellite operators search, reserve, update, and cancel passes on the network, paired with Mission Architects who guide each customer from first conversation through deployment, so operators get a live link without building ground infrastructure of their own.

What comes next

  • Faster links. Northwood released Prism, a parabolic dish built for multi-gigabit links, and together with Portal, Prism will take the network to global coverage, with sites capable of 100-plus gigabits per second in high-demand regions by the end of 2027.
  • A government milestone. Northwood demonstrated its multi-orbit capability in a prototype for the United States Space Force, delivering dedicated satellite command capacity as a service to geostationary orbit, including a full-duplex link to a government GEO satellite from a Northwood Portal phased-array site, building on the company's existing low Earth orbit demonstrations.
  • More people and places. Northwood has grown to more than 140 employees and plans to add 400 jobs in Torrance and Washington, DC over three years across RF engineering, manufacturing, software, and site operations. The company also said several site and network expansions will be announced in the first half of 2027.
  • The test to watch. The round answers the funding question, but the network itself is the product to judge, and the next test is whether the new sites, the faster links, and the hiring turn into booked capacity that satellite operators use instead of building their own ground stations.
