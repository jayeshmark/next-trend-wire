New Zealand Election Ends With No Winner as Labour Edges National by 3,650 Votes

By Jayesh Marathe · November 9, 2026 · 7 min read

New Zealanders voted on Saturday and still do not know who will govern them. The preliminary count puts Labour ahead of National by just 3,650 votes, yet National holds more seats, and neither side can form a government until nearly 400,000 special votes are counted and the official result lands on November 27.

The count

  • The headline. With 100 percent of voting places counted in the preliminary count, Labour won 710,330 party votes, or 29.36 percent, and National won 706,680, or 29.21 percent, according to the Electoral Commission.
  • The seats. National holds 42 seats, all of them won in electorate races, while Labour holds 36 seats, with 21 electorate seats and 15 list seats.
  • The rest. The Green Party took 321,902 votes, or 13.30 percent, for 16 seats, ACT New Zealand took 264,121, or 10.92 percent, for 13 seats, and New Zealand First took 236,630, or 9.78 percent, for 12 seats.
  • The newcomers. The Opportunity Party took 144,298 votes, or 5.96 percent, for 7 seats, and Te Pāti Māori took 13,845 votes, or 0.57 percent, but still won 5 electorate seats.
  • The total. The preliminary count covers 2,418,565 party votes across a Parliament of 131 seats, and the Electoral Commission says it is not the formal declaration of results.
  • The date. The Electoral Commission aims to publish the official results on Friday, November 27, with detailed information for the House of Representatives expected by Monday, December 14.

The arithmetic

  • The governing bloc. National plus ACT plus New Zealand First holds 67 seats, one more than the 66 needed for a majority in a 131 seat Parliament.
  • The opposition bloc. Labour plus the Green Party plus Te Pāti Māori reaches 57 seats, which leaves it nine short of a majority even with every ally on board.
  • The gap. A majority of one seat is the slimmest possible working margin for a three party coalition, and it would leave the government hostage to every disagreement between its partners.
  • The catch. None of this arithmetic is final, because the special votes still to be counted can move seats between parties and even change which bloc sits above the line.

The kingmaker

  • The newcomer. The Opportunity Party is led by Qiulae Wong, 38, a business consultant and political newcomer, and it passed the 5 percent threshold to enter Parliament for the first time.
  • The position. Opportunity holds 7 seats and is unaligned with either bloc, and Wong has said she could work with either side to form a government.
  • The leverage. Seven seats is enough to frustrate both the left and the right, because neither side can reach a majority without either Opportunity or a deal that holds all of its existing partners together.
  • The message. Voters threw more support behind minor parties on the ideological flanks than before, and the two major parties that have long dominated New Zealand politics both collapsed to about 29 percent each.

What happens next

  • The count continues. Electoral officials began counting nearly 400,000 special votes on Sunday, including ballots from New Zealanders living abroad, in a process expected to take about three weeks.
  • The negotiations wait. The parties will not begin negotiations to form a government until the final tallies are released on November 27, which confirms the exact number of seats each has won.
  • The leaders wait too. Prime Minister Christopher Luxon did not claim victory on election night, and opposition leader Chris Hipkins showed no signs of conceding, so both camps are holding their positions until the count is done.
  • The stakes. Whoever forms the next government inherits a Parliament where a single seat decides everything, and the special votes now being counted will decide whether the current coalition survives by that single seat or falls short.
