Israel Voted. Nobody Won. Now the Coalition Talks Decide Everything.

By Editorial Team · 7 min read

Israelis voted on Tuesday, October 27, 2026, for the 26th Knesset, and nobody won. The count is still partial, the official results are not final, and no bloc holds the 61 seats a majority needs. What happens next will be decided in coalition talks, not at the ballot box.

What happened

  • A record turnout. Israelis went to the polls on Tuesday, October 27, 2026, to elect the 26th Knesset, with polls closing at 22:00 Israel time. Turnout reached 77.7 percent, the highest Israel has recorded since 1999, up 7.1 percentage points from the 2022 election, while turnout among Arab Israelis reached 80 percent.
  • A partial count. The Central Elections Committee has begun releasing official results, but the figures are preliminary and partial. At its 5:49 a.m. update, with about 37.4 percent of votes counted, the current coalition holds 57 seats, the change bloc 47, and the Arab parties 16. Likud receives 28 seats, Yashar 21, the Joint List 12, Togther 11, United Torah Judaism and Yisrael Beytenu 8 each, and the Democrats, Otzma Yehudit, Shas, and the joint Religious Zionist-Zehut list 7 each. Ra'am is above the threshold with 3.81 percent of the vote, giving it 4 seats.
  • Exit polls, not results. The exit polls tell a similar story, but they are not official results. The three broadcasters, Kan 11, Channel 13, and N12, each projected the Netanyahu-led bloc at 50 seats, the opposition bloc at 52 to 53 seats, and the Arab-led lists at 17 to 18 seats. A majority requires 61 of 120 seats, so every bloc in every exit poll falls short.
  • A count still running. About 90 percent of votes should be counted by about 08:00 on October 28, with double-envelope ballots counted on October 29. The official final results are expected on November 4, and then the President consults the lists. Until then, every seat number in this story is provisional.
  • Thirty-five lists, one threshold. Thirty-five lists ran, from 38 approved by the Central Elections Committee. The threshold is 3.25 percent of valid votes, about 4 seats.

Why it matters

  • A negotiation, not a government. A hung parliament changes who has power. In Israel, the prime minister is not chosen directly by voters. The Knesset is elected, and then the President asks the leader most likely to build a coalition of at least 61 seats to form a government. When no bloc reaches 61, the election produces a negotiation, not a government.
  • Bloc totals beat party totals. The partial count gives the current coalition 57 seats, four short. The change bloc has 47, fourteen short. The Arab parties hold 16. No two of those groups reach a governing coalition without a deal that crosses a line somebody promised not to cross.
  • More than a week of waiting. Final results are not expected until November 4, and the President's consultations come after that, so Israel will spend more than a week knowing roughly what happened without having a government.

Second order effects

  • Small parties become kingmakers. A bloc is a collection of parties that agree on one thing, usually who should not be prime minister, and disagree on almost everything else. When a bloc falls short of 61, its leader has to keep it together while negotiating with outsiders, and every party inside it gains leverage.
  • The Arab parties hold the balance. The Arab parties hold 16 seats in the partial count, more than the gap between either large bloc and a majority. Arab parties have often been treated as votes that count for the total but not for the coalition. That is harder to sustain now. Any government that reaches 61 without them has to pull together parties that disagree sharply, and any government that reaches 61 with them crosses a line Israeli politics has treated as a boundary for years.
  • A caretaker clock. The count will take days, and the negotiations longer, while the outgoing government stays as a caretaker that can keep the lights on but cannot make big decisions or pass a budget.

Third order effects

  • Trust is the slow casualty. A record turnout is a vote of confidence in voting. A hung parliament is not a failure of voting, but it can feel like one when people voted in record numbers expecting a decision. If the talks produce a government that looks like a deal between parties voters did not expect to see together, many will conclude the election decided less than they thought, and that shows up next time as lower turnout or votes for parties that promise to break the system.
  • The threshold reshapes the map. Every list that missed the 3.25 percent line spent months campaigning for nothing, which pushes small parties to merge or disappear. The parties that barely cleared it, like Ra'am at 3.81 percent, now hold seats worth far more than their vote share.
  • The real questions stay open. Voters do not vote on arithmetic. They vote on security, on the cost of living, on the war, on who should lead. A hung parliament settles none of those questions. It hands them to negotiators, who will trade them against each other to reach 61 seats.

What to watch next

  • The count. About 90 percent should be counted by about 08:00 on October 28, and double-envelope ballots on October 29. Watch whether the current coalition stays at 57 and the Arab parties at 16.
  • The threshold. Any list near 3.25 percent can still cross the line or fall below it, and a list that drops below it loses all its seats.
  • November 4. Watch the official final results on November 4, then the President's consultations, when the President asks who should get the first chance to form a government.
  • What leaders stop ruling out. Watch what the party leaders say. The first statements are usually about who a party will not sit with. The real signal is who a party stops ruling out.
