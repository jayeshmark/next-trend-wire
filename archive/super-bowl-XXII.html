Super Bowl XXII: Washington vs Denver Broncos

By Editorial Team

Result in brief: Washington won its second Super Bowl title of the decade, scoring 35 points in the second quarter, a Super Bowl record for one quarter, behind Doug Williams, the first Black quarterback to start and win a Super Bowl. Note on names: this page refers to the team as Washington throughout, following the current team name. Contemporary sources from 1988 use the team's former name.

The game story

Denver arrived in San Diego as the favorite, and the first quarter gave every reason to believe the oddsmakers. The Broncos, back in the Super Bowl for a second straight year after losing to the New York Giants, were led by John Elway, the league's reigning Most Valuable Player, and had finished 10 and 4 and 1 in a season shortened by a players' strike. Washington had finished 11 and 4, also in the strike shortened season, and was led by a quarterback few had expected to see on this stage. Doug Williams had begun the season as Washington's backup and had been promoted to starter midway through the season, and had become the first Black quarterback ever to start a Super Bowl. Denver was favored by three points. Washington's starting running back was another surprise: rookie Timmy Smith, who had gained only 126 yards in the entire regular season, was told shortly before kickoff that he would start.

Denver's start could hardly have been better. On the Broncos' first play from scrimmage, Elway threw a 56 yard touchdown pass to rookie Ricky Nattiel, the fastest touchdown in Super Bowl history to that point in terms of game time, and Rich Karlis added a 24 yard field goal for a 10 to 0 lead. Worse for Washington, late in the first quarter Williams hurt his left knee while setting up to pass, limped off the field, and was replaced for two plays by Schroeder. When Williams returned at the start of the second quarter, Washington trailed 10 to 0, had gained almost nothing on offense, and looked beaten. What happened over the next fifteen minutes of football is the greatest single quarter any team has ever played in a Super Bowl, and it remains unmatched.

Williams threw an 80 yard touchdown pass to Ricky Sanders on Washington's first possession of the quarter, cutting the lead to 10 to 7. On the next possession, he threw a 27 yard touchdown pass to Gary Clark, giving Washington a 14 to 10 lead. Then Smith broke free for a 58 yard touchdown run, making it 21 to 10. Then Williams found Sanders again, this time for 50 yards, making it 28 to 10. Then, with just over a minute left in the half, Williams threw an 8 yard touchdown pass to tight end Clint Didier, making it 35 to 10. Five touchdowns, five consecutive possessions, eighteen plays, 356 yards, and less than six minutes of possession time. Williams completed 9 of 11 passes in the quarter for 228 yards and four touchdowns, a Super Bowl record for touchdown passes in one quarter. Smith gained 122 rushing yards on five carries in the quarter. Sanders gained 168 receiving yards on five catches in the quarter. Denver's defense, which had shut Washington down for a full quarter, had no answer for any of it, and the Broncos went to halftime trailing by twenty five points in a game they had led by ten after fifteen minutes.

The second half was, by comparison, almost an afterthought. Washington, with a huge lead, kept mostly to the ground, and the only score of the second half came in the fourth quarter, when Smith ran 4 yards for his second touchdown, making the final score 42 to 10. Smith finished with 204 rushing yards on 22 carries, breaking Marcus Allen's Super Bowl record, and Sanders finished with 193 receiving yards on nine catches, breaking Lynn Swann's Super Bowl record. Williams finished 18 of 29 for 340 yards and four touchdowns, breaking Joe Montana's Super Bowl passing yardage record, and was named MVP. He became the first Black quarterback to win a Super Bowl, a milestone that carried weight far beyond the game, and he did it after leaving the game with an injured knee in the first quarter. Denver, meanwhile, had lost its second straight Super Bowl by a combined score of 81 to 30, and Elway had fallen to 0 and 2 on the sport's largest stage.

The legacy of this game is the second quarter itself. No team before or since has scored 35 points in one quarter of a Super Bowl, and no quarterback has thrown four touchdown passes in one quarter of a Super Bowl. The game also marked a social milestone: Williams' performance answered, on the field and in front of more than 80 million television viewers, the long standing doubts about whether a Black quarterback could lead a team to a championship, doubts Williams himself had faced since his college days at Grambling. For Washington, it was a second Super Bowl title of the decade, and the franchise's last championship for more than three decades. For Denver, it was the middle chapter of a trilogy of defeats that would not end until Elway's championships at the end of his career. The game was played in San Diego for the first time, and its halftime show, with 88 grand pianos on the field, was nearly as outsized as the second quarter that decided the game.

Why this game mattered

This game mattered most for what Doug Williams represented and what he proved. Williams had been a star at Grambling under Eddie Robinson, had been drafted by Tampa Bay, had been doubted and discarded, and had spent years out of the starting lineup before Washington gave him a second chance. His second quarter, played on an injured knee, in front of the largest television audience of his life, became a landmark moment for Black quarterbacks in professional football, opening a door that had been closed by decades of prejudice about who could lead a team. The game was also a product of its strange season: the 1987 players' strike had cancelled one week of games and filled three weeks with replacement players, yet the championship game that emerged from that chaos produced the most explosive quarter in Super Bowl history. San Diego's first Super Bowl also showed the event's expanding geography, moving beyond its traditional homes in Miami, New Orleans, Pasadena, and the Detroit area to a new host city on the West Coast.
