Super Bowl LIII: New England Patriots vs Los Angeles Rams

By Editorial Team

New England won its sixth Super Bowl title, tying the Pittsburgh Steelers for the most in league history at the time, by beating Los Angeles 13-3 in the lowest scoring Super Bowl ever played. It was the first Super Bowl in which neither team scored a touchdown in the first three quarters.

The game story

The stakes were a rematch of generations. New England arrived at 11-5, in its third straight Super Bowl and its ninth with Tom Brady and Bill Belichick together, seeking a sixth title. Los Angeles arrived at 13-3, with the league's youngest head coach in Sean McVay and a high scoring offense built around Jared Goff and Todd Gurley, seeking the franchise's second title and its first since the St. Louis days. The Rams had reached the game through a controversial NFC title win over New Orleans, a game in which the officials missed an obvious pass interference call late, and the league later acknowledged the error. New England had reached it through an overtime win at Kansas City. The matchup promised offense. It delivered the opposite.

The first half was a stalemate. New England's opening drive reached the Los Angeles 32 yard line but ended with Brady's pass intercepted by Cory Littleton. Los Angeles punted on its first series, and the pattern held: punts, field position, and missed chances. New England finally scored with 10:33 left in the second quarter, on a 42 yard Stephen Gostkowski field goal, and that 3-0 lead held to halftime. It was a low scoring first half, and the Rams' offense, which had been prolific in the regular season, had nothing to show for its first two quarters.

The third quarter brought the Rams' only points. Greg Zuerlein kicked a 53 yard field goal with 2:15 left in the quarter, tying the game at 3-3. That tie lasted into the fourth quarter, and the tension in the stadium came from the strangeness of it: a Super Bowl, deep into the fourth quarter, with no touchdown by either team and a 3-3 score. New England finally broke the game open with the drive that decided it. Brady found Gronkowski for 18 yards, then again for 29 yards to the Los Angeles 2 yard line, the longest play of the game to that point. Two plays later, Sony Michel ran 2 yards for a touchdown, and Gostkowski's kick made it 10-3 with 7:03 left.

Los Angeles had one last chance. Goff drove the Rams to the New England 27 yard line, but on second down, Stephon Gilmore stepped in front of a pass intended for Brandin Cooks and intercepted it, ending the threat. New England ran down the clock, added a 41 yard Gostkowski field goal with 1:16 left, and won 13-3. Brady finished without a touchdown pass, Goff finished without one either, and the combined 16 points set a new low for Super Bowl scoring, below the 21 points of Super Bowl VII.

The legacy of the game is defined by its defense and its place in the Brady and Belichick story. Belichick's game plan, which confused Goff with shifting coverages and took away the Rams' play action passing game, is now studied as one of the great defensive performances in a Super Bowl. Brady's sixth title, at age 41, made him the most decorated player in the game's history. Edelman, with 10 catches for 141 yards, was named MVP, a remarkable honor for a player who had missed the previous season with a knee injury. For Los Angeles, the loss was a harsh lesson for a young team: the Rams scored 3 points after one of the best offensive seasons in franchise history, and McVay later admitted he had been outcoached. The Rams would return to the Super Bowl three years later and win it, but this night in Atlanta belonged to New England's defense.

Why this game mattered

Super Bowl LIII is remembered as much for its reception as for its result. The game drew the smallest Super Bowl television audience in a decade, and the halftime show, headlined by Maroon 5 with Travis Scott and Big Boi, was widely rated as one of the weakest of the era, arriving after several prominent artists reportedly declined the slot amid the league's handling of player protests. The show's most talked about moment was not a song but a brief SpongeBob SquarePants clip, a nod to a fan campaign honoring the show's late creator, which lasted only seconds and disappointed viewers who had hoped for a full tribute performance.

On the field, the game became a reference point for defensive coaching. Belichick's plan against McVay's offense, using a 6-1 defensive front look and disguised coverages to confuse Goff, was broken down by coaches and analysts for months afterward, and it forced the league's young offensive coaches to rethink how they handled pre snap disguise. The game also closed a chapter: it was the last Super Bowl of the Brady and Belichick era in New England, with Brady leaving for Tampa Bay the following season, and it gave the Patriots a sixth title that tied the Steelers' record at the time.
