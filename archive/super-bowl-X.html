The game story

Super Bowl X was played in America's Bicentennial year, both teams wore Bicentennial patches, and the pregame and halftime shows celebrated 200 years of American history. The football, for three quarters, was far less celebratory. Dallas scored first when Roger Staubach threw a 29 yard touchdown pass to Drew Pearson, and Pittsburgh answered when Terry Bradshaw threw a 7 yard touchdown pass to Randy Grossman, a drive set up by a spectacular 32 yard catch from Lynn Swann, who leaped over a Dallas defender and kept both feet in bounds. A Toni Fritsch field goal gave Dallas a 10 to 7 halftime lead, and the third quarter produced no points at all. For a game that would become famous, it spent a long time looking ordinary.

The fourth quarter changed everything, and it started with a confrontation. Pittsburgh kicker Roy Gerela missed his second field goal of the game, and Dallas safety Cliff Harris mockingly patted him on the helmet. Pittsburgh linebacker Jack Lambert threw Harris to the ground, and the Steelers, who had been flat, suddenly played with an edge they had lacked all day. What followed was one of the great fourth quarters in Super Bowl history. First, Reggie Harrison blocked a Dallas punt through the end zone for a safety, making it 10 to 9. Then Gerela, the same kicker Harris had mocked, kicked field goals of 36 and 18 yards to give Pittsburgh a 15 to 10 lead. Then Bradshaw threw a 64 yard touchdown pass to Swann, and Pittsburgh led 21 to 10. Swann's catch, his fourth of the day, capped a performance of 161 yards on 4 catches, a Super Bowl record for receiving yards at the time, and made him the game's MVP. It was remarkable in part because Swann had suffered a severe concussion in the AFC Championship Game and had been doubtful to play at all.

Dallas did not quit. Staubach threw a 34 yard touchdown pass to Percy Howard to cut the lead to 21 to 17, and Dallas got the ball back with a chance to win it in the final minute. Staubach drove the Cowboys to the Pittsburgh end zone, but his final passes fell short: one bounced off Howard's helmet, and the last, a Hail Mary with three seconds left, was tipped by Mike Wagner into the arms of Glen Edwards for an interception as time expired. Pittsburgh won 21 to 17 and became the third team to win back to back Super Bowls, after Green Bay and Miami.

The game is remembered for Swann's catches, and rightly so, because they were the kind of plays that changed how people thought about what a receiver could do in a championship game. But it is also remembered for its temper: the Harris pat, Lambert's response, and a fourth quarter in which Pittsburgh scored 14 points after being shut out in the third. Bradshaw finished with 209 passing yards and 2 touchdowns without an interception, while Staubach threw for 204 yards and 2 touchdowns but was intercepted 3 times and sacked 7 times. Dallas had been the first wild card team to reach a Super Bowl, after beating the Los Angeles Rams 37 to 7 in the NFC Championship Game, and the loss left the Cowboys still searching for a second title. Pittsburgh, meanwhile, had confirmed that Super Bowl IX was no fluke. The Steelers were not just champions. They were a dynasty in the making, and this game, more than the defensive struggle of the year before, showed they could win a shootout finish as well as a defensive struggle.

Scoring timeline

  1. Dallas strikes first. Staubach's 29 yard touchdown pass to Drew Pearson makes it 7 to 0.
  2. Pittsburgh answers. Bradshaw's touchdown pass to Grossman, set up by Swann's leaping catch, ties it at 7.
  3. Dallas leads at halftime. Fritsch's field goal makes it 10 to 7, and the third quarter is scoreless.
  4. The Harris pat lights the fuse. After Gerela's missed field goal, Harris pats him on the helmet, Lambert throws Harris down, and Pittsburgh finds its edge.
  5. The blocked punt safety. Harrison's block cuts the lead to 10 to 9 and starts Pittsburgh's fourth quarter run.
  6. Gerela's redemption. Two field goals give Pittsburgh a 15 to 10 lead.
  7. Swann's 64 yard touchdown. Bradshaw's deep pass to Swann makes it 21 to 10 and seals Swann's MVP award.
  8. Dallas's last chance dies in the end zone. Staubach's final Hail Mary is tipped by Wagner and intercepted by Edwards as time expires.

Why this game mattered

Super Bowl X was one of the first major events of America's Bicentennial year, and the whole broadcast leaned into that theme, from the patches on the uniforms to the halftime tribute. It also marked a turning point in how Super Bowls were remembered: not just as championships, but as highlight reels. Swann's catches became some of the most replayed plays in NFL Films history, and they helped make the wide receiver a glamour position in a league that had been dominated by running backs and quarterbacks. The game also confirmed Pittsburgh as the team of the decade's second half. The Steelers had now won two in a row, and they would win two more before the decade ended. For Dallas, the loss was the second Super Bowl defeat in franchise history, and it set up the rematch culture that would define the Steelers and Cowboys rivalry for the rest of the decade.
