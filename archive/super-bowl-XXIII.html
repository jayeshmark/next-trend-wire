Super Bowl XXIII: San Francisco 49ers vs Cincinnati Bengals

By Editorial Team

Result in brief: San Francisco won its third Super Bowl title, in Bill Walsh's final game as head coach, on a 92 yard drive in the final minutes. Jerry Rice was named MVP after catching 11 passes for 215 yards.

The game story

This was a rematch seven years in the making, and for most of the afternoon it looked like Cincinnati's revenge. San Francisco had beaten Cincinnati 26 to 21 in Super Bowl XVI, and the Bengals had waited since then for another chance. Cincinnati arrived with the better regular season record, 12 and 4 against San Francisco's 10 and 6, and with a team that had survived a difficult season to reach Miami. San Francisco arrived as the defending NFC power, but a vulnerable one: the 49ers had lost six regular season games, tied at the time for the most ever by a Super Bowl champion, and head coach Bill Walsh had privately decided this would be his final game. The Bengals were led by Boomer Esiason, the league's Most Valuable Player, and by running back Ickey Woods, whose touchdown dance, the Ickey Shuffle, had become a national fad. San Francisco was favored by seven points, but many observers believed this was Cincinnati's year. The first half did nothing to settle the argument, because almost nothing happened.

The game began with injuries that changed both teams. Cincinnati nose tackle Tim Krumrie broke his leg while trying to tackle Roger Craig early in the game, a gruesome injury that silenced the stadium and removed the Bengals' defensive anchor. San Francisco lost its starting offensive tackle to a broken ankle in the same stretch. From there, the first half became a field goal contest. Mike Cofer kicked a 41 yard field goal for San Francisco in the first quarter, Jim Breech answered with a 34 yard field goal for Cincinnati in the second quarter, and the teams went to halftime tied 3 to 3, the first tie at halftime in Super Bowl history. The third quarter followed the same pattern: Breech kicked a 43 yard field goal to make it 6 to 3, Cofer answered with a 32 yard field goal to tie it at 6 to 6, and the game seemed headed for a finish decided by kickers. Then, with seconds left in the third quarter, the game exploded. Stanford Jennings took Cofer's kickoff 93 yards for a touchdown, giving Cincinnati a 13 to 6 lead and the stadium's loudest moment of the night.

San Francisco answered immediately, and the answer came from its best player. Montana led an 85 yard drive in four plays, finishing with a 14 yard touchdown pass to Jerry Rice, tying the game at 13 to 13 early in the fourth quarter. Cincinnati answered with a drive of its own, capped by a 40 yard Breech field goal with 3 minutes and 20 seconds left, taking a 16 to 13 lead. San Francisco returned the ensuing kickoff to its own 15 yard line, but an illegal block penalty pushed the ball back to the 8 yard line, leaving Montana 92 yards from the end zone with 3 minutes and 10 seconds left and only one timeout. In the huddle before the drive, Montana, famously calm, pointed into the stands and told his teammates to look at actor John Candy in the crowd. The tactic worked, loosening a huddle that had every reason to be tight, and Montana went to work. He completed passes to Craig, to tight end John Frank, and to Rice, moving the ball steadily downfield. A penalty for an illegal man downfield pushed San Francisco back to second down and 20 with 1 minute and 15 seconds left. Montana answered with a 27 yard pass to Rice, then an 8 yard pass to Craig, reaching the Cincinnati 10 yard line. With 39 seconds left, Montana threw a 10 yard touchdown pass to John Taylor on a post pattern, giving San Francisco a 20 to 16 lead with 34 seconds remaining. Cincinnati's last drive ended when Esiason's pass to Collinsworth was broken up as time expired.

Rice was named MVP after catching 11 passes for 215 yards and a touchdown, a Super Bowl record for receiving yards at the time, and the first time a 49er other than Montana had won the award. Montana's line, 23 of 36 for 357 yards and two touchdowns, was itself a Super Bowl record for passing yards at the time, and the final drive, 11 plays and 92 yards in under three minutes, became known simply as The Drive in 49ers history. For Walsh, it was a perfect ending: his third Super Bowl victory, in his final game as San Francisco's head coach, after a season in which his team had struggled and nearly missed the playoffs. For Cincinnati, it was a second Super Bowl loss to the same opponent, and the franchise would wait more than three decades before returning to a Super Bowl. The Bengals led in the fourth quarter, led with 3 minutes and 20 seconds left, and lost on a drive that began at their opponent's 8 yard line. That is why this game is still ranked among the greatest Super Bowls ever played, not for its scoring, which was modest, but for its finish, which was perfect.

Scoring timeline

  1. San Francisco strikes first. Cofer's 41 yard field goal gives San Francisco a 3 to 0 lead.
  2. Cincinnati ties it. Breech's 34 yard field goal makes it 3 to 3 at halftime, the first halftime tie in Super Bowl history.
  3. Cincinnati takes the lead. Breech's 43 yard field goal makes it 6 to 3 in the third quarter.
  4. San Francisco ties it again. Cofer's 32 yard field goal makes it 6 to 6.
  5. Jennings breaks the tie. His 93 yard kickoff return gives Cincinnati a 13 to 6 lead late in the third quarter.
  6. Rice ties it again. His 14 yard touchdown catch early in the fourth quarter makes it 13 to 13.
  7. Cincinnati retakes the lead. Breech's 40 yard field goal makes it 16 to 13 with 3 minutes and 20 seconds left.
  8. Montana drives 92 yards. Taylor's 10 yard touchdown catch with 34 seconds left gives San Francisco a 20 to 16 win.

Why this game mattered

This game arrived at a hinge point for the Super Bowl as a television event. The halftime show, with its 3-D effects, its Elvis impersonating magician, and its audience participation card trick, was an attempt to make halftime itself must see television, after years in which halftime had been treated as an intermission for marching bands. It was widely mocked at the time, and it is still ranked among the worst halftime shows ever, but it showed the league searching for a bigger halftime idea, a search that would end four years later with Michael Jackson. The game also closed the Bill Walsh era in San Francisco, one of the most influential coaching tenures in league history, and it began the longest championship drought in Cincinnati sports history. For the wider culture, the Ickey Shuffle, the John Candy anecdote, and the 92 yard drive all entered the sport's folklore, proving that a low scoring game could still become a classic if its final minutes were perfect.
