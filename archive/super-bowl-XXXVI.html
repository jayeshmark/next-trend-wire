The game story

The gap between the two teams looked enormous before kickoff. St. Louis arrived as the defending champion from two seasons earlier, the owner of the offense known as the Greatest Show on Turf, and a 14 point favorite. New England arrived as a team that had finished 5-11 the previous season, a team starting a second year quarterback who had begun the season on the bench. On paper, the Rams were supposed to win with speed and points. On the field, the Patriots won with patience, physical defense, and a refusal to be rushed into mistakes.

That contrast shaped the opening. St. Louis moved first, as expected, and Jeff Wilkins kicked a 50 yard field goal for a 3-0 lead. New England did not answer with a spectacular drive. It answered by making the Rams work for every yard and by waiting for a mistake. The mistake came in the second quarter, when Kurt Warner threw toward Isaac Bruce and Ty Law stepped in front of the pass, then ran 47 yards for a touchdown. The Patriots were no longer trying to survive the Rams offense. They were ahead of it, and they could play the kind of close, careful game that suited them best.

The second New England touchdown, just before halftime, showed how quickly a disciplined team can turn a single takeaway into points. Antwan Harris forced Ricky Proehl to fumble, Terrell Buckley recovered, and five plays later Tom Brady found David Patten in the corner of the end zone for an 8 yard touchdown. The score was 14-3 at halftime, and for the first time all season, according to contemporary accounts of the game, St. Louis trailed by more than eight points. The Rams had more talent on offense, but they were now chasing a game against a defense that had already taken the ball away twice and given nothing away in return.

New England extended the lead in the third quarter after another interception. Torry Holt slipped coming off the line, Otis Smith intercepted Warner and returned the ball 30 yards, and Adam Vinatieri kicked a 37 yard field goal for a 17-3 lead. The Rams had been held scoreless for two full quarters, their timing was broken, and the Patriots had turned three takeaways into 17 points without committing a turnover of their own. A team that lived on rhythm could not find any, because New England kept hitting receivers early and making Warner hold the ball longer than he wanted to hold it.

Then came the fourth quarter, and with it the part of the story that makes this game more than a simple upset. St. Louis finally reached the end zone, and it did so only after surviving what looked like a decisive New England touchdown. Warner fumbled near the goal line, Tebucky Jones picked up the ball and ran 97 yards for an apparent score, and a holding penalty on Willie McGinest wiped the play out. Two plays later, Warner scored on a 2 yard run, cutting the lead to 17-10. The Rams defense then forced two quick New England punts, Warner found Ricky Proehl for a 26 yard touchdown, and with 1 minute 30 seconds left the score was tied at 17-17. The team that had trailed by 14 points had erased the whole lead in one quarter, and the game that had looked settled was suddenly open again.

What happened next is the reason Brady became a star that night rather than merely a winning quarterback. New England took over at its own 17 yard line with 1 minute and 21 seconds left and no timeouts, and the cautious choice, voiced on the broadcast by John Madden, was to run out the clock and try to win in overtime. Belichick and Brady chose instead to play for the win in regulation. Brady completed short passes to J. R. Redmond, then found Troy Brown for 23 yards and Jermaine Wiggins for 6 yards, moving New England to the St. Louis 30 yard line. Brady spiked the ball with seven seconds left, and Vinatieri kicked a 48 yard field goal as time expired. It was the first time a Super Bowl had been won by a score on the final play.

The legacy of the game is larger than one kick. For New England, it was the first Super Bowl title in franchise history and the start of a run that would define the next two decades of the league. Brady, who finished with 145 passing yards and one touchdown, was named MVP not for gaudy numbers but for the final drive, when the game asked for calm and accuracy and he supplied both. For St. Louis, it was the end of a peak. The Rams had reached the Super Bowl for the second time in three seasons, and they would not return to the game as the St. Louis Rams. For the league, it was proof that a team built around defense, ball security, and situational nerve could beat a more explosive opponent, and proof that a young quarterback could be trusted with the final minute of the biggest game of the year. That trust became the foundation of everything that followed.

Why this game mattered

This was the first Super Bowl played in February. The game had originally been scheduled for January 27, 2002, and was moved to February 3 after the September 11, 2001 attacks led the NFL to postpone a week of regular season games. It was also designated a National Special Security Event, the first Super Bowl to receive that designation, reflecting the heightened security of the time.

The pregame and halftime presentations carried that context directly. Mariah Carey sang the national anthem, former President George H. W. Bush took part in the coin toss, and U2 used the halftime show to project the names of September 11 victims. Budweiser used its commercial time for a tribute rather than a joke. The game became, for one night, a national gathering point less than five months after the attacks.

The game also changed a Super Bowl custom. New England chose to be introduced as a team rather than by individual starters, a choice that stood out against the usual individual introductions and that later became standard practice for both teams.

Finally, the result reset the league hierarchy. A 14 point underdog beat the most celebrated offense of its era, a second year quarterback beat a former league MVP, and a franchise that had never won a Super Bowl began a run of sustained success. The final drive, played with no timeouts and no margin for a mistake, became the template for how Brady and Belichick would be described for the rest of their time together: calm late, exact when it mattered, and willing to play for the win instead of settling for overtime.
