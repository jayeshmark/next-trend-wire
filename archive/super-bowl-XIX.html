Super Bowl XIX: San Francisco 49ers vs Miami Dolphins

By Editorial Team

Result in brief: San Francisco won its second Super Bowl title, behind Joe Montana's second Super Bowl MVP award. Dan Marino, playing in his first Super Bowl at age 23, would never return to one.

The game story

The buildup to this game was the most hyped quarterback matchup the Super Bowl had ever seen, and for once the hype was justified by the numbers. Miami's Dan Marino, in only his second season, had just produced the greatest passing season in league history to that point: 5,084 yards and 48 touchdown passes, both records, for a 14 and 2 team coached by Don Shula. San Francisco's Joe Montana, already a Super Bowl MVP, led a 15 and 1 team coached by Bill Walsh, the first team ever to win fifteen regular season games in a sixteen game schedule. The combined records of the two teams, 33 and 3 including playoff games, remain the best combined records for a Super Bowl matchup. The game was billed as a battle between Marino's vertical passing attack and Montana's precision offense, between the future of quarterbacking and its present master. It was played at Stanford Stadium, effectively a home game for San Francisco, on the same day Ronald Reagan was privately sworn in for a second term as president, with Reagan performing the coin toss by satellite from the White House.

The first quarter suggested a shootout. Miami kicked a 37 yard field goal, San Francisco answered with a 33 yard touchdown pass from Montana to Carl Monroe, and Miami answered again with a 2 yard touchdown pass from Marino to Dan Johnson, taking a 10 to 7 lead. It was the last lead Miami would hold. The second quarter belonged entirely to San Francisco, and it decided the championship before halftime. Montana found Roger Craig for an 8 yard touchdown pass, then ran 6 yards for a touchdown himself, then watched Craig run 2 yards for another score. In less than a full quarter, a 10 to 7 deficit had become a 28 to 10 lead. Miami added two field goals before halftime, both by Uwe von Schamann, the second as time expired, but those kicks only made the score 28 to 16, and they would prove to be Miami's final points of the game. Montana had thrown for two touchdowns and run for another in the half, and San Francisco's defense had already begun to solve Marino, taking away the deep throws to Mark Clayton and Mark Duper that had defined Miami's season.

The second half was a demonstration of control. San Francisco kicked a 27 yard field goal by Ray Wersching early in the third quarter, then Montana found Craig for a 16 yard touchdown pass, Craig's third touchdown of the game, to make it 38 to 16. From there, San Francisco's defense finished the job, and Miami's offense, which had led the league in scoring with 513 points, never scored again. The final numbers captured the imbalance. Montana finished 24 of 35 for 331 yards, a Super Bowl record at the time, with three touchdown passes, plus 59 rushing yards and a rushing touchdown, a record for a quarterback in a Super Bowl. Marino finished 29 of 50 for 318 yards, making this the first Super Bowl in which both starting quarterbacks threw for more than 300 yards, but his two interceptions and Miami's nonexistent running game told the truer story. Craig finished with three touchdowns, then a Super Bowl record for one game, and 135 total yards from scrimmage. San Francisco's 537 yards of total offense broke the previous Super Bowl record by more than 100 yards.

The legacy of this game is larger than the score. For Montana, it was a second Super Bowl MVP award, and it settled, for that era at least, the argument about who was the best quarterback in football. For Marino, it became a haunting footnote: the greatest young quarterback of his generation, playing in his first Super Bowl at age 23, would never return to one, despite a Hall of Fame career that lasted seventeen seasons. For Walsh, the win confirmed that his system could beat not just any opponent but the most explosive passing offense the league had ever seen, by controlling the ball, scoring in bunches, and defending the pass with a secondary in which all four starters had made the Pro Bowl. The game also began the NFC's thirteen game Super Bowl winning streak, a run of dominance that would last until the 1996 season. And in a quieter historical note, it was the first Super Bowl televised by ABC, completing the three network rotation that would carry the game for decades.

Why this game mattered

This game was played on Inauguration Day, the only Super Bowl ever played on that date, and the coin toss itself became a piece of presidential history, performed by a sitting president via satellite from the White House. The broadcast was also a milestone for television, as ABC televised the Super Bowl for the first time, joining CBS and NBC in the rotation. On the field, the game became a symbol of a changing league: the precision, ball control offense beating the vertical passing game, and the NFC beginning a run of dominance that would define the next decade of professional football. The most lasting cultural thread, though, is Marino's. A 23 year old quarterback who had just set every major passing record played the game of his life in the conference championship and then lost the Super Bowl, and never got back. The game is now remembered as much for what it started, the Montana legend's second act and the NFC's long reign, as for what it ended, Marino's only chance at a championship.
