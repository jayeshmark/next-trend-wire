Super Bowl XXXIII: Denver Broncos vs Atlanta Falcons

By Editorial Team

Result in brief: Denver won back to back titles, the first AFC team to do so since Pittsburgh in the 1970s, and gave John Elway a second championship in his final game. Atlanta suffered its first Super Bowl defeat.

The game story

Super Bowl XXXIII was supposed to be a farewell, and it was also a reunion with a grudge. Denver was the defending champion, trying to win a second straight title. Atlanta was playing in its first Super Bowl, coached by Dan Reeves, who had coached Denver to three Super Bowl losses in the 1980s and had been fired by the Broncos. Reeves had rebuilt Atlanta around running back Jamal Anderson and the Dirty Birds defense, and the Falcons had gone 14 and 2, the same record as Denver. John Elway, at 38, had already announced that this would be his final game, or at least that it was widely expected to be. The stage was set for a close game. It was close for a quarter, then Elway threw one pass that changed everything.

Atlanta scored first, on a 32 yard field goal by Morten Andersen. Denver answered with a 1 yard run by Howard Griffith, then a 26 yard field goal by Jason Elam, for a 10 to 3 lead. Atlanta drove to the Denver 8 yard line late in the second quarter, with a chance to cut the lead to 10 to 6 or tie the game. On third down and goal, Atlanta could not score, and Andersen missed a 26 yard field goal attempt wide right. On the very next play, Elway threw deep to Rod Smith, who ran past two Atlanta defenders and caught the ball in stride for an 80 yard touchdown. A possible 10 to 6 game became a 17 to 3 Denver lead in one play. Andersen kicked a 28 yard field goal before halftime, but Denver led 17 to 6, and the game had already turned.

The third quarter was scoreless, which helped Denver more than Atlanta. The Falcons needed points, and they could not find them. Elam missed two field goals early in the second half, giving Atlanta brief hope, but Gordon ended that hope with two interceptions on consecutive Atlanta possessions inside the Denver 20 yard line. The first set up Griffith's second 1 yard touchdown run, making it 24 to 6. The second set up Elway's 3 yard touchdown run, making it 31 to 6. Atlanta scored on a 94 yard kickoff return by Tim Dwight, Denver answered with a 37 yard field goal by Elam, and Atlanta scored once more on a 3 yard pass from Chandler to Terance Mathis with 2 minutes and 4 seconds left, with a failed two point pass. The final score was 34 to 19.

Elway was named most valuable player, at 38 the oldest player to win the award at the time. He completed 18 of 29 passes for 336 yards, with one touchdown and one interception, and he ran for another touchdown. Davis, the league most valuable player that season after rushing for more than 2,000 yards, carried 25 times for 102 yards, his seventh straight 100 yard game in the postseason, a record at the time. Smith caught 5 passes for 152 yards, including the 80 yard touchdown that broke the game open.

For Denver, the win made the Broncos the first AFC team to win back to back titles since Pittsburgh in the 1970s, and it gave Elway the perfect ending: a second championship in his final game, with the most valuable player trophy to go with it. For Atlanta, the loss was a first Super Bowl defeat, and it made Reeves the fourth coach to lose four Super Bowls. The Falcons would not return to a Super Bowl for eighteen years. This game is remembered for Elway going out on top, for Smith's 80 yard catch, and for a missed field goal that turned into a touchdown on the very next play, the kind of swing that decides championships.

Cultural context

This was Elway's last game, and the NFL treated it that way. Elway had been the face of Denver sports for sixteen seasons, and his retirement after a second straight title gave the league a storybook ending it rarely gets. The game also marked the end of the 1990s as a football decade. Dallas, San Francisco, Green Bay, and Denver had all won titles in the decade, and the next decade would belong to New England, Pittsburgh, and Indianapolis. Atlanta's Dirty Birds, with their touchdown dance and their underdog run, became a beloved footnote, and Reeves' fourth Super Bowl loss closed the book on a coaching career that reached four Super Bowls without winning one. The halftime show, mixing soul, salsa, and swing, was a fitting close to a decade in which the halftime show had grown from a marching band break into a major pop event, a change that began with Michael Jackson six years earlier and ended with Stevie Wonder and Gloria Estefan sharing a stage in Miami.
