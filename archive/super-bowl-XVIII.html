Super Bowl XVIII: Los Angeles Raiders vs Washington

By Editorial Team

Result in brief: Los Angeles won the franchise's third championship, and its first in Los Angeles, beating the defending champions by 29 points, a Super Bowl record margin at the time. Note on names: this page refers to the team as Washington throughout, following the current team name. Contemporary sources from 1984 use the team's former name.

The game story

Washington arrived in Tampa as the defending champion and the favorite, and the case for Washington was formidable. The defending champions had finished 14 and 2, had set a then NFL record with 541 points in the regular season, and had allowed the fewest rushing yards in the league. John Riggins had run for 1,347 yards in the regular season, Joe Theismann had been named the league's Most Valuable Player, and the Hogs offensive line was regarded as the best in football. The Los Angeles Raiders, in only their second season in Los Angeles after moving from Oakland, had finished 12 and 4. They were talented and volatile in equal measure, a team built around Jim Plunkett's steady quarterbacking, Marcus Allen's versatility, and a defense that hit hard and took chances. Washington was favored by three points. Almost nobody expected what followed.

The Raiders scored in all three phases before the game was half over, and that fact alone explains the result. Early in the first quarter, Los Angeles blocked a Washington punt, and Derrick Jensen recovered the ball in the end zone for a touchdown. It was the kind of play that changes a favorite's posture: Washington, a team built to play with a lead and run the ball, was behind before its offense had found any rhythm. Plunkett then found Cliff Branch for a 12 yard touchdown pass in the second quarter to make it 14 to 0. Washington answered with a Mark Moseley field goal, but the defining play of the half came with seven seconds left. Washington, trying to score before halftime from near its own goal line, called a screen pass. Squirek, a reserve linebacker inserted specifically because the Raiders expected the screen, stepped in front of Theismann's throw and walked 5 yards into the end zone. The score was 21 to 3 at halftime, and the game, for practical purposes, was over. Washington's sideline knew it. The Raiders' sideline knew it. The only remaining question was the size of the margin.

The second half answered that question with Marcus Allen. Washington opened the third quarter with its best drive of the night, capped by a 1 yard Riggins touchdown run, but the extra point was blocked, leaving the score at 21 to 9. Los Angeles answered with a 5 yard Allen touchdown run to make it 28 to 9. Then, on the last play of the third quarter, Allen produced the run that defines this game and much of his career. Taking a handoff designed to go left, Allen cut back against the flow of the defense, broke into the open field, and ran 74 yards for a touchdown, then the longest touchdown run in Super Bowl history. The run made the score 35 to 9, and it gave Allen 191 rushing yards for the game, breaking the Super Bowl record Riggins had set the year before. NFL Films narrator John Facenda, in what would be his final voiceover work for the league's highlight films, described the run with a line that has outlived the game itself, about Allen running with the night. In the fourth quarter, a 39 yard Allen run set up a 21 yard Chris Bahr field goal, and the final score was 38 to 9.

The margin, 29 points, was a Super Bowl record at the time, as were the Raiders' 38 points. Allen finished with 20 carries for 191 yards and two touchdowns, plus two catches for 18 yards, for 209 total yards from scrimmage, and was named MVP. He became only the third Heisman Trophy winner to win Super Bowl MVP, after Roger Staubach and Jim Plunkett. For Washington, the defeat became known as Black Sunday, a humiliation for a team that had scored more points than any team in league history and then managed only nine in the championship game. The loss also ended Washington's bid to repeat as champions, only the second time a defending champion had lost a Super Bowl. For the Raiders, the win was the franchise's third championship, and its first in Los Angeles, achieved in only the team's second season in the city. It remains, as of this draft, the Raiders' most recent Super Bowl championship.

  2. Plunkett finds Branch. A 12 yard touchdown pass makes it 14 to 0 in the second quarter.
  3. Washington gets on the board. Moseley's 24 yard field goal makes it 14 to 3.
  4. Squirek breaks the game open. His 5 yard interception return with seven seconds left in the half makes it 21 to 3.
  5. Washington answers, briefly. Riggins' 1 yard run makes it 21 to 9, but the extra point is blocked.

Why this game mattered

Two commercials from this broadcast outlived the game itself, which is rare for any Super Bowl. Apple's 1984 spot, directed by Ridley Scott, introduced the Macintosh two days before the computer went on sale, and it turned the Super Bowl ad break into a cultural event in its own right, with companies from then on designing commercials specifically to be talked about on Monday morning. Wendy's Where's the Beef spot gave the decade one of its defining catchphrases. The game also marked a turning point for the Raiders franchise: the first championship for Los Angeles since the Rams in 1951, and the last championship the Raiders have won in any city. On the Washington side, the scale of the defeat reshaped how the defending champions were remembered. A team that had set a scoring record and was favored to repeat was beaten in every phase, on special teams, on defense, and on the ground, in a game that is still cited as the clearest example of a favorite collapsing on the largest stage.
