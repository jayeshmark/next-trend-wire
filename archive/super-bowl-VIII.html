The game story

Miami arrived in Houston as the defending champion, but not quite the same team that had gone undefeated the year before. The 1973 Dolphins had lost twice in the regular season, then beaten Cincinnati and Oakland in the playoffs to reach a third straight Super Bowl. Minnesota arrived with its own claim to attention: the Vikings had beaten Washington and Dallas in the playoffs, and they brought Fran Tarkenton, one of the most inventive quarterbacks of his era, plus a defense known as the Purple People Eaters. On paper, this looked like a matchup between a great running team and a great defensive team. On the field, only one of those descriptions held up.

Miami scored on its first two possessions, and both drives looked almost identical: long, patient, physical marches that Minnesota could not stop. The first went 62 yards in 10 plays and ended with Larry Csonka running 5 yards for a touchdown. The second went 56 yards in 10 plays and ended with Jim Kiick running 1 yard for a touchdown. By the end of the first quarter, Miami led 14 to 0, had run 20 plays for 118 yards, and had held Minnesota to 25 yards on 6 plays. The game was not over in any formal sense, but anyone watching could see the shape of it. Miami's offensive line was moving Minnesota's defensive line backward, play after play, and Csonka was the runner doing the damage.

The second quarter brought Minnesota's one real chance. The Vikings drove from their own 20 to the Miami 7, with less than a minute left in the half, and faced fourth down and 1 from the 6. Instead of kicking a field goal, Minnesota tried to run for the first down. Oscar Reed took the ball, was hit by Nick Buoniconti, fumbled, and Scott recovered. Miami then added a 28 yard field goal by Garo Yepremian before halftime to lead 17 to 0, and the missed opportunity hung over Minnesota for the rest of the day. A field goal there would not have changed the final result by itself, but it would have given Minnesota something to build on. Instead, the Vikings went into halftime scoreless, having come within 6 yards of the end zone and come away with nothing.

Miami put the game away in the third quarter when Csonka ran 2 yards for his second touchdown, making it 24 to 0. Only then, in the fourth quarter, did Minnesota score, when Tarkenton ran 4 yards for a touchdown. Minnesota even recovered an onside kick after that score, but a penalty ended the brief rally, and Miami ran out the clock behind Csonka and Kiick. The final statistics told the story in miniature: Griese threw only 7 passes, completing 6 for 73 yards, the fewest attempts by a winning team in Super Bowl history to that point, while Csonka carried 33 times for 145 yards, both Super Bowl records at the time. He became the first running back to win Super Bowl MVP.

The win made Miami the second team to win back to back Super Bowls, after Green Bay, and the first team to reach three straight Super Bowls. It also marked the peak of the early 1970s Dolphins. Within two years, Csonka, Kiick, and Paul Warfield would leave for the World Football League, and Miami would not return to the Super Bowl until after the 1982 season. For Minnesota, the loss was the second of four Super Bowl defeats, and it followed a familiar pattern: a strong regular season, a strong playoff run, and a Super Bowl in which the Vikings never really threatened. The 1973 Dolphins are often overshadowed by the perfect 1972 team, but this game showed what made them great in a different way. The perfect team had won with balance and defense. This team won by lining up, running the ball, and daring Minnesota to stop it, and Minnesota could not.

Why this game mattered

Super Bowl VIII was the first Super Bowl played at a stadium that was not home to an NFL team, Rice Stadium in Houston, and the first played outside the Los Angeles, Miami, and New Orleans areas. It was also the last Super Bowl in which the goal posts stood at the front of the end zone. On the field, it confirmed Miami as the team of the early 1970s, the first club to play in three straight Super Bowls and only the second to win two in a row. The game also became a reference point for how a running game can control a championship: Miami threw 7 passes, ran 53 times, held the ball for 33 minutes 45 seconds, and never trailed. For Minnesota, it added another chapter to a painful pattern that would repeat twice more in the decade, a team good enough to reach the Super Bowl four times and unable to win any of them.
