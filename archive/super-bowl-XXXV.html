Super Bowl XXXV: Baltimore Ravens vs New York Giants

By Editorial Team

Baltimore won its first Super Bowl title. New York was seeking its third Super Bowl title. The Giants did not score an offensive touchdown, and their only touchdown came on a kickoff return.

The game story

The stakes were straightforward, and they pointed in opposite directions. Baltimore arrived with the best defense of its era, a unit that had carried a limited offense all season and had given up very few points in the playoffs. New York arrived as the NFC champion with a balanced team and a quarterback, Kerry Collins, who had played the best football of his career in the postseason. A Baltimore win would give the franchise its first title since the old Cleveland Browns became the Ravens. A New York win would restore a proud franchise to the top of the league after a decade away from the Super Bowl.

The first half showed why Baltimore was favored, even if the scoreboard stayed close enough to keep New York hopeful. Trent Dilfer threw a 38 yard touchdown pass to Brandon Stokley in the first quarter, and the Ravens led 7-0. The Giants offense, meanwhile, could not get started against a Baltimore defense that played fast, tackled hard, and left almost no room on the edges. New York punted again and again, and the only points of the second quarter came on a 47 yard Matt Stover field goal that made it 10-0 at halftime. Ten points did not look like much, but the way the half had gone made it feel larger. New York had not threatened to score, and Baltimore had not needed to take any risks.

The third quarter settled the game in a burst that remains one of the strangest sequences in Super Bowl history. First, Duane Starks intercepted Collins at the New York 49 yard line and ran it back for a touchdown, pushing the lead to 17-0. On the kickoff that followed, Ron Dixon ran 97 yards for a New York touchdown, and suddenly the Giants had life at 17-7. That life lasted exactly one kickoff. Jermaine Lewis took the next kickoff 84 yards for a Baltimore touchdown, restoring a 24-7 lead before the stadium had time to settle. Three touchdowns on three consecutive plays, two of them on kickoffs, in a span that felt like seconds. The Giants never recovered from the swing, because the swing exposed the real shape of the game: Baltimore could score without its offense doing anything special, and New York could not score with its offense at all.

The fourth quarter was a formality with one more Baltimore touchdown. Jamal Lewis, who had run for 102 yards on the night, scored from three yards out with 8:51 left, and Stover added a 34 yard field goal to close the scoring at 34-7. The final numbers told the story plainly. Collins threw four interceptions and finished with 112 passing yards. The Giants finished the night without an offensive touchdown, a fact that has followed the game ever since. Baltimore, by contrast, had scored on offense, on defense, and on special teams, which is about as complete a team win as a Super Bowl can produce.

The legacy of the game belongs mostly to the Baltimore defense. Ray Lewis was named MVP, the second linebacker to win the award, and the Ravens defense entered the conversation about the best single season defenses in league history. That reputation has lasted because the performance was not a one game spike: Baltimore had dominated through the playoffs, and the Super Bowl was the final proof rather than a surprise. For New York, the game became a harsh lesson in what happens when a good team meets a historically great defense on a night when its quarterback cannot find a rhythm. The Giants would return to the Super Bowl seven years later and win, but this night in Tampa belonged entirely to Baltimore, and the scoreboard, 34-7, still reads like a fair summary of how far apart the two teams were.

Scoring timeline

The third quarter sequence decided the game and defined how it is remembered. Starks turned a Collins pass into a 49 yard touchdown, Dixon answered with a 97 yard kickoff return, and Jermaine Lewis answered that with an 84 yard kickoff return. The Giants went from a 17-0 deficit to a 17-7 deficit to a 24-7 deficit without their offense taking a snap in between. After that exchange, Baltimore led by 17 points with most of the second half still to play, and New York, which had not scored on offense all night, had no plausible path back.

Why this game mattered

Super Bowl XXXV is remembered as a defensive statement. Baltimore's win gave weight to the idea that a great defense, paired with a careful offense that avoided mistakes, could win a championship outright, and it made Ray Lewis the face of that idea. The game also marked a changing of the guard in how the Super Bowl felt as a television event: the halftime show paired a classic rock band with the biggest pop act of the moment, plus three guest stars, a multi-genre format that became the standard for years afterward. For the Giants, the loss was a reminder of how quickly a season can end when the offense stalls; their lone touchdown came from the return game, not from a drive, and that detail is why the game is still used as an example of a defense winning a Super Bowl on its own terms.
