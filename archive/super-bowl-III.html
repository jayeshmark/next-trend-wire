The game story

Super Bowl III was a mismatch on paper, and that was exactly the point. Baltimore arrived as champion of the established NFL, a team that had lost only once in the regular season and then shut out Cleveland 34 to 0 in the NFL Championship Game. New York arrived as champion of the younger AFL, a league still fighting for respect after Green Bay had won the first two championship games between the leagues by wide margins. The Jets had earned their place by beating Oakland 27 to 23 in the AFL Championship Game, but few outside their locker room treated them as equals. Baltimore was a heavy favorite, and the week in Miami turned into a referendum on whether the AFL belonged on the same field at all.

Joe Namath answered that question before kickoff, and his answer became part of American sports language. At a Miami Touchdown Club dinner three nights before the game, Namath said the Jets would win and guaranteed it. The remark was bold to the point of recklessness, given Baltimore's favorite status and recent history. On Sunday they played like a team that expected to win.

The first quarter gave no hint of the upset on the scoreboard. Neither team scored. The tension came from what Baltimore could not do. The Colts moved the ball at times, and Tom Matte ran with real power, but the Jets defense kept turning promising drives into empty ones. That pattern shaped the afternoon.

The breakthrough came in the second quarter, and it came the way the Jets had planned it. Namath worked the ball with short, careful passes, leaning on George Sauer. Matt Snell did the heavy work on the ground. Snell capped an 80 yard drive with a 4 yard touchdown run, and Jim Turner added the extra point for a 7 to 0 lead. New York had marched the length of the field.

Baltimore had chances to answer before halftime, and each missed chance added weight to the Jets lead. Earl Morrall, who had stepped in for the injured Johnny Unitas during the season and won NFL MVP honors, threw three interceptions in the game. Randy Beverly intercepted Morrall in the end zone early in the second quarter. Later in the half, after Matte broke free for a 58 yard run, Johnny Sample intercepted Morrall at the Jets 2 yard line. Then came the play that still defines the game for many who study it. On the final play of the first half, Baltimore ran a flea flicker. Matte took the handoff, pitched the ball back to Morrall, and Jimmy Orr broke open near the end zone while the Jets defense reacted to the run. Morrall did not see Orr. He threw instead toward Jerry Hill, and Jim Hudson intercepted the pass as time expired. Baltimore went to halftime down 7 to 0, scoreless despite several trips deep into Jets territory.

The second half turned a warning into control. New York did not chase a spectacular finish. New York played for field position, for clock, and for points that forced Baltimore to chase the game. Turner kicked a 32 yard field goal in the third quarter, then a 30 yard field goal later in the same quarter, and the lead grew to 13 to 0. In the fourth quarter, Turner added a 9 yard field goal, and the score reached 16 to 0. The Jets held the ball for more than 36 minutes.

Don Shula eventually turned to Unitas, the injured star who had missed most of the season with a sore elbow. Unitas led Baltimore to its only score, a 1 yard touchdown run by Jerry Hill with 3 minutes and 19 seconds left, followed by a Lou Michaels extra point. The score made it 16 to 7, and briefly raised the question of a miracle finish. New York answered with the ball, running the clock down, forcing Baltimore to use its timeouts, and punting with only seconds left. Baltimore tried two more passes, and the game ended. Namath ran off the field holding up one finger, a gesture that needed no explanation.

The legacy goes beyond the final score, and beyond Namath. This was the first Super Bowl victory for the AFL, and it changed how the coming merger between the leagues would be understood. The AFL had already forced a merger agreement in 1966, with full merger set for 1970, but an agreement on paper is not the same as respect on the field. After two lopsided losses, the AFL needed proof that its champion could beat the NFL champion when it mattered. The Jets gave it that proof with a complete performance: a controlled passing game, a strong running game, disciplined kicking, and a defense that intercepted four passes and never let Baltimore settle.

For the Jets, the win remains a defining moment in franchise history. For Namath, it secured a place in football lore. He won MVP honors without throwing a touchdown pass and without throwing a pass in the fourth quarter, a reminder that this was a team victory built on control rather than highlight plays. For Baltimore, the loss remains one of the great shocks in the sport. And for the Super Bowl itself, the game helped turn a championship contest into a cultural event.

Why this game mattered

Super Bowl III mattered because it tested a claim about two leagues, not just two teams. The NFL was the established league. The AFL was the challenger, and after two championship losses it was widely treated as inferior. A Jets win did not erase that history in one afternoon, but it gave the AFL a result it could point to before the full merger in 1970. It showed that an AFL champion could control a game against an NFL champion with defense, ball control, and kicking, not only with offense.

The game also mattered because of how it entered public memory. Namath's guarantee gave people a story before kickoff, and his delivery gave that story an ending. The upset turned Namath into a national celebrity and turned the Super Bowl into more than a title game. It became a stage where confidence, narrative, and a single result could reshape how a league was seen. That is why Super Bowl III is still cited as one of the greatest upsets in American sports history, even though the final margin was only 9 points. The shock was not the margin. The shock was who won, and what the win proved.
