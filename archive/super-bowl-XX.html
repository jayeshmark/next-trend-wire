Super Bowl XX: Chicago Bears vs New England Patriots

By Editorial Team

Result in brief: Chicago won its first Super Bowl title, behind the 46 defense, scoring 44 consecutive points after New England's opening field goal. Chicago's 46 points set a Super Bowl record, and its 36 point margin was the largest in Super Bowl history at the time.

The game story

No team in the modern era entered a Super Bowl with a larger cultural footprint than the 1985 Chicago Bears, and no team entered one with a thinner margin for error than the New England Patriots. Chicago had finished 15 and 1, becoming only the second team ever to win fifteen regular season games, and had outscored its opponents 456 to 198. Its defense, coordinator Buddy Ryan's 46 defense, had allowed the fewest points, the fewest total yards, and the fewest rushing yards in the league, and had recorded two shutouts in the playoffs, beating the New York Giants 21 to 0 and the Los Angeles Rams 24 to 0. The Bears had also recorded a rap song and music video, the Super Bowl Shuffle, during the season, in which each player announced his intention to go to New Orleans. The song reached the Billboard charts and was nominated for a Grammy, and it made the Bears famous beyond football before they had won anything. New England, by contrast, had finished 11 and 5 as a wild card team, third in its own division, and had reached the Super Bowl by winning three road playoff games, at the New York Jets, at the Los Angeles Raiders, and at Miami, where the Patriots had not won since 1966. Chicago was favored by ten points, and that line looked generous to New England.

The game began with the fastest score in Super Bowl history, and for about ninety seconds it looked like an upset might be possible. Chicago's Walter Payton fumbled on the Bears' first possession, New England recovered at the Chicago 19 yard line, and Tony Franklin kicked a 36 yard field goal only 1 minute and 19 seconds into the game. Then the Bears scored 44 consecutive points. Kevin Butler kicked field goals of 28 and 24 yards, Matt Suhey ran 11 yards for a touchdown, and Chicago led 13 to 3 after the first quarter. In the second quarter, McMahon ran 2 yards for a touchdown, and Butler added another 24 yard field goal as time expired in the half, making it 23 to 3. The first half yardage told the story more brutally than the score: Chicago 236 yards, New England negative 19. New England's offense, which had scored 31 points at Miami in the conference championship, could not complete a pass, could not run the ball, and could not protect its quarterbacks against a defense that seemed to arrive at the snap.

The second half removed any remaining doubt, and it produced the game's most enduring images. After New England's first drive of the half ended in a punt to the Chicago 4 yard line, the Bears marched 96 yards in nine plays, capped by a 1 yard McMahon touchdown run, making McMahon the first quarterback to rush for two touchdowns in a Super Bowl. Phillips then returned an interception 28 yards for a touchdown. Then came the play every highlight reel still shows: William Perry, the 300 plus pound defensive tackle nicknamed the Refrigerator, lined up at fullback near the goal line and plunged 1 yard for a touchdown, while Payton, the greatest running back in franchise history, watched from the backfield without scoring in the game. New England finally scored a touchdown in the fourth quarter, when Grogan found Irving Fryar for an 8 yard pass, but Waechter answered with a safety, sacking Grogan in the end zone, and the final score was 46 to 10. Chicago's 46 points set a Super Bowl record, and its 36 point margin was the largest in Super Bowl history at the time.

The legacy of this game is complicated by what did not happen next. Dent was named MVP, the fourth defensive player to win the award, after 1.5 sacks and two forced fumbles, and head coach Mike Ditka became only the second man to win a Super Bowl as both a player and a head coach. Yet the Bears never returned to a Super Bowl with this core, losing in the divisional round the following season and gradually breaking apart amid injuries and internal disputes. Payton, who gained only 61 yards on 22 carries because New England devoted its defense to stopping him, never scored a touchdown in a Super Bowl, a fact that still surprises fans who remember the 1985 Bears as invincible. For New England, the defeat was the franchise's first Super Bowl appearance, and it would be fifteen years before the Patriots returned. The game also fell two days before the Space Shuttle Challenger disaster, and the Bears' planned White House visit was cancelled in the aftermath, a somber footnote to a season remembered mostly for noise, swagger, and a defense that may have been the best ever assembled.

Why this game mattered

The 1985 Bears were a cultural phenomenon before they were champions, and this game is remembered as much for the team around the score as for the score itself. The Super Bowl Shuffle, recorded months before the championship, turned a football team into a pop act, and the Bears' brashness, from McMahon's headbands to Perry's goal line carries to Ryan's defense, made them the most famous team of the decade. The game also sits in a somber week in American history: it was played on January 26, 1986, two days before the Challenger disaster, which cancelled the team's White House visit and cast a shadow over the celebration. The Bears did not visit the White House as champions until 2011, long after Payton's death. On the field, the game became the standard against which great defenses are measured, with Chicago's seven sacks, seven rushing yards allowed, and 36 point margin standing as a benchmark for dominance. It also left a bittersweet legacy for Payton, arguably the greatest running back of his era, who dominated the season, drew the entire New England defense in the Super Bowl, and finished without a touchdown on the sport's largest stage.
