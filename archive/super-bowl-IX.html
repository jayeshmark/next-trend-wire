The game story

This was a matchup of the two best defenses in football, and it played out exactly that way, only more so. Pittsburgh's Steel Curtain against Minnesota's Purple People Eaters was the billing, and for a half, neither offense could do anything at all. The first half produced one score, and it came from the defense. Fran Tarkenton fumbled a handoff to Dave Osborn, scrambled to recover the ball in his own end zone, and was tackled there by Dwight White for a safety. Pittsburgh led 2 to 0 at halftime, a baseball score in a football championship, and the Vikings had managed only 76 total yards and 6 first downs in the half.

Pittsburgh's defensive plan was specific and ruthless. The Steelers lined up Joe Greene at an angle over Minnesota center Mick Tingelhoff and had Ernie Holmes play directly over him, sometimes taking Tingelhoff on so Greene could slip through, sometimes crashing into him while Holmes looped behind. The result was that Tarkenton rarely had a clean pocket and Minnesota's running game never got started. The Vikings finished with 17 rushing yards as a team, which is less than many single runs in other Super Bowls, and Tarkenton completed only 11 of 26 passes for 102 yards with 3 interceptions. Chuck Foreman, Minnesota's most dangerous offensive player, was both the team's leading rusher and its leading receiver, with 18 rushing yards and 50 receiving yards, which tells you how little room Minnesota had anywhere.

The game turned for good on the opening kickoff of the second half. Minnesota's Bill Brown fumbled the kickoff, Marv Kellum recovered for Pittsburgh at the Minnesota 30, and four plays later Franco Harris ran 9 yards for a touchdown. Harris was the difference between a great defense and a championship team. He carried 34 times for 158 yards, a Super Bowl record at the time, breaking the mark Larry Csonka had set the year before. Rocky Bleier added 65 yards, and Pittsburgh ran the ball 57 times in all, another Super Bowl record. Harris alone gained more yards on the ground than the entire Minnesota offense gained in total.

Minnesota did get back into the game, briefly, in the fourth quarter, but not through its offense. Matt Blair blocked a Bobby Walden punt, Terry Brown recovered in the end zone, and the extra point failed, so Pittsburgh's lead was cut to 9 to 6. That was as close as Minnesota got. Pittsburgh answered with a 66 yard drive in 11 plays, capped by Bradshaw's 4 yard touchdown pass to Larry Brown with 3:31 left, and the final score was 16 to 6. Mike Wagner intercepted Tarkenton soon after, and Pittsburgh ran out the clock.

The win was Pittsburgh's first Super Bowl title and the first championship of any kind in franchise history, which had begun in 1933. It was also the start of something larger: the Steelers would win four Super Bowls in six seasons, and this defense-first, run-first formula was the foundation. For Minnesota, it was a third Super Bowl loss in three tries, and the most one sided of the three, because the Vikings never scored on offense at all. Chuck Noll, at 42 years and 7 days, became the youngest head coach to win a Super Bowl at the time. The game is remembered as a defensive masterpiece, but it is worth remembering what it cost Pittsburgh to produce it: two starting linebackers lost to injury, a quarterback who threw for under 100 yards, and a running back who simply would not be stopped.

Scoring timeline

  1. A scoreless first quarter. Two great defenses cancel each other out, and neither offense finds rhythm.
  2. The first safety in Super Bowl history. White downs Tarkenton in the end zone for a 2 to 0 Pittsburgh lead at halftime.
  3. The second half kickoff fumble. Bill Brown fumbles, Kellum recovers at the Minnesota 30, and Harris scores four plays later for a 9 to 0 lead.
  4. Harris takes over. His 158 rushing yards set a Super Bowl record and keep Pittsburgh in control.
  5. Minnesota's special teams score. Blair's blocked punt and Terry Brown's recovery cut the lead to 9 to 6, but the extra point fails.
  6. Pittsburgh closes it out. Bradshaw's touchdown pass to Larry Brown makes it 16 to 6, and Wagner's interception ends Minnesota's last hope.

Why this game mattered

Super Bowl IX was the last professional game played at Tulane Stadium, and it closed that building with a defensive performance that still stands out in Super Bowl history. It launched the Pittsburgh Steelers dynasty of the 1970s, a run of four championships in six years built on defense and running, and it gave Chuck Noll's program its first title after years of losing. For Minnesota, it deepened a reputation that would follow the franchise for decades: a team that could reach the biggest game repeatedly and could not win it. The 2 to 0 halftime score remains one of the strangest in Super Bowl history, a reminder that the championship game was not always a shootout, and that for one January afternoon in New Orleans, a safety was enough to lead at halftime of a Super Bowl.
