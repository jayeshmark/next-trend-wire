Decisive defensive plays

  • Mike Jones tackle: on the final play, Rams linebacker Mike Jones tackled Titans receiver Kevin Dyson at the 1 yard line as time expired, stopping a potential game tying touchdown. The play is known as The Tackle and as One Yard Short.
  • Todd Lyght blocked field goal: on Tennessee's opening drive of the second half, Lyght blocked a 47 yard Al Del Greco field goal attempt, keeping the Titans scoreless and preserving a 9-0 Rams lead at that point.
  • No turnovers: neither team lost a turnover in the game. St. Louis won the yardage battle, 436 yards to 367, while Tennessee held the ball for 36 minutes and 26 seconds to St. Louis's 23 minutes and 34 seconds.

The game story

Both teams arrived in Atlanta with 13-3 regular season records and with the same prize in sight, a first Super Bowl title. For St. Louis, the road there had been startling. The Rams had gone 4-12 the season before, then lost planned starter Trent Green to a season ending knee injury in the preseason. Kurt Warner, an undrafted quarterback who had played in the Arena Football League and NFL Europe, took over and led an offense nicknamed The Greatest Show on Turf. The Pro Football Hall of Fame records that Warner threw for 4,353 yards and 41 touchdowns in 1999, was named NFL Most Valuable Player, and led an offense that scored 526 points. Tennessee had a different kind of unlikely run. This was the franchise's first season under the Titans name after retiring the Oilers name, its first playoff run since 1993, and a wild card run at that. In the wild card round, Kevin Dyson scored on a kickoff lateral from Frank Wycheck in the closing seconds against Buffalo, a play remembered as the Music City Miracle, in a 22-16 win. Tennessee then beat Indianapolis 19-16 and Jacksonville 33-14 to reach its first Super Bowl.

The first half did not match the hype of the Rams offense, and that was part of what made it tense. St. Louis moved the ball at will but could not finish drives. The Rams outgained Tennessee 294 yards to 89 in the first half, yet led only 9-0 at halftime on three Jeff Wilkins field goals. A bobbled snap aborted one St. Louis field goal attempt, Wilkins missed another, and Tennessee missed a field goal of its own. The Titans defense deserves credit for that scoreline. It bent repeatedly without breaking in its own red zone, and it kept St. Louis stuck on three points at a time. Tennessee, meanwhile, could not establish any rhythm on offense. Steve McNair and Eddie George found little room, and the Rams went into the break looking like the better team without having produced a touchdown to prove it.

The second half opened with a warning that the game was not settled. Tennessee drove into scoring range on the first possession after halftime, but Todd Lyght blocked Al Del Greco's 47 yard field goal attempt. St. Louis answered on the next drive, when Warner threw a 9 yard touchdown pass to Torry Holt for a 16-0 lead with 7 minutes and 20 seconds left in the third quarter. At that point the game looked done. It was not. Tennessee responded with two long, patient touchdown drives, both finished by Eddie George, first from 1 yard out and then from 2 yards out. A two point conversion attempt after the first touchdown failed, so the second touchdown cut the lead to 16-13 rather than tying it. St. Louis could score in a single play. Tennessee needed time, carries, and short passes, and it was using the clock as a weapon.

With 2 minutes and 12 seconds left, Del Greco kicked a 43 yard field goal and the score was tied at 16-16. St. Louis had blown a 16 point lead, the largest deficit erased in a Super Bowl to that point, and Tennessee had all the momentum. It lasted one play. On the next play from scrimmage, Warner threw deep down the right sideline to Isaac Bruce, who caught the ball, cut inside, and outran the defense for a 73 yard touchdown. St. Louis led 23-16 with 1 minute and 54 seconds left. Tennessee still had a chance, and McNair made it real. He escaped pressure, kept the drive alive, and completed a 16 yard pass to Dyson that put Tennessee at the St. Louis 10 yard line in the final seconds. With no timeouts left, Tennessee had one play. McNair threw a quick slant to Dyson, who caught the ball in stride and turned toward the end zone. Mike Jones met him, wrapped him up, and brought him down at the 1 yard line as time expired. Dyson stretched the ball toward the goal line, but it never crossed it.

The finish is why this game endures, but the wider story matters too. Warner was named Super Bowl MVP after his record 414 yards, becoming the first undrafted quarterback to win a Super Bowl and the sixth player to win the NFL MVP and Super Bowl MVP in the same season. The win gave the Rams their first Super Bowl title and their first NFL championship since 1951, when the franchise played in Los Angeles. For Tennessee, the loss was brutal in its precision. A team that had survived the Music City Miracle by inches lost a Super Bowl by a yard, and in some accounts by even less. The final play, The Tackle, became the game's shorthand, a reminder that a season built on one miracle can end on one tackle. It also marked a changing league. The Rams had shown that a fast, pass first offense could win a championship indoors, and the Titans had shown that toughness, clock control, and a quarterback who could run could nearly beat it. Twenty five years on, the score is almost secondary. People remember the stretch, the tackle, and the yard that was not there.

  4. Third quarter, Holt 9 yard touchdown from Warner: St. Louis leads 16-0 with 7 minutes and 20 seconds left in the third quarter, and the game looks decided.
  5. Third quarter, George 1 yard touchdown run, two point attempt failed: Tennessee cuts the lead to 16-6 with 14 seconds left in the third quarter and starts its comeback.
  6. Fourth quarter, George 2 yard touchdown run: Tennessee cuts the lead to 16-13 with 7 minutes and 21 seconds left, after a 79 yard drive.
  7. Fourth quarter, Del Greco 43 yard field goal: Tennessee ties the game 16-16 with 2 minutes and 12 seconds left, completing a comeback from 16 points down.

Notable commercials

This game is often called the Dot-com Super Bowl because so many internet companies bought advertising during it, at the height of the dot-com bubble. On average, a 30 second spot sold for about $2.1 million to $2.2 million, after ABC initially sought $1.8 million to $1.9 million, with some late units selling for as much as $3 million.

Why this game mattered

Super Bowl XXXIV sat at a hinge point in American culture. It was played on January 30, 2000, weeks into a new millennium, and its halftime show was literally a Disney millennium celebration, Tapestry of Nations, brought indoors. Its advertising told the same story from the other side: a wave of internet companies, many of which would not survive the year, paying top prices to introduce themselves to a mass television audience. The game is therefore remembered in two ways at once, as a classic finish and as a time capsule of the dot-com peak.

On the field, it also marked a shift in how championships could be built. St. Louis won with speed, spread passing, and a quarterback nobody had planned around, after a 4-12 season. Tennessee nearly won with the opposite formula: a punishing running game, a mobile quarterback, clock control, and a defense that refused to break in the red zone. The final image, Dyson stretching for a goal line he could not reach, gave the NFL one of its most replayed finishes and gave Warner's unlikely rise a lasting frame. Warner's story was later dramatized in the 2021 film American Underdog. For the Titans, the game remains the franchise's closest brush with a championship in the Titans era, one yard away after a playoff run that had already produced one miracle.
