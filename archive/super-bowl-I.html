The game story

The game played in Los Angeles on January 15, 1967, was not yet called the Super Bowl in any official sense. It was the AFL-NFL World Championship Game, the first meeting between the champions of two rival professional leagues. The National Football League had been the established league for decades. The American Football League, which began play in 1960, was younger, brasher, and still fighting to be taken seriously. On June 8, 1966, the leagues had agreed to merge, with the merger to take full effect in 1970. A championship game between their winners was part of that agreement. This game was the first test of the idea, and the test carried a clear question: was the gap between the leagues as wide as the NFL believed?

Green Bay arrived as the standard of the older league. Vince Lombardi's Packers had won the NFL title in 1965 and defended it on January 1, 1967, with a 34-27 win over the Dallas Cowboys. Kansas City arrived as the AFL champion after a 31-7 win over the Buffalo Bills on the same day. Hank Stram's Chiefs were confident and well coached, but almost everyone outside their camp expected a Green Bay win. The Packers were favored by 14 points, and Lombardi felt the weight of that expectation. A loss would not just have cost Green Bay a trophy.

For a half, Kansas City refused to follow the script. Green Bay struck first when Bart Starr found reserve receiver Max McGee for a 37 yard touchdown. McGee had caught only four passes for 91 yards and one touchdown during the regular season, and he entered the game only after starter Boyd Dowler was hurt early. His first touchdown gave Green Bay a 7-0 lead and remains the first touchdown in Super Bowl history. Kansas City answered in the second quarter when Len Dawson threw a 7 yard touchdown pass to Curtis McClinton, tying the game at 7-7. Green Bay responded with a long drive capped by Jim Taylor's 14 yard touchdown run, the first rushing touchdown in Super Bowl history. A 31 yard field goal by Kansas City kicker Mike Mercer cut the lead to 14-10 at halftime. The AFL champion was not only still in the game, it had moved the ball well enough to suggest the second half would be a contest.

The contest ended almost as soon as the second half began. On Kansas City's first possession after halftime, Green Bay safety Willie Wood intercepted Dawson and returned the ball 50 yards to the Kansas City 5 yard line. Elijah Pitts scored on the next play, and the lead grew to 21-10. From that point, Green Bay's defense took control completely. Kansas City did not score again. Green Bay added a second touchdown pass from Starr to McGee, this one for 13 yards, and a final touchdown on a 1 yard run by Pitts. The final score, 35-10, was built on 21 unanswered points in the second half.

The turning point was therefore not a single spectacular play in isolation. It was the sequence that began with Wood's interception and continued through Green Bay's adjustments on defense. In the first half, Kansas City had shown it could design plays and execute them against an NFL defense. In the second half, Green Bay's pass rush and coverage removed that comfort. Dawson, who had been effective early, was sacked repeatedly and forced into hurried throws.

Starr was named the Most Valuable Player after completing 16 of 23 passes for 250 yards and two touchdowns. Starr kept the offense steady while the defense closed the game out. McGee, with seven catches for 138 yards and two touchdowns, was the unexpected star, and Pitts, with two rushing touchdowns, gave Green Bay the short yardage finishes its drives needed. For Kansas City, Dawson finished with 211 passing yards and one touchdown, but the second half interception and the pressure that followed defined his afternoon.

The finish also exposed how new the whole event was. The Coliseum did not sell out. The Green Bay Packers' own account notes that more than 30,000 seats remained empty. The game was shown on two networks at once, CBS and NBC, and the second half kickoff had to be redone because NBC was still in a commercial break when the first kickoff took place. In 1967, the production was still catching up with the idea.

The legacy of the game is larger than the margin. Green Bay's win confirmed, for the moment, the NFL's claim to superiority, and Lombardi's Packers went on to win Super Bowl II as well. Two years later, the New York Jets won Super Bowl III. The year after that, Kansas City itself won Super Bowl IV, beating the Minnesota Vikings 23-7. The leagues merged fully in 1970, and the championship trophy was renamed for Lombardi on September 10, 1970, after his death. The game that began as an awkward, half empty experiment in Los Angeles became the annual championship of a single league.

Scoring timeline

  1. First quarter, Green Bay 7-0: Starr to McGee for 37 yards, the first touchdown in Super Bowl history, after McGee replaced the injured Dowler.
  2. Second quarter, tied 7-7: Dawson to McClinton for 7 yards, completing a 66 yard Kansas City drive and showing the AFL champion could answer.
  3. Second quarter, Green Bay 14-7: Taylor's 14 yard touchdown run, the first rushing touchdown in Super Bowl history, after Starr converted four straight third downs on the drive.
  4. Second quarter, Green Bay 14-10: Mercer's 31 yard field goal, which kept Kansas City within four points at halftime.
  5. Third quarter, Green Bay 21-10: Wood's 50 yard interception return to the Kansas City 5, followed immediately by Pitts' 5 yard touchdown run. This was the turning point.
  6. Third quarter, Green Bay 28-10: Starr to McGee for 13 yards, extending the lead and confirming that Kansas City's defense could not stop Green Bay's passing game in the second half.
  7. Second half, no Kansas City score: Kansas City was shut out for the entire second half.
  8. Fourth quarter, Green Bay 35-10: Pitts' 1 yard touchdown run, the final score of the game.

Notable commercials

A 30 second commercial cost $42,000 on both CBS and NBC for this game.

BrandConceptWhy it is remembered
Goodyear"When There's No Man Around": a woman is stranded at night after her car tire fails, and the narrator says, "This flat tire needs a man," then adds that when there is no man around, Goodyear should be. The spot promoted the Goodyear Double Edge Tire, described as "A Tire in a Tire."It is the most widely cited surviving Super Bowl I commercial, and it is now discussed as an example of how early Super Bowl advertising spoke almost entirely to men. A Smithsonian account of the earliest Super Bowl commercials quotes the spot and notes that advertisers were not yet expecting women to watch.

Other advertisers reported for the game included Ford, Chrysler, RCA, RJ Reynolds Tobacco, McDonald's, and Budweiser, among others.

Why this game mattered

This game mattered because it turned a business agreement into a public contest. The NFL and AFL had agreed to merge, but agreement on paper did not settle which league played better football. This game was the first place that question was answered on the field, in front of a national audience on two networks at once. Green Bay's win let the NFL keep its claim to superiority for two more years, but Kansas City's competitive first half, and the AFL wins that followed in Super Bowls III and IV, showed that the merger was not a takeover by one league of a weaker rival. It was the start of a single league.

The game also mattered because of what it was not yet. It was not a sellout, it was not a polished television spectacle, and its halftime show was marching bands, a jazz trumpeter, a drill team, and, in contemporary accounts, jet pack flyers, balloons, and pigeons, rather than a pop headliner. The second half kickoff had to be redone for television, an early sign that broadcast needs would shape the event. The trophy Green Bay won was then called the World Professional Football Championship Trophy. It was renamed the Vince Lombardi Trophy in 1970. The distance between that afternoon in a half empty Coliseum and the modern Super Bowl is the story of how a championship game became a national holiday, and this game is where that story starts.
