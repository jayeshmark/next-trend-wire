Super Bowl XXIX: San Francisco 49ers vs San Diego Chargers

By Editorial Team

Result in brief: San Francisco won its fifth Super Bowl title, the first team to reach five, behind Steve Young's Super Bowl record 6 touchdown passes. San Diego was playing in the only Super Bowl appearance in franchise history.

The game story

Steve Young had waited years for this game, and he played it as if he had no intention of waiting any longer. Young had replaced Joe Montana in San Francisco, had won league most valuable player awards, and had still heard the same question every January: could he win the big game? Montana had won four Super Bowls. Young had won none as a starter. Against San Diego, in Miami, Young answered the question in the first five minutes.

San Francisco took the opening kickoff and scored in three plays. Young threw a 44 yard touchdown pass to Jerry Rice only 1 minute and 24 seconds into the game. On the next San Francisco possession, Young threw a 51 yard touchdown pass to Ricky Watters, and the score was 14 to 0 before San Diego had found its footing. The 49ers needed only seven offensive plays to score twice. Young threw for 112 yards in the opening minutes and ran for 21 more, and the game already felt decided.

San Diego did fight back once. The Chargers put together their best drive of the game, 13 plays for 78 yards over more than seven minutes, and Natrone Means ran 1 yard for a touchdown to cut the lead to 14 to 7. San Francisco answered with a 70 yard drive and a 5 yard touchdown pass from Young to William Floyd. Young threw his fourth touchdown pass of the half, 8 yards to Watters, and the halftime score was 28 to 10 after a San Diego field goal. Young had already tied the Super Bowl record for touchdown passes in a half, and the second half was a formality with highlights.

Watters ran 9 yards for a touchdown in the third quarter, and Rice caught a 15 yard touchdown pass from Young for a 42 to 10 lead. Coleman returned the ensuing kickoff 98 yards for a San Diego touchdown, with a two point conversion, but Rice caught his third touchdown pass of the game, 7 yards from Young, and the lead was 49 to 18. San Diego scored once more, on a 30 yard pass from Humphries to Tony Martin with a two point conversion, for the final score of 49 to 26.

Young finished with 6 touchdown passes, breaking Montana's Super Bowl record of 5. Rice caught 3 of them and finished with 10 catches for 149 yards. Watters scored 3 touchdowns in total, two receiving and one rushing. San Francisco gained 455 yards and scored 7 touchdowns. The combined total of 75 points broke the previous Super Bowl record of 69, set two years earlier in Dallas against Buffalo.

For San Francisco, the win was a fifth Super Bowl title, the first team to reach five, and it gave Young the championship that had eluded him. For San Diego, the loss was still the only Super Bowl appearance in franchise history. The Chargers had reached Miami as heavy underdogs after upsetting Pittsburgh in the AFC Championship Game, and they played like a team that had already exceeded expectations. The gap in talent was plain from the opening drive, and no single play explains this game. Young was simply better than everyone else on the field, and Rice and Watters made sure San Diego could not focus on stopping only one of them.

Cultural context

The halftime show was the strangest of the decade, and it is still the one people describe first when they talk about this Super Bowl. Disney turned the field into a live Indiana Jones adventure, complete with a stolen Lombardi Trophy, a temple, a nightclub, fire, and skydivers. It was advertising as entertainment, built to sell a theme park ride, and it showed how far the halftime show had traveled from a marching band performance. The game itself marked the peak of the 49ers dynasty. San Francisco had won five Super Bowls in fourteen years, and Young had finally stepped out of Montana's shadow. The team would remain competitive for a few more years, but it never won another Super Bowl in that era. San Diego, meanwhile, became a footnote in a record breaking game, remembered more for reaching Miami at all than for anything that happened once the game began.
