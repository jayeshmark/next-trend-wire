Decisive defensive plays

  • Buffalo defensive end Bruce Smith sacked Jeff Hostetler in the end zone for a safety in the second quarter, giving Buffalo a 12 to 3 lead.
  • New York cornerback Everson Walls pushed James Lofton out of bounds at the New York 8 yard line after Lofton's 61 yard catch in the first quarter. Buffalo settled for a field goal on that drive instead of a touchdown.
  • New York linebacker Cornelius Bennett broke up a third down pass in the fourth quarter with New York stopped at the Buffalo 3 yard line. New York then kicked the field goal that provided the final 20 to 19 margin.
  • New York defensive coordinator Bill Belichick used extra defensive backs and a physical approach against Buffalo's no huddle offense, aiming to take away deep passes while accepting yardage on the ground. His game plan is now held by the Pro Football Hall of Fame.

The game story

Buffalo arrived in Tampa as the team built to score in bursts. The Bills had finished the regular season 13 and 3, led the league in points scored with 428, and had beaten the Los Angeles Raiders 51 to 3 in the AFC Championship Game. Their no huddle attack, known as the K-Gun, was designed to keep a defense from substituting and to turn a small opening into a large gain. Jim Kelly ran it with confidence, Thurman Thomas gave it balance, and Andre Reed and James Lofton gave it speed. Buffalo was playing in its first Super Bowl, and it was favored by seven points.

New York arrived with the opposite identity. The Giants had also finished 13 and 3, but they had allowed a league low 211 points in the regular season. Their defense, led by Lawrence Taylor, was the backbone of the team. Their offense was built to control the ball and to make an opponent earn every yard. The problem was at quarterback. Phil Simms, who had led New York to its first Super Bowl title four years earlier, had broken his foot late in the season. Jeff Hostetler, his backup, had to carry the offense on the largest stage in American football. That contrast shaped how most people framed the game before kickoff.

Buffalo took control in the second quarter. Don Smith scored on a one yard run to put Buffalo ahead 10 to 3. Moments later, Bruce Smith sacked Hostetler in the end zone for a safety, and the lead grew to 12 to 3. New York did not lose its structure or start chasing points. Hostetler led an 87 yard drive in 10 plays just before halftime, and with 25 seconds left in the half he threw a 14 yard touchdown pass to Stephen Baker. The score cut Buffalo's lead to 12 to 10. A nine point deficit would have felt very different from a two point deficit, especially for a team whose plan depended on staying close and controlling the clock in the second half.

New York received the second half kickoff and produced the drive that best explains this victory. The Giants went 75 yards in 14 plays, used 9 minutes and 29 seconds of clock, and finished with a one yard touchdown run by Ottis Anderson. It was the longest drive by time in Super Bowl history to that point, and it gave New York a 17 to 12 lead, its first lead since the opening minutes. Anderson was the right runner for that kind of football. He ran with power, he fell forward, and he made Buffalo's defenders tackle him again and again. He finished with 102 yards on 21 carries, and he was named the game's Most Valuable Player.

Buffalo still had the speed to answer, and it did so at the start of the fourth quarter. Thomas broke free for a 31 yard touchdown run, and Buffalo led again, 19 to 17. That run showed the cost of New York's plan. By choosing to defend the pass first, New York had left room for Thomas, who finished with 135 rushing yards and 190 total yards from scrimmage. But New York had decided before kickoff that Thomas running well was a price it could pay if it stopped Buffalo from scoring quickly through the air. New York answered with another long drive, 74 yards in 14 plays using 7 minutes and 32 seconds, and Matt Bahr kicked a 21 yard field goal for a 20 to 19 lead. Buffalo then had one last chance from its own 10 yard line with 2 minutes and 16 seconds left. Kelly moved the Bills to the New York 29 yard line, but Scott Norwood's 47 yard field goal attempt with eight seconds left sailed wide right, and New York ran out the clock.

The final margin, one point, remains the smallest winning margin in Super Bowl history. It was also the first Super Bowl in which neither team committed a turnover. New York did not win by forcing mistakes. It won by limiting Buffalo's possessions, by making Buffalo work for every score, and by trusting a backup quarterback and a veteran running back to execute a narrow plan under pressure. For New York, it was a second Super Bowl title in five years, and a validation of Bill Parcells, defensive coordinator Bill Belichick, and a defense first roster. Belichick's game plan became a coaching document studied long after the night, and it helped launch the head coaching careers of Belichick and of receivers coach Tom Coughlin. For Buffalo, the loss was the first of four consecutive Super Bowl defeats, a run no other team has matched. This was Buffalo's best chance in that run, a game it led in the second quarter, led again in the fourth quarter, and lost on a kick that missed by less than a yard. That is why the game is still remembered by a phrase rather than by a score: wide right.

Scoring timeline

  1. New York strikes first. Bahr's 28 yard field goal gives New York a 3 to 0 lead and rewards a long opening drive.
  2. Buffalo answers fast. Kelly's 61 yard pass to Lofton sets up Norwood's 23 yard field goal, but Walls stops Lofton short of the end zone, so Buffalo ties rather than takes the lead on a touchdown.
  3. Buffalo takes control. Don Smith's 1 yard touchdown run caps a 12 play, 80 yard drive and puts Buffalo ahead 10 to 3.
  4. Buffalo adds a safety. Bruce Smith sacks Hostetler in the end zone, extending the lead to 12 to 3 and putting New York's backup quarterback under severe pressure.
  5. New York answers before halftime. Hostetler's 14 yard touchdown pass to Baker, with 25 seconds left in the half, cuts the lead to 12 to 10 and shifts momentum.
  6. New York owns the third quarter. Anderson's 1 yard touchdown run caps a 14 play, 75 yard drive lasting 9 minutes and 29 seconds, giving New York a 17 to 12 lead.
  7. Buffalo retakes the lead. Thomas scores on a 31 yard run at the start of the fourth quarter, putting Buffalo ahead 19 to 17.
  8. New York retakes the lead for good. Bahr's 21 yard field goal caps a 14 play, 74 yard drive lasting 7 minutes and 32 seconds, making it 20 to 19.
  9. Buffalo's last chance misses. Kelly drives Buffalo to the New York 29 yard line in the final minutes, but Norwood's 47 yard field goal attempt with eight seconds left goes wide right, and New York runs out the clock.

Why this game mattered

This game was played ten days into the Persian Gulf War, while Operation Desert Storm was underway. That context shaped almost everything around the football. Security was heightened, fans waved American flags in the stands, and the pregame program carried a weight that a normal championship game does not carry. Whitney Houston's performance of "The Star-Spangled Banner," backed by the Florida Orchestra, became one of the most famous anthem performances in American sports history. It was later released as a single, and it was reissued after the September 11 attacks in 2001.

The halftime story fits the same moment. A light, Disney produced celebration was set aside on live television for a news report on the war, which showed how quickly a sporting event could become secondary to events overseas. The game itself then gave the country a different kind of shared experience: a close, well played contest with no turnovers, decided in the final seconds.

The result also reshaped two franchises. New York confirmed a model based on defense, ball control, and coaching preparation, and the Belichick game plan became a reference point for coaches facing a fast, pass first offense. Buffalo began a run that still defines the franchise in national memory: four straight Super Bowl appearances, four defeats, starting with the closest defeat of all. The phrase wide right entered sports language as shorthand for a championship lost by the smallest possible margin, and it has stayed attached to Buffalo long after the details of the drives that led to that kick have faded.
