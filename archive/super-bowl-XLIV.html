Super Bowl XLIV: New Orleans Saints vs Indianapolis Colts

By Editorial Team

New Orleans won its first Super Bowl title in its first appearance, beating Indianapolis 31-17 after trailing 10-0 in the first quarter. The Saints scored 18 unanswered points in the fourth quarter, sealed by Tracy Porter's 74 yard interception return touchdown.

The game story

The stakes were about history on one side and confirmation on the other. New Orleans had never played in a Super Bowl in 43 seasons, and the Saints carried the hopes of a city still rebuilding after Hurricane Katrina. Indianapolis had been here before, had won Super Bowl XLI three years earlier, and had gone 14-2 behind Peyton Manning, who was chasing a second title. Both teams were the top seeds in their conferences, the first time since Super Bowl XXVIII that the two number one seeds had met in the game. The matchup of quarterbacks, Drew Brees against Manning, promised a shootout.

The first quarter suggested a Colts win. Matt Stover kicked a 38 yard field goal, then Manning found Pierre Garcon for a 19 yard touchdown, and Indianapolis led 10-0 after one quarter. New Orleans had no points and no rhythm, and the Saints offense looked a step slow against the Colts defense. The second quarter was quieter but important: Garrett Hartley kicked field goals of 46 and 44 yards, and the halftime score was Indianapolis 10, New Orleans 6. The Saints had not scored a touchdown, but they had stopped the bleeding, and they had a plan for the second half that nobody in the stadium saw coming.

The plan arrived on the opening kickoff of the third quarter. Instead of kicking deep, New Orleans attempted a surprise onside kick, recovered by Chris Reis near midfield. The gamble, called "Ambush" by the Saints, gave New Orleans the ball to start the half, and the Saints made it pay. Brees found Pierre Thomas for a 16 yard touchdown, and New Orleans led for the first time at 13-10. Indianapolis answered with a 4 yard touchdown run by Joseph Addai, retaking the lead at 17-13. Hartley then kicked a 47 yard field goal, his third of the night, and the third quarter ended with Indianapolis ahead by a single point, 17-16.

The fourth quarter belonged entirely to New Orleans. Indianapolis drove into scoring range but Stover missed a 51 yard field goal with 10:44 left, a miss that kept the margin at one point instead of four. New Orleans took over and drove the other way. Brees found Jeremy Shockey for a 2 yard touchdown with 5:46 left, and then, on the two point conversion, Brees found Lance Moore, giving the Saints a 24-17 lead. Indianapolis still had time and still had Manning, but the next possession ended the game. Manning threw toward Reggie Wayne, and Tracy Porter stepped in front of the pass at the New Orleans 26 and ran 74 yards for a touchdown with 3:24 left. Hartley's extra point made it 31-17, and the Colts never threatened again.

The legacy of the game starts with the onside kick. Coaches still cite "Ambush" as the boldest call in Super Bowl history, not because onside kicks are rare but because of when it came: trailing at halftime, to open the second half, in a game the Saints were expected to lose. Sean Payton's decision said that New Orleans would not wait for the game to come to them, and the recovery changed the field position and the psychology of the second half at once. For Brees, the MVP award capped a performance of remarkable accuracy: 32 completions on 39 attempts, tying a Super Bowl record set by Tom Brady in Super Bowl XXXVIII, with two touchdowns and no interceptions. For the Saints, the win was the franchise's first championship, and it arrived for a city that had waited through decades of losing seasons and then through a disaster. The final image of the night was not a single play but a celebration: a franchise and a city that had never won anything, finally winning everything, in one night.

Why this game mattered

Super Bowl XLIV mattered because of where it was won and who won it. The Saints were not just a team seeking a first title. They were the team of a city that had been devastated by Hurricane Katrina four and a half years earlier, and whose stadium, the Superdome, had become a symbol of the disaster when it sheltered thousands of residents. A Saints title in that context was treated across the country as something larger than a sports result, and the celebrations in New Orleans after the game were covered as civic news, not just sports news.

The game also changed how coaches think about the opening kickoff of the second half. Payton's onside kick was not the first surprise onside kick in football history, but it was a bold surprise onside kick in a Super Bowl, trailing, with the season on the line, and it worked. Since then, the "Ambush" kick has become the standard example coaches cite when they argue for aggression over caution in championship games. The halftime show pointed in the opposite direction: The Who, with only two original members left, played a five song medley of hits from 1969 to 1978, the latest in the league's run of legacy rock acts after the 2004 halftime controversy. The contrast between a conservative halftime booking and a radical coaching gamble is, in miniature, the story of the night.

Finally, the game confirmed Brees as one of the era's defining quarterbacks. His 32 completions tied a Super Bowl record, and his zero interceptions against a Colts defense built to force mistakes showed a precision that became his signature. The Snickers commercial that won the Ad Meter that night, with Betty White playing backyard football, launched a campaign that outlasted the game itself, and is still quoted whenever a celebrity plays against type in a Super Bowl ad.
