Super Bowl XXIV: San Francisco 49ers vs Denver Broncos

By Editorial Team

Result in brief: San Francisco became only the second team to repeat as champion since the Green Bay Packers of the 1960s, tying Pittsburgh with four Super Bowl victories. San Francisco's 55 points and 45 point margin of victory were both Super Bowl records.

The game story

This game was billed as a duel between two great quarterbacks, and it turned into a coronation for one of them. San Francisco arrived in New Orleans as the defending champion, seeking to become the first team in a decade to repeat as Super Bowl winner. Under first year head coach George Seifert, who had taken over after Bill Walsh retired following Super Bowl XXIII, the 49ers had finished with a league best 14 and 2 record, losing their two games by a combined five points. Joe Montana had won the league's Most Valuable Player award, Jerry Rice had caught 17 touchdown passes, and Roger Craig had rushed for more than 1,000 yards. Denver arrived with an 11 and 5 record, seeking to avoid a fourth Super Bowl defeat, which would have tied Minnesota's record, and seeking to avenge two previous Super Bowl losses to NFC teams. John Elway, brilliant in the AFC Championship Game against Cleveland, was the Broncos' hope. San Francisco was favored by twelve points, one of the largest spreads in Super Bowl history, and the game followed the spread almost exactly.

San Francisco scored on its first drive, and never trailed, never tied after that drive, and never looked threatened. Montana threw a 20 yard touchdown pass to Rice early in the first quarter, Denver answered with a 42 yard David Treadwell field goal, and then Montana found tight end Brent Jones for a 7 yard touchdown pass, although Mike Cofer missed the extra point, leaving the score at 13 to 3 after the first quarter. The second quarter was a clinic. San Francisco embarked on a 14 play, 7 minute drive built around fullback Tom Rathman, who caught passes, ran the ball, and finally scored on a 1 yard run to make it 20 to 3. Just before halftime, Montana found Rice again, this time for 38 yards, and San Francisco led 27 to 3 at the break. Denver had managed only a field goal in thirty minutes, and its offense had been stopped, sacked, and intercepted with a regularity that suggested the second half would be a formality.

It was. Rice caught his third touchdown pass, a 28 yard throw from Montana, early in the third quarter, and John Taylor caught a 35 yard touchdown pass minutes later, making it 41 to 3. Denver finally scored a touchdown when Elway ran 3 yards into the end zone, after a pass interference call had given the Broncos the ball near the San Francisco goal line, making it 41 to 10. That was Denver's last score. In the fourth quarter, Rathman scored again on a 3 yard run, and Craig added a 1 yard touchdown run, and the final score was 55 to 10. Montana finished 22 of 29 for 297 yards and five touchdown passes, a Super Bowl record at the time, with no interceptions, and was named MVP for the third time in his career. Rice finished with seven catches for 148 yards and three touchdowns, numbers that would have won the MVP award in almost any other game. Elway finished with 108 passing yards, two interceptions, and six sacks, in what was, to that point, the lowest passing output of his career.

The records fell in bunches. San Francisco's 55 points were the most ever scored by one team in a Super Bowl, its 45 point margin was the largest ever, and its eight touchdowns were the most ever. Montana's five touchdown passes set a single game Super Bowl record, and his third MVP award, in his fourth Super Bowl victory, put him alone at the top of the game's individual honors. San Francisco became only the second team to repeat as champion since the Green Bay Packers of the 1960s, and tied Pittsburgh with four Super Bowl victories. For Denver, the defeat was a fourth Super Bowl loss, tying Minnesota's record, and Elway fell to 0 and 3 in Super Bowls, a record that would haunt his reputation until his championships at the end of his career. The game is often ranked among the worst Super Bowls ever played, not because San Francisco played poorly, but because it played so well that the contest ended as a competition before halftime. It was, in the end, the most complete performance by a team in Super Bowl history to that point, and the final statement of the 49ers dynasty of the 1980s.

Why this game mattered

This game closed the 1980s, and it closed them with the decade's defining team at its peak. The 49ers of the 1980s won four Super Bowls in nine seasons, produced two of the greatest players in league history in Montana and Rice, and changed how offenses were designed. Super Bowl XXIV was their most complete performance, and it came under a new head coach, proving the system was larger than any one man, even Walsh. The halftime show, pairing New Orleans jazz with Peanuts characters, was the last of an era in which halftime was a themed pageant rather than a pop concert, an era that would end three years later with Michael Jackson. For Denver, the game became the low point of a proud franchise's history, the fourth defeat that tied a record no team wants, and the starting point for a long climb back to championship contention. The television audience, about 73.85 million viewers, was the smallest for a Super Bowl since 1969, a reminder that even the largest event in American television depends on a competitive game to hold its audience.
