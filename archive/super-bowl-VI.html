The game story

Dallas came to New Orleans with a reputation to erase. A year earlier, the Cowboys had lost Super Bowl V on a last second field goal, and the talk around Tom Landry's team was that it could win plenty of games but not the one that mattered most. Miami came in as the new power in the AFC, a young team built by Don Shula around a strong running game and a fast defense, playing in its first Super Bowl. The matchup looked even on paper. It was not even on the field.

Dallas scored first and never trailed, but the first half was closer than the final score suggests. Mike Clark kicked a 9 yard field goal on Dallas's opening drive, and the Cowboys spent much of the half grinding out yards on the ground while Miami's defense bent without breaking. Miami had a chance to tie it early in the second quarter, but Garo Yepremian missed a 49 yard field goal attempt. Dallas then put together its best drive of the half, 76 yards in 10 plays, and Roger Staubach threw a 7 yard touchdown pass to Lance Alworth for a 10 to 0 lead. Miami answered just before halftime, moving 44 yards in four plays so Yepremian could kick a 31 yard field goal with four seconds left. Dallas led 10 to 3 at the break, and the game still felt open.

It did not stay open for long. Dallas took the second half kickoff and drove 71 yards in 8 plays, almost entirely on the ground, and Duane Thomas scored on a 3 yard run to make it 17 to 3. That drive set the tone for everything that followed. Dallas had run inside successfully in the first half, so Miami adjusted to stop the inside run, and Dallas simply ran outside instead. Miami's offense, meanwhile, could not get started at all. The Dolphins did not earn a first down in the third quarter, and their longest advance in that period reached only their own 42 yard line. Shula later described his offense in that stretch as destroyed, and the numbers backed him up: Miami ran only 44 offensive plays all game to Dallas's 69.

The final Dallas touchdown came from a mistake Miami could not afford. Early in the fourth quarter, with Miami driving toward midfield, Griese threw toward Jim Kiick and Howley stepped in front of it, returning the interception 41 yards before tripping untouched at the Miami 9. Three plays later, Staubach threw a 7 yard touchdown pass to Mike Ditka, and the score was 24 to 3. Miami drove to the Dallas 16 on its next possession, but Griese fumbled the snap and Dallas recovered, and the game was effectively over.

Staubach's line was modest by modern standards, 119 passing yards, but it told only part of his game. He had taken over as starter midway through the season after Landry alternated him with Craig Morton, and he then led Dallas to 10 straight wins, capped by this one. He was the first Heisman Trophy winner to win Super Bowl MVP. For Dallas, the win ended the talk about not winning the big game and gave the franchise its first championship. For Miami, the loss was the only defeat the team would suffer in the calendar year 1972, because the Dolphins went undefeated the following season and won Super Bowl VII. In that sense, Super Bowl VI was both an ending and a beginning: the end of Dallas's doubt, and the start of Miami's run to perfection.

Why this game mattered

Super Bowl VI confirmed the Super Bowl as a national television event in its own right, with an estimated 56.64 million viewers on CBS. It also gave the Dallas Cowboys the first championship in franchise history, a win that turned Landry's carefully built team from a nearly team into a champion and set up the Cowboys as one of the defining franchises of the decade. The halftime tribute to Louis Armstrong, who had died the previous summer, gave the broadcast a New Orleans flavor that fit the host city. And the game sits in history as the hinge between two famous runs: it closed Dallas's search for a first title, and it was the last loss before Miami's perfect season. No team since has prevented its Super Bowl opponent from scoring a touchdown, which keeps this Dallas defensive performance standing alone.
