The game story

Green Bay arrived in Miami as the defending champion, but not as the same comfortable power that had won the first AFL NFL championship game a year earlier. The Packers had finished the regular season 9 wins, 4 losses, and 1 tie. Both starting running backs from the previous season were gone, and the replacements brought in to fill those roles had also been lost to season ending injuries. Coach Vince Lombardi was left to piece together a running game from second year reserve Donny Anderson, rookie Travis Williams, and free agent fullbacks Chuck Mercein and Ben Wilson. Quarterback Bart Starr, 33 years old, had missed four games with injuries and had thrown nearly twice as many interceptions as touchdown passes during the season. The defense, though, remained the spine of the team, allowing only 209 points in the regular season, with a secondary led by Willie Wood, Herb Adderley, and Bob Jeter.

The road to Miami had already given Green Bay its most famous win of the season. In the NFL Championship Game, played in brutal cold at Lambeau Field, Green Bay beat Dallas 21 to 17 in the game remembered as the Ice Bowl, with Starr scoring the winning touchdown in the closing seconds. That win explained why Green Bay was still favored, and why the talk around this game was less about whether Green Bay would win than about whether Lombardi would retire after it.

Oakland arrived with the better regular season record and the louder offense. The Raiders had gone 13 and 1, led all teams in scoring with 468 points, and had beaten Houston 40 to 7 in the AFL Championship Game. Lamonica had thrown 30 touchdown passes, and a defense nicknamed the 11 Angry Men had made life hard for every AFL opponent. Yet most writers and fans still treated the NFL as the stronger league, and Oakland was cast as the challenger that had to prove the AFL belonged on the same field.

Green Bay settled that question early, and then never let it reopen. The Packers scored on each of their first three offensive possessions. Chandler kicked a 39 yard field goal on the opening drive for a 3 to 0 lead. A long drive early in the second quarter stalled at the Oakland 13 yard line, and Chandler added a 20 yard field goal for a 6 to 0 lead. Less than two minutes later, Starr found Boyd Dowler wide open down the middle for a 62 yard touchdown, and the lead was 13 to 0. Oakland finally answered when Lamonica hit Bill Miller for a 23 yard touchdown, cutting the lead to 13 to 7, but Green Bay got the points back before halftime. After a fumbled punt return by Oakland gave Green Bay the ball back, Starr completed a short pass to Dowler to set up Chandler's third field goal, a 43 yard kick as time expired in the half, for a 16 to 7 lead.

The second half removed any remaining doubt. Green Bay held the ball for almost the entire third quarter. On the Packers second drive of the half, Starr completed a 35 yard pass to Max McGee, the final catch of McGee's career, and then found Anderson near the goal line before Anderson scored on a 2 yard run for a 23 to 7 lead. Chandler then kicked his fourth field goal, a 31 yard kick that hit the crossbar and bounced over, for a 26 to 7 lead. In the fourth quarter, after Starr was knocked out of the game with a jammed thumb on his throwing hand, Adderley stepped in front of a pass intended for Biletnikoff and returned it 60 yards for a touchdown, making it 33 to 7. Oakland scored once more, on a second 23 yard touchdown pass from Lamonica to Miller, but that only made the final score look closer than the game had felt.

The final margin was 19 points, and the shape of the game was control rather than drama. Green Bay ran the ball 41 times, protected it, kicked field goals when drives stalled, and let its defense punish Oakland's mistakes. Starr, named Most Valuable Player for the second straight year, completed 13 of 24 passes for 202 yards and one touchdown. Chandler's 4 field goals were the quiet engine of the win, turning stalled drives into points.

For Green Bay, the win completed a third straight championship and closed the Lombardi era in Green Bay. Lombardi was carried off the field by his players, and this was his last game as Packers coach, his ninth straight playoff victory. It was also the final game for McGee, for guard Fuzzy Thurston, and for Chandler. For Oakland, the loss was a reminder of how far the AFL still had to travel, even with a 13 and 1 record and the highest scoring offense in either league. That gap would close fast, first with the Jets a year later and then with Kansas City. But on this January afternoon in Miami, the old order still held.

Why this game mattered

This game is remembered less for suspense than for endings. It was Lombardi's last game as Green Bay coach, and the last game for several players who had defined the Packers dynasty, including McGee and Chandler. Green Bay's third straight championship closed an era in which one coach, one quarterback, and one defense had set the standard for professional football. The Packers would not win another championship for nearly three decades, which only sharpened how this game came to be seen: not as one more win, but as the final act of a dynasty.

The game also sits at a hinge point in league history. It was played when the AFL and NFL were still separate leagues meeting only in this championship game, and when most of the football world still assumed the NFL team would win. Green Bay's comfortable win reinforced that assumption for one more year. Within two years, that assumption would be gone, first with the Jets in Super Bowl III and then with Kansas City in Super Bowl IV. Super Bowl II is therefore the last comfortable win of the old order, played in the same Miami stadium that would host the upset a year later.

The broadcast itself has its own odd legacy. The CBS telecast is treated as lost, with only photographs and NFL Films footage surviving in the accounts checked, and part of the country lost the video feed during the second quarter and again at halftime because of a breakdown in telephone company lines. For a game that closed so much history, very little of the live broadcast itself remains to watch.
