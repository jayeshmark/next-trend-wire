Super Bowl XLVI: New York Giants vs New England Patriots

By Editorial Team

New York won its fourth Super Bowl title, beating New England 21-17 on an Ahmad Bradshaw touchdown with 1:04 left. The game was a rematch of Super Bowl XLII, also won by New York, 17-14, four years earlier.

The game story

The stakes were about a rematch that nobody expected to happen again. Four years earlier, in Super Bowl XLII, the Giants had ended New England's perfect season with a 17-14 upset built on a miracle catch and a relentless pass rush. Now the same two teams met again, but the roles had shifted. New England was 13-3, the top seed in the AFC, with Tom Brady chasing a fourth title. New York was 9-7, a team that had needed to win its final regular season game just to make the playoffs, and that had won three road playoff games to get to Indianapolis. Eli Manning, who had won the MVP award in XLII, was trying to prove that the first win was not a fluke.

The game started with a shock. On New England's first possession, Brady dropped back in his own end zone, was pressured by Justin Tuck, and threw the ball away. The officials ruled intentional grounding in the end zone, a safety, and New York led 2-0 less than three minutes into the game. The Giants extended the lead on their next drive, when Manning found Victor Cruz for a 2 yard touchdown, and New York led 9-0 at the end of the first quarter. New England had not scored, had not led, and looked rattled in a way the Patriots rarely do.

New England answered in the second quarter. Stephen Gostkowski kicked a 29 yard field goal, and then, with 15 seconds left in the half, Brady found Danny Woodhead for a 4 yard touchdown. The extra point gave New England a 10-9 lead at halftime, its first lead of the night, and the game had the shape of a classic: two quarters of Giants pressure, answered by two quarters of Patriots patience. The third quarter tilted it further toward New England. Brady found Aaron Hernandez for a 12 yard touchdown, and the Patriots led 17-9. Lawrence Tynes answered with field goals of 38 and 33 yards for New York, and the third quarter ended with New England ahead 17-15. The Giants were within two points, but they had scored no touchdown since the first quarter.

The fourth quarter turned on two drives, one that failed and one that did not. New England took over with about nine minutes left, leading 17-15, and drove to midfield. On third down, Brady threw deep to Wes Welker, and the pass fell incomplete. New England punted, and New York took over at its own 12 yard line with 3:53 left, trailing by two points, needing a field goal to win.

What followed was one of the great final drives in Super Bowl history. On the first play, Manning threw deep down the left sideline to Mario Manningham, who caught the ball for 38 yards and stayed in bounds with both feet as he was pushed out. New England challenged the ruling, and the call stood. Manning kept moving the Giants: 16 yards to Manningham, 14 yards to Hakeem Nicks, and a series of short gains that brought New York to the New England six yard line with just over a minute left. On second down, Ahmad Bradshaw ran up the middle for a 6 yard touchdown with 1:04 left. The two point conversion attempt failed, so the lead was 21-17, and New England had 57 seconds and one timeout to answer.

New England could not. Brady moved the Patriots to midfield, but the drive stalled, and his final pass, a deep throw toward Aaron Hernandez with five seconds left, fell incomplete. The clock ran out on a 21-17 Giants win.

The legacy of the game is that it closed the book on the Patriots' dynasty era in a way no other loss did. New England had now lost two Super Bowls to the same team, by a combined seven points, and Brady's record in Super Bowls fell to 3-2. For Eli Manning, the second MVP award put him in rare company: only the third quarterback in a row to win the award, after Drew Brees and Aaron Rodgers, and the first to beat Brady twice on the game's biggest stage. For the Giants, the 9-7 regular season record reframed what a championship run could look like. The Manningham catch, upheld on review on the first play of the final drive, joined the Helmet Catch from XLII as the two plays that define the rivalry: two sideline catches, four years apart, both made by Giants receivers against the Patriots, both surviving a challenge or a review, both leading to the winning touchdown.

Why this game mattered

Super Bowl XLVI mattered because it was a rematch, and rematches in the Super Bowl are rare enough that the story wrote itself. The XLII upset had been treated as a once in a generation event: an 18-0 team losing to a wild card team on a miracle catch. When the same two teams met again four years later, the question was whether the first result had been luck. The Giants answered it by winning again, by four points instead of three, with another sideline catch on the final drive. Two wins over the same dynasty, by a combined seven points, turned a fluke into a matchup problem: New England's offense, built on timing and precision, twice failed to finish against a Giants pass rush that arrived late and stayed late.

The halftime show made its own news. Madonna's set, with LMFAO, Nicki Minaj, M.I.A., and CeeLo Green, drew a reported 114 million viewers, but the night's most reported moment was an unplanned one: M.I.A. gave the middle finger to the camera during "Give Me All Your Luvin'", a gesture the broadcast delay failed to blur, which led to a public apology from NBC and a long legal dispute between the NFL and the performer. The incident was followed by tighter league control over halftime performances, and it is still cited whenever a live broadcast delay is discussed.

The commercials reflected a Super Bowl economy at full stretch. A 30 second slot cost $3.5 million, up from $3 million the year before, and 30 percent of the spots ran 60 seconds or longer, a shift toward longer, more emotional storytelling. Chrysler's "Halftime in America" spot, narrated by Clint Eastwood, was criticized by some as political and praised by others as the night's best writing, and it showed that the serious, civic minded car ad pioneered the year before had become a genre of its own. The Giants' win, meanwhile, gave the postseason a new template: a 9-7 team that got hot in January could now point to Indianapolis and say that the regular season record did not matter, only the last four games did.
