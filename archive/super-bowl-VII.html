The game story

Miami arrived in Los Angeles with a perfect record and almost nobody willing to believe in it. The Dolphins had gone 14 and 0 in the regular season, then beaten Cleveland and Pittsburgh in the playoffs, yet they were one point underdogs against Washington. The reasons were easy to list: Miami had lost the previous Super Bowl to Dallas, its schedule had been soft, and its starting quarterback, Bob Griese, had broken his leg during the season and only recently returned. Washington, coached by George Allen and built around veteran players nicknamed the Over the Hill Gang, had beaten Green Bay and Dallas impressively in the playoffs and looked like the tougher, more tested team.

The first half did not follow that script at all. Miami scored on its first real chance, when Griese threw a 28 yard touchdown pass to Howard Twilley late in the first quarter. Then, just before halftime, Jim Kiick ran 1 yard for a touchdown to make it 14 to 0. The Dolphins were not dominating the statistics. Larry Csonka was running well, with 112 yards on the day, but Miami finished with only 253 total yards to Washington's 228, and Washington actually held the ball longer. What Miami did was play almost mistake free football in the moments that mattered, while Washington kept wasting its chances. Billy Kilmer threw interceptions, drives stalled, and the Washington offense, which had looked so sharp in the playoffs, could not finish.

The second half became a slow exercise in frustration for Washington. Miami did not score again, and Washington kept getting the ball with time to come back, but every drive seemed to end the same way: a punt, a turnover, or a missed opportunity. The Dolphins defense, which had no single star big enough to name the unit after, simply kept Washington out of the end zone, quarter after quarter. By the fourth quarter, the shutout itself had become the story. Washington had not scored a point, and Miami's perfect season was less than two minutes from being complete.

Then came the play that nearly ruined everything, and that everyone remembers more than the game itself. With 2:07 left, Miami lined up for a 42 yard field goal that would have made it 17 to 0. The kick was blocked. Kicker Garo Yepremian picked up the loose ball, tried to throw a pass instead of falling on it, and batted the ball into the air. Washington cornerback Mike Bass caught it and ran 49 yards for a touchdown. Suddenly it was 14 to 7, Washington had the ball back on an onside kick attempt to worry about, and a perfect season that had lasted four months was in danger of collapsing in the final two minutes.

It did not collapse. Miami recovered, and Jake Scott intercepted Kilmer in the end zone and returned it 55 yards to seal the win. Scott's two interceptions made him the game's MVP, a fitting choice for a defense that had carried the team all season without a famous name. The final score, 14 to 7, made Miami 17 and 0, the first and still the only team in NFL history to finish a season undefeated and untied. The Dolphins did not get their White House visit at the time because of the Watergate scandal, and only made the trip in 2013. That delay fits the strange history of this team: the only perfect team in league history, and for decades the team that other unbeaten teams were measured against every autumn, until the last unbeaten team lost and the old Dolphins could pop their champagne again.

Why this game mattered

Super Bowl VII is remembered less for how Miami won than for what the win completed: the only perfect season in NFL history. Every unbeaten team since has been compared to the 1972 Dolphins, and every late season loss by an unbeaten team has revived the story. The game also gave football one of its most replayed bloopers in Yepremian's blocked kick, a play that is funny precisely because it almost cost a perfect season. The fact that Washington's only points came without its offense ever scoring, and that Miami won despite holding the ball for less time than Washington, makes the game a useful reminder that football is decided by points and turnovers, not by yardage or possession. The Dolphins' delayed White House visit in 2013, forty years later, closed a strange loop: a team that had been denied its celebration by a political scandal finally got it from a different president, with most of the roster present.
