Super Bowl XVII: Washington vs Miami Dolphins

By Editorial Team

Result in brief: Washington won its first Super Bowl title, behind John Riggins, who rushed for 166 yards on 38 carries, both Super Bowl records at the time, and was named MVP. Note on names: this page refers to the team as Washington throughout, following the current team name. Contemporary sources from 1983 use the team's former name.

The game story

This Super Bowl was played at the end of a season unlike any before it. A 57 day players' strike had cut the 1982 regular season from sixteen games to nine, and the league had replaced its normal playoff format with a sixteen team tournament in which division standings were ignored. Washington finished 8 and 1, the best record in the NFC, while Miami finished 7 and 2. Both teams had survived three playoff rounds to reach Pasadena, and the matchup was a rematch of Super Bowl VII ten years earlier, when Miami had completed its perfect 17 and 0 season at Washington's expense. Washington head coach Joe Gibbs, in only his second season, had built his team around an offensive line nicknamed the Hogs and a 33 year old fullback, John Riggins, who had returned to football after sitting out a season in a contract dispute. Miami head coach Don Shula, coaching in his fifth Super Bowl, had built his team around defense, with the Killer Bees unit allowing the fewest yards in the league.

For most of the first half, Miami's big plays beat Washington's steady progress. Jimmy Cefalo caught a 76 yard touchdown pass from David Woodley on only the second play of one Miami drive, giving Miami a 7 to 0 lead. Washington answered with a field goal and then a 4 yard touchdown pass from Theismann to Alvin Garrett, tying the game at 10 to 10 late in the second quarter. Thirteen seconds later, Miami was back in front: Fulton Walker returned the ensuing kickoff 98 yards for a touchdown, then the longest kickoff return in Super Bowl history, and Miami led 17 to 10 at halftime. The halftime numbers told a confusing story. Washington had controlled the ball and moved it consistently, yet trailed, because Miami's two touchdowns had come on a single long pass and a single long return. Washington's coaches could reasonably believe their plan was working. Miami's coaches could reasonably believe their team only needed one more big play.

The second half belonged entirely to Washington's running game, and to Riggins in particular. Mark Moseley kicked a 20 yard field goal in the third quarter to cut the lead to 17 to 13, and then came the sequence that saved Washington's season. With Miami leading by four and Washington backed up near its own goal line, Theismann's pass was tipped into the air, and Bokamper settled under it for what looked like a certain interception and a short field for Miami. Theismann himself raced over and knocked the ball away. Had Miami recovered there, it would have led by four with the ball deep in Washington territory late in the third quarter, and Washington's ground game would have been finished as a strategy. Instead Washington punted, got the ball back, and drove to the Miami 43 yard line early in the fourth quarter, facing fourth down and one yard to go.

What happened next is one of the most replayed plays in Super Bowl history. Theismann handed to Riggins, who ran left behind the Hogs, brushed off a tackle attempt by Miami cornerback Don McNeal with a stiff arm, and ran 43 yards for a touchdown. Washington led 20 to 17, and the Rose Bowl crowd of 103,667, the largest for a Super Bowl to that point, roared for a play that had been designed to gain a single yard. Riggins was not finished. On Washington's next possession he carried on eight of twelve plays, chewing nearly seven minutes off the clock, before Theismann found Charlie Brown for a 6 yard touchdown that made the final score 27 to 17. Miami's last drive ended on a failed fourth down conversion, and Washington ran out the clock.

Riggins finished with 166 yards on 38 carries, both Super Bowl records at the time, and was named MVP. His 181 total yards from scrimmage exceeded Miami's entire team total of 176 yards, only the second time in Super Bowl history that one player had outgained an entire opposing team. The win gave Washington its first Super Bowl title and its first NFL championship since 1942. For Miami, the defeat was the start of a long Super Bowl drought: the Dolphins would reach one more Super Bowl, two years later, and lose that one too. For Riggins, the run on fourth down became the defining image of his career, a 33 year old fullback who had once walked away from the game carrying his team to a championship one yard at a time, and then forty three yards all at once.

Scoring timeline

  1. Miami strikes deep. Cefalo's 76 yard touchdown catch gives Miami a 7 to 0 lead.
  2. Washington answers twice. Moseley's field goal and Garrett's touchdown catch tie the game at 10 to 10 late in the second quarter.
  3. Miami answers in thirteen seconds. Walker's 98 yard kickoff return restores a 17 to 10 lead at halftime.
  4. Washington closes the gap. Moseley's 20 yard field goal in the third quarter makes it 17 to 13.
  5. Theismann saves a drive. His knockdown of a tipped pass prevents a Miami interception deep in Washington territory.
  6. Riggins takes the lead. His 43 yard touchdown run on fourth down and one gives Washington a 20 to 17 lead.
  7. Washington seals it. Theismann's 6 yard touchdown pass to Brown, after a drive on which Riggins carried eight times, sets the final score at 27 to 17.

Why this game mattered

This game closed a season that tested whether professional football could survive a long labor stoppage and still produce a legitimate champion, and the answer, in front of more than 100,000 fans in Pasadena, was yes. It was also the first Super Bowl broadcast followed by a major network lead out program launch, as NBC premiered The A-Team immediately after the game, beginning a tradition of using the Super Bowl as a launching pad for new television. On the field, the game became a referendum on two ways to build a team: Miami's defense and big plays against Washington's offensive line and grinding running game, and the grinding game won, decisively, in the second half. Riggins' fourth down run entered the sport's visual language, replayed every January as an example of a coach trusting his best player in the game's biggest moment. The strike shortened season also gave the game a strange footnote: Washington's Mark Moseley had been named the league's Most Valuable Player for the regular season, the first and still only placekicker to win that award, a reminder of how unusual the 1982 season was in every respect.
