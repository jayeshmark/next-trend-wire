Super Bowl XLI: Indianapolis Colts vs Chicago Bears

By Editorial Team

Indianapolis won its first Super Bowl title since the franchise's Baltimore era, beating Chicago after trailing 14-6 in the first quarter. Chicago was seeking its first Super Bowl title since the 1985 season. This was the first Super Bowl in which both head coaches were Black, Indianapolis head coach Tony Dungy and Chicago head coach Lovie Smith. Dungy became the first Black head coach to win a Super Bowl.

The game story

The stakes were personal before they were tactical. Peyton Manning arrived in Miami as the best regular season quarterback of his generation and the owner of a reputation he could not shake: brilliant in the regular season, short in the playoffs. He had already lost three playoff games to New England, and the question hanging over the Colts was whether a team built around his passing could win in January and February. Chicago arrived with the opposite profile, a 13-3 team built on defense and running, with Rex Grossman at quarterback and Devin Hester as the most dangerous returner in the league. The coaching matchup made history on its own. Tony Dungy of Indianapolis and Lovie Smith of Chicago were the first two Black head coaches to reach a Super Bowl, and one of them would become the first to win one.

The game could hardly have started worse for Indianapolis. Hester took the opening kickoff and ran 92 yards for a touchdown, 14 seconds into the game, and the Bears led before Manning had touched the ball. Manning answered with a 53 yard touchdown pass to Reggie Wayne, but the extra point was botched, so the Colts trailed 7-6 instead of tying the game. Grossman then found Muhsin Muhammad for a 4 yard touchdown, and Chicago led 14-6 at the end of the first quarter. In steady rain that never let up, the ball was slick, footing was uncertain, and every possession felt harder than it should have.

The second quarter belonged to Indianapolis, and it belonged to patience rather than fireworks. Adam Vinatieri kicked a 29 yard field goal. Dominic Rhodes ran one yard for a touchdown, and Vinatieri's extra point gave Indianapolis its first lead at 16-14. The Colts had a chance to stretch the lead before halftime, but Vinatieri missed a 36 yard field goal as time expired in the half. The lead was two points, and the game still felt open.

The third quarter turned a close game into a Colts win built in small pieces. Vinatieri kicked field goals of 24 and 20 yards, and Indianapolis led 22-14. Robbie Gould answered with a 44 yard field goal for Chicago, cutting the lead to 22-17, but those were the last points Chicago would score. The Bears offense, which had moved the ball early, could no longer sustain drives in the rain, and Grossman's mistakes began to pile up.

The fourth quarter settled it with one defensive play. With 11:59 left, Grossman threw deep toward Muhammad, and Kelvin Hayden intercepted the pass at the Indianapolis 44 and returned it 56 yards for a touchdown. Chicago challenged the ruling, and the call stood. Vinatieri's extra point made it 29-17, and the rest of the game was a formality. Indianapolis had outscored Chicago 23-3 after the first quarter.

The legacy of the game splits between the field and the stage. For Manning, the win removed the one line from his resume that critics always reached for, and his MVP award, on a modest line of 25 of 38 for 247 yards and a touchdown with an interception, reflected the game as it was played: careful, mistake free enough, and won on the ground and on defense as much as through the air. Rhodes ran for 113 yards, Thomas Jones ran for 112 in defeat, and the Colts won because they stopped giving the game away after the first quarter. For Dungy, the win made him the first Black head coach to win a Super Bowl, a milestone that mattered far beyond one night in Miami. And the halftime show, Prince performing "Purple Rain" in an actual downpour, became the standard against which later halftime shows are measured. The rain that made the football slippery made the performance unforgettable, and Super Bowl XLI is remembered as much for that twelve minutes as for the game around it.

Why this game mattered

Super Bowl XLI mattered for two reasons that had little to do with the final score. The first was the coaching matchup. Dungy and Smith had both been passed over for head coaching jobs earlier in their careers, and their meeting in Miami was treated, rightly, as a milestone for Black coaches in the NFL. Dungy's win gave that story its ending, and it is still cited whenever the league's hiring record is discussed.

The second was the weather, and what Prince did in it. The game was played in rain, and the downpour shaped the football: a botched extra point hold, slippery footing, and a low scoring grind after a wild first quarter. At halftime, Prince refused to shorten his set or avoid the rain, and closed with "Purple Rain" as the rain fell hardest. The performance is now the usual answer to the question of the best halftime show ever, and it changed what audiences expect from the slot: not just a medley of hits, but a moment that could not have happened anywhere else.

On the field, the game also closed a chapter for Manning. The Colts team that won in Miami was not the record setting passing machine of earlier seasons. It ran the ball with Rhodes and Addai, kicked field goals, and let its defense make the decisive play. That is why the win answered the criticism of Manning in a way another shootout could not have: he won a wet, ugly game in which his team trailed early and never panicked.
