Super Bowl XIV: Pittsburgh Steelers vs Los Angeles Rams

By Editorial Team

Result in brief: Pittsburgh became the first team to win four Super Bowls, doing it in six seasons. Note on the Rams: the Rams in this game were the Los Angeles Rams. They played the 1979 season based in Los Angeles, and did not move to St. Louis until 1995.

The game story

Super Bowl XIV looked, on paper, like a mismatch. Pittsburgh was the defending champion, 12 and 4 in the regular season, with the Steel Curtain defense and Terry Bradshaw throwing to Lynn Swann and John Stallworth. Los Angeles was 9 and 7, the first team with nine wins or fewer to reach a Super Bowl since the league expanded to a 16 game schedule, and the Rams had lost their starting quarterback, Pat Haden, to injury, leaving the job to Vince Ferragamo. Pittsburgh was favored by 10.5 points. The game was played at the Rose Bowl in Pasadena, about 10 miles from downtown Los Angeles, which made the Rams the first team to play a Super Bowl in its home market, even though the Rose Bowl was not the Rams' home stadium.

For three quarters, the Rams made the mismatch look like a myth. Pittsburgh scored first on a 41 yard field goal by Matt Bahr, but Los Angeles answered with a 1 yard touchdown run by Cullen Bryant to lead 7 to 3. Pittsburgh regained the lead when Franco Harris ran 1 yard for a touchdown, making it 10 to 7, but Los Angeles kicked field goals of 31 and 45 yards by Frank Corral to lead 13 to 10 at halftime. The Rams were not just hanging around. They were controlling the ball, Ferragamo was accurate, and Pittsburgh's offense, which had led the league in scoring, was struggling to find room.

The third quarter swung back and forth twice more. Early in the half, Bradshaw threw a 47 yard touchdown pass to Swann, who leaped over two defenders to catch it, and Pittsburgh led 17 to 13. Los Angeles answered immediately: Ferragamo threw a 50 yard pass to Billy Waddy to reach the Pittsburgh 24, and on the next play, running back Lawrence McCutcheon threw a 24 yard touchdown pass to Ron Smith on a halfback option. Corral missed the extra point, but Los Angeles led 19 to 17, and the underdog Rams had now led three times in the game. Bradshaw threw two of his three interceptions in the third quarter, Rod Perry knocked Swann out of the game with a hard hit, and a fourth Pittsburgh title looked far from certain.

The fourth quarter belonged to Pittsburgh, and to one play in particular. On Pittsburgh's first possession of the final period, Bradshaw threw deep to Stallworth, who looked back over his shoulder, reached over the outstretched arms of Rod Perry, caught the ball in stride, and ran into the end zone for a 73 yard touchdown. Pittsburgh led 24 to 19, and the Rose Bowl, which had been roaring for the Rams, went quiet for the home market team. Los Angeles tried to come back, but Jack Lambert intercepted Ferragamo to give Pittsburgh the ball again, and Bradshaw drove the Steelers down the field, with a 45 yard pass to Stallworth as the key play, before Harris ran 1 yard for his second touchdown of the game. The final score was 31 to 19.

Bradshaw was named MVP for the second straight year, despite his 3 interceptions, because he completed 14 of 21 passes for 309 yards and 2 touchdowns, and because both touchdowns came when Pittsburgh needed them most. He also set Super Bowl career records at the time for passing yards (932) and touchdown passes (9). Pittsburgh became the first team to win four Super Bowls, doing it in six seasons, a record that still stands as the fastest run to four titles. For Los Angeles, the loss was painful but not disgraceful: a 9 and 7 team had led the defending champions going into the fourth quarter of a Super Bowl, in its own backyard, and had lost to two great throws. The Rams would not return to the Super Bowl until after they moved to St. Louis, where they won Super Bowl XXXIV. Pittsburgh would not win another Super Bowl for a quarter century, until Super Bowl XL. This game, then, was the end of the 1970s Steelers dynasty, and it ended the way that dynasty had played all decade: with a great quarterback, great receivers, and a defense that made the stop when it mattered.

Notable commercials

BrandConceptWhy it is remembered
Coca-Cola, "Hey Kid, Catch!""Mean" Joe Greene, limping off the field after a game, is offered a Coke by a young boy, drinks it, and tosses the boy his jersey with the line "Hey kid, catch!"The ad had debuted on October 1, 1979, and was re-aired during Super Bowl XIV, where it reached its largest audience. It won a Clio Award as one of the best commercials of 1979 and is repeatedly listed among the greatest commercials of all time.

Why this game mattered

Super Bowl XIV set a Super Bowl attendance record at the time, with 103,985 fans at the Rose Bowl, and it was the first Super Bowl in which a participating team played in its home market. It closed the Pittsburgh Steelers dynasty of the 1970s, the first team to win four Super Bowls, and it did so with a play, Stallworth's 73 yard touchdown, that is still shown as an example of a great receiver beating great coverage. The game is also remembered for what aired during it: the Coca-Cola "Hey Kid, Catch!" commercial, starring the Steelers' own "Mean" Joe Greene, became one of the most famous advertisements ever made, and its Super Bowl airing is often credited with making it a national cultural moment rather than just a good ad. The irony was not lost on viewers: Greene, the fiercest player on the fiercest defense in football, became a national soft heart in a 60 second commercial during the game in which his team won its fourth title.
