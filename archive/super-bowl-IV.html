The game story

Minnesota arrived in New Orleans as the team that was supposed to restore order. The Vikings had finished 12 and 2, best in the NFL, leading the older league in points scored with 379 and in fewest points allowed with 133. Their defense, the Purple People Eaters, built around Carl Eller, Jim Marshall, Alan Page, and Gary Larsen, was treated as the most intimidating front four in football. Their offense was led by Joe Kapp, a quarterback famous for lowering his shoulder into tacklers rather than sliding. Minnesota had beaten the Los Angeles Rams 23 to 20 and then Cleveland 27 to 7 to reach New Orleans, and was favored by 13.5 points.

Kansas City arrived as the team that was supposed to lose, and as a team that had not even won its own division. The Chiefs had finished 11 and 3, second behind Oakland, and reached the championship game only because the AFL had expanded its playoffs that season. They won two road playoff games, beating the defending champion New York Jets 13 to 6 and then Oakland 17 to 7 in the final AFL title game. Quarterback Len Dawson had missed much of the season with a knee injury, and five days before the championship game his name had been linked to a federal gambling investigation in Detroit. He was later cleared, but he called that week the toughest of his life. Dawson also carried a personal score to settle: his Chiefs had lost Super Bowl I to Green Bay 35 to 10.

Coach Hank Stram had a plan for all of it, and the plan worked from the first quarter. He had his line double team Eller and Marshall, and he built the passing game around short, quick throws that got the ball out before the rush arrived. He used trap plays that let Minnesota's aggressive defense work against itself. And he put Buchanan, at 285 pounds, or Culp, at 295 pounds, nose to nose with center Mick Tingelhoff, who weighed 235 pounds, disrupting the Vikings running game. Minnesota hesitated for a moment on play after play, and a moment was all Kansas City needed.

The scoreboard followed the plan with unusual tidiness. Kansas City scored on its first drive, on a 48 yard field goal by Jan Stenerud, a championship game record at the time. Stenerud added a 32 yard field goal early in the second quarter and a 25 yard field goal later in the quarter, after a short drive set up by a long end around run from Frank Pitts. Then came the play that became the signature of the game. On a trap play called 65 Toss Power Trap, Dawson handed to Mike Garrett, guard Mo Moorman pulled and cleared a huge hole by blocking Page, and Garrett ran 5 yards for a touchdown and a 16 to 0 lead. Minnesota reached the Kansas City 41 yard line on the next drive, but a sack by Buchanan and a missed 56 yard field goal attempt by Fred Cox ended the threat, and Kansas City went to halftime ahead 16 to 0. Minnesota had rushed for 24 yards in the half and had not converted a single third down.

Minnesota did fight back after halftime, at least briefly. After Kansas City punted to open the third quarter, the Vikings drove 69 yards in 10 plays, converting their first third down of the game, and Osborn scored on a 4 yard run to cut the lead to 16 to 7. The comeback lasted exactly one Kansas City possession. Pitts picked up a key first down on a reverse, and Dawson threw a short pass to Otis Taylor at the Minnesota 41 yard line. Taylor broke two tackles, ran down the sideline, and scored on a 46 yard play for a 23 to 7 lead. The fourth quarter was only about closing the game out: Kansas City forced 3 interceptions on 3 Minnesota possessions, and the Vikings final play was an interception thrown by backup Gary Cuozzo to Emmitt Thomas.

Dawson was named Most Valuable Player, completing 12 of 17 passes for 142 yards and one touchdown. The numbers that mattered most, though, belonged to the defense and to the running game. Minnesota managed 67 rushing yards as a team in New Orleans. Washington, who had averaged more than 21 yards per catch during the season, caught one pass for 9 yards. Kansas City had beaten the NFL's best team by 16 points.

For Kansas City, the win was the last championship game before the AFL and NFL merged into one league, and it squared the series between the leagues at two wins each. It was also the first major professional sports championship won by a Kansas City team. The Chiefs would not return to a Super Bowl for 50 years, until Super Bowl LIV in 2020, which made this win stand alone in franchise history for half a century. For Minnesota, the loss began a pattern the franchise has never shaken: four Super Bowl appearances, all in the 1970s, and four defeats. This one was the most surprising of the four, because Minnesota was not beaten by a better record or a hotter team, but by a game plan that took away everything Minnesota did best, one short pass and one trap play at a time.

Scoring timeline

  1. Kansas City strikes first. Stenerud's 48 yard field goal, a championship game record at the time, gives Kansas City a 3 to 0 lead on its first drive.
  2. Kansas City extends the lead. Stenerud's 32 yard field goal early in the second quarter makes it 6 to 0.
  3. Kansas City extends it again. Stenerud's 25 yard field goal, set up by Pitts on an end around run, makes it 9 to 0.
  4. Kansas City breaks it open. Garrett's 5 yard touchdown run on 65 Toss Power Trap makes it 16 to 0 before halftime.
  5. Minnesota answers in the third quarter. Osborn's 4 yard touchdown run caps a 69 yard drive and cuts the lead to 16 to 7.
  6. Kansas City answers at once. Taylor's 46 yard touchdown reception from Dawson, breaking two tackles on the way to the end zone, restores a 23 to 7 lead and ends the comeback.
  7. Kansas City closes the game out. The defense forces 3 interceptions on 3 Minnesota possessions in the fourth quarter, and no further points are scored.

Why this game mattered

This was the final AFL NFL championship game before the merger, and it decided how that era would be remembered. If Minnesota had won easily, as most people expected, the story would have been that the Jets win a year earlier was a fluke and the NFL was still clearly superior. Kansas City's 23 to 7 win made the final tally two wins each, and it gave the AFL a respectable closing argument as it disappeared into the merged league. The game also proved that the AFL's best ideas, Stram's shifting formations, short passing game, and trap running plays, could beat the NFL's best defense on a neutral field.

The game is also remembered for sound. NFL Films convinced Stram to wear a hidden microphone during the game, the first time a head coach had been miked for a Super Bowl. His sideline calls became famous, above all his repeated urging to keep moving the ball down the field and his celebration of the 65 Toss Power Trap touchdown. That film turned a one sided game into one of the most watched championship films in league history, and it made Stram, more than any player on the field that day, the voice people associate with Super Bowl IV.

Off the field, the game showed how big the event had already become. All seats were priced at 15 dollars, up from 12, 8, and 6 dollars the year before, and the attendance of 80,562 was the highest of the four pre merger championship games. The halftime show, with its battle reenactment, cannons, and parade, was the most elaborate the game had staged to that point, a sign that the championship was becoming a spectacle as much as a football game. And for Kansas City, the win began a wait that lasted 50 years, which is why the players from this team were still being honored at a championship parade half a century later.
